Q1Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
For isomeric haloalkanes, the boiling point ______ with increase in branching.
ExplanationBranching makes a molecule more compact and more nearly spherical, so its surface area falls and the van der Waals contact between neighbouring molecules weakens. Weaker forces mean less energy is needed to separate them, so the boiling point drops.Source · CFQ
Q2Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which of o- and p-dibromobenzene has the higher melting point, and why?
ExplanationMelting point is about how well molecules fit into a crystal, not about polarity. The para isomer is highly symmetrical, so its molecules stack neatly and a great deal of energy is needed to break the lattice apart. Note this runs opposite to boiling point, where the more polar ortho isomer wins — boiling depends on attraction between molecules, melting on packing.Source · Reasoning
Q3Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
During electrophilic substitution of chlorobenzene, which product usually dominates?
Explanation–Cl is deactivating but still o,p-directing, because its lone pair is donated into the ring by resonance and that reaches only the ortho and para positions. Of those two, the para product dominates: the chlorine is bulky, so attack next to it is sterically hindered.Source · CFQ
Q4Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Electrophilic substitution in haloarenes occurs slowly compared with benzene because:
ExplanationA halogen has two opposing effects. Its −I effect pulls electron density out of the ring, making it poorer at attracting an electrophile — so the ring is deactivated and reacts slowly. Its +R effect donates a lone pair back, but only to the ortho and para positions, which is why the reaction is slow yet still o,p-directing.Source · Reasoning
Q5Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
An alkyl halide heated with alcoholic KCN gives X, while the same halide with alcoholic AgCN gives Y. X and Y are:
ExplanationCN− is an ambident nucleophile — it can attack through carbon or nitrogen — and which end attacks is decided by the cation. KCN is largely ionic, so the free CN− attacks through carbon and gives the cyanide (nitrile). AgCN is largely covalent, so only the nitrogen lone pair is available and the product is the isocyanide.Source · CFQ
Q6Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
A solution of KOH hydrolyses CH3CH(Cl)CH2CH3 and CH3CH2CH2CH2Cl. Which is hydrolysed more easily, and why?
Explanation2-Chlorobutane ionises to a secondary carbocation, stabilised by the +I effect and hyperconjugation of two alkyl groups; 1-chlorobutane would have to form a much less stable primary one. Since the SN1 rate is set entirely by carbocation stability, the secondary halide is hydrolysed the more easily.Source · Reasoning
Q7Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
2-Chlorobutane on heating with alcoholic KOH gives mainly:
ExplanationAlcoholic KOH eliminates (aqueous KOH would substitute and give the alcohol). Of the two possible alkenes, Saytzeff's rule says the more substituted — and therefore more stable — alkene predominates, which is but-2-ene.Source · CFQ
Q8Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which of C6H5CH(CH3)Br and C6H5CH(C6H5)Br is more reactive in an SN1 substitution?
ExplanationBoth ionise to a benzylic carbocation, so the question is which cation is better stabilised. The diphenyl compound gives a cation flanked by two rings, so the positive charge is delocalised over both by resonance — far more effective than one ring plus the weak hyperconjugation of a methyl group.Source · Reasoning
Q9Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
2-Bromobutane is optically active but 1-bromobutane is not, because:
ExplanationIn CH3CH(Br)CH2CH3 the second carbon carries four different groups — –H, –Br, –CH3 and –C2H5 — so it is a stereocentre and the molecule is chiral. In 1-bromobutane the carbon bearing Br also carries two hydrogens, so no carbon has four different groups and the molecule is achiral.Source · Reasoning
Q10Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which reacts faster in an SN2 displacement?
ExplanationSN2 needs the nucleophile to attack the carbon from directly behind the leaving group, so the less crowded that carbon, the faster the reaction: 1° > 2° > 3°. Note this is the exact reverse of the SN1 order, which depends on carbocation stability instead — always check which mechanism is named before quoting an order.Source · Reasoning
Q11Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Haloarenes are much less reactive than haloalkanes towards nucleophilic substitution because:
ExplanationThe halogen lone pair is delocalised into the ring, so the C–X bond gains partial double-bond character: it is shorter and stronger, and therefore far harder to break. The ring is also electron rich, which repels an incoming nucleophile, and the sp2 carbon holds its electrons more tightly than an sp3 one.Source · Reasoning
Q12Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Why can aryl halides not be prepared by reacting phenol with HCl in the presence of ZnCl2?
ExplanationThe Lucas-type route works by protonating the –OH and losing water to give a carbocation, which the chloride then captures. For phenol that would mean a phenyl cation, with the positive charge in an sp2 orbital lying in the plane of the ring, where the π system cannot stabilise it at all. It does not form, so no aryl halide is produced. The C–O bond in phenol also has partial double-bond character and is unusually strong.Source · CFQ
Q13Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which reacts faster in an SN2 reaction, and why?
ExplanationBoth have the same, entirely unhindered carbon skeleton, so sterics are identical and only the leaving group differs. Iodine is the largest halogen, so the C–I bond is the weakest and I− the most stable and polarisable of the halide ions — the best leaving group. Reactivity runs R–I > R–Br > R–Cl.Source · Reasoning
Q14Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Peroxide formation in ethers is a ______ reaction, and can be prevented by adding a small amount of ______ to the ether.
ExplanationAtmospheric oxygen attacks the C–H bond next to the ether oxygen by a free-radical chain, giving an explosive peroxide on long storage. Adding Cu2O destroys the peroxide as it forms, which is why ethers are stored over it and why old ether must never be distilled to dryness.Source · CFQ
Q15Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
The C–O–H bond angle in alcohols is slightly less than 109°28′, whereas the C–O–C angle in ethers is slightly greater. Why?
ExplanationBoth oxygens are sp3, so both start near the tetrahedral angle. In an alcohol the two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than the pairs repel each other, squeezing the angle below 109°28′. In an ether the two bulky alkyl groups repel each other more strongly still, pushing the angle above it.Source · Reasoning
Q16Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Primary alcohols on oxidation give ______, which on further oxidation give ______ with the same number of carbon atoms.
ExplanationA primary alcohol has two hydrogens on the carbinol carbon, so it can be oxidised twice: first to the aldehyde, then on to the carboxylic acid. No carbon is gained or lost. A mild reagent such as PCC stops at the aldehyde; acidified KMnO4 carries straight on to the acid.Source · CFQ
Q17Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Although the phenoxide ion has more resonating structures than the carboxylate ion, a carboxylic acid is a stronger acid than phenol. Why?
ExplanationCounting resonance structures is not enough — what matters is how much each contributes. In the carboxylate the charge is spread over two electronegative oxygens in two equivalent structures, which is maximally effective. In the phenoxide the charge is pushed onto carbon atoms of the ring, which are far less able to bear it. So the carboxylate is better stabilised despite having fewer structures.Source · Reasoning
Q18Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Phenol gives a ______ coloured complex with ______ ferric chloride solution.
ExplanationPhenol forms an iron–phenoxide complex of intense violet colour, and the FeCl3 must be neutral: acid would suppress the ionisation of the phenol and alkali would precipitate iron hydroxide. It is the standard test that tells a phenol from an alcohol.Source · CFQ
Q19Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
(CH3)3C–O–CH3 with HI gives CH3OH and (CH3)3C–I, not (CH3)3C–OH and CH3I, because:
ExplanationHI first protonates the ether oxygen, so that the leaving group is a neutral alcohol rather than an alkoxide. The question is then which C–O bond breaks — and the tert-butyl side breaks on its own because it gives a stable tertiary carbocation, which I− captures. Attack at the methyl carbon by SN2 is possible in principle but far slower, so it does not compete.Source · Reasoning
Q20Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Dehydration of ethanol with conc. H2SO4 gives ______ at 413 K but ______ at 443 K.
ExplanationAt the lower temperature a second ethanol molecule acts as the nucleophile and the two condense to the ether. At the higher temperature elimination wins and the alkene is formed. The same acid, the same alcohol — only the temperature decides.Source · CFQ
Q21Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
p-Nitrophenol is more acidic than o-nitrophenol because:
ExplanationBoth isomers are acidified by the −I and −R effects of –NO2, which reach the ortho and para positions alike. What separates them is that in the ortho isomer the –OH is locked into an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the adjacent nitro group, so that proton is less readily given up. Note this is the same chelation that makes o-nitrophenol the more volatile of the two.Source · Reasoning
Q22Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Phenol on reaction with bromine water gives a ______ precipitate of ______.
Explanation–OH activates the ring so strongly that bromination needs no catalyst and does not stop at one substitution: all three available ortho and para positions are brominated, giving a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. In a non-polar solvent such as CS2 the reaction can be stopped at the monobromo stage.Source · CFQ
Q23Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Which reagent converts a primary alcohol to an aldehyde without going further?
ExplanationAlcohol to aldehyde is an oxidation that must be stopped half way. Pyridinium chlorochromate in CH2Cl2 is the standard mild oxidant, and having no water present means the aldehyde is never hydrated and so cannot be oxidised on. The strong acidic oxidants carry straight through to the carboxylic acid, and LiAlH4 runs the reaction the wrong way altogether — it reduces.Source · CFQ
Q24Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Which of o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol is more volatile, and why?
ExplanationIn the ortho isomer the –NO2 and –OH are adjacent, so the molecule hydrogen bonds to itself (chelation). Its –OH is then unavailable to bond to a neighbour, so the molecules do not associate and it is steam volatile. The para isomer cannot chelate, so it forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds, associates, and is far less volatile. This is how the two are separated.Source · CFQ
Q25Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
In alcohols, the boiling point decreases with increase in branching because:
ExplanationAll the isomers can hydrogen bond equally through their single –OH, so that is not what separates them. What changes is the shape: a branched molecule is more compact and more nearly spherical, so neighbouring molecules touch over a smaller area and the van der Waals attraction between them falls.Source · Reasoning
Q26Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of acidity: phenol, o-nitrophenol, o-cresol.
ExplanationAcidity is decided by the stability of the phenoxide. –NO2 is −I and −R: it withdraws electrons and spreads the negative charge, stabilising the anion, so o-nitrophenol is the strongest. –CH3 is +I: it pushes electron density in and destabilises the anion, so o-cresol is the weakest. Phenol, with neither, sits between.Source · CFQ
Q27Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Aliphatic and ______ aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent to give a ______.
ExplanationTollens' reagent, ammoniacal AgNO3, is reduced by both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes, depositing metallic silver as a mirror. That is worth contrasting with Fehling's solution, which gives a red precipitate of Cu2O but is reduced by aliphatic aldehydes only — benzaldehyde does not respond to it.Source · CFQ
Q28Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Aromatic carbonyl compounds are ______ reactive than their aliphatic analogues in nucleophilic ______ reactions.
ExplanationThe ring is conjugated with the C=O group, so electron density is fed towards the carbonyl carbon by resonance. That reduces the partial positive charge which a nucleophile has to attack, making the aromatic carbonyl less electrophilic. The bulky ring adds steric hindrance as well. The reaction itself is nucleophilic addition across C=O.Source · CFQ
Q29Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of acid strength: 4-nitrobenzoic acid, benzoic acid, 3,4-dinitrobenzoic acid, 4-methoxybenzoic acid.
ExplanationAcid strength follows the stability of the carboxylate anion. –NO2 is strongly electron withdrawing, so it disperses the negative charge and strengthens the acid — and two nitro groups do so more than one. –OCH3 is electron releasing by resonance from the para position, so it destabilises the anion and gives the weakest acid of the four.Source · CFQ
Q30Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Ketones are ______ polar than the corresponding isomeric aldehydes, because of the two electron-______ alkyl groups on the carbonyl carbon.
ExplanationTwo alkyl groups, each electron releasing (+I), push electron density towards the carbonyl carbon and so increase the separation of charge in the C=O bond — the ketone has the larger dipole moment. Note the apparent paradox: the very same +I effect makes the carbonyl carbon less electrophilic, which is why ketones are less reactive than aldehydes even though they are more polar.Source · CFQ
Q31Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of reactivity towards hydrolysis: CH3COCl, CH3CONH2, CH3COOCH3, (CH3CO)2O.
ExplanationReactivity of an acid derivative is set by how good the leaving group is and how much the attached atom donates electrons back into the carbonyl. Cl− is the best leaving group and chlorine donates least, so the acid chloride is the most reactive. –NH2 donates its lone pair strongly into the C=O and NH2− is a terrible leaving group, so the amide is the least.Source · CFQ
Q32Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
The carboxylate ion is ______ stable than the carboxylic acid, because it is ______ stabilised by ______.
ExplanationIn the carboxylate ion the two oxygens are equivalent: the negative charge is shared equally between them, and both resonance structures are of the same energy, which is the most effective kind of delocalisation. In the undissociated acid the contributing structures involve charge separation and are not equivalent, so the acid gains far less. The anion being well stabilised is exactly why carboxylic acids are acidic.Source · CFQ
Q33Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Cyclohexanone forms a cyanohydrin in good yield but 2,4,6-trimethylcyclohexanone does not, because:
ExplanationNucleophilic addition needs CN− to reach the carbonyl carbon, approaching roughly perpendicular to the C=O plane. Three methyl groups placed around the ring block that approach. Note the methyls are also +I, which reduces the positive charge on the carbonyl carbon, but here the dominant effect is steric.Source · Reasoning
Q34Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
An aliphatic carboxylic acid gives ______ on reduction with red P and HI, but ______ on reduction with LiAlH4.
ExplanationRed phosphorus with HI is a drastic reducing system: it takes the –COOH group all the way down to –CH3, giving the alkane. LiAlH4 is selective and stops at the primary alcohol — note it does not stop at the aldehyde, because the aldehyde is reduced faster than the acid.Source · CFQ
Q35Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Monochloroethanoic acid has a higher pKa than dichloroethanoic acid because:
ExplanationChlorine is −I, so it pulls electron density away from the carboxylate and disperses the negative charge, stabilising the anion. Two chlorines do this more than one, so dichloroethanoic acid is the stronger acid. A stronger acid has the lower pKa — so the weaker monochloro acid has the higher one. The inverse relation between strength and pKa is what this question is really testing.Source · Reasoning
Q36Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of reactivity towards HCN: acetaldehyde, acetone, di-tert-butyl ketone, methyl tert-butyl ketone.
ExplanationTwo effects run the same way here. Sterically, the bulkier the groups flanking the carbonyl, the harder it is for CN− to reach the carbon — two tert-butyl groups block it almost completely. Electronically, each alkyl group is +I and reduces the positive charge on that carbon. So reactivity falls as alkyl substitution grows, and the least substituted, acetaldehyde, is the most reactive.Source · CFQ
Q37Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Would benzaldehyde be more or less reactive than propanal in nucleophilic addition?
ExplanationThe ring is conjugated with the C=O, and resonance feeds electron density towards the carbonyl carbon. That reduces its partial positive charge, so a nucleophile is attracted less strongly and benzaldehyde is less reactive. Having no α-hydrogen is true of benzaldehyde but is irrelevant here — it governs aldol and Cannizzaro, not addition.Source · Reasoning
Q38Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
(CH3)3C–CHO does not undergo the aldol condensation because:
ExplanationThe aldol reaction begins with a base removing an α-hydrogen — one on the carbon next to the C=O — to give the carbanion that attacks a second molecule. In (CH3)3C–CHO that neighbouring carbon is quaternary: it carries three methyl groups and no hydrogen at all. Formaldehyde and benzaldehyde fail the same test, and being unable to do aldol is exactly what lets them do Cannizzaro instead.Source · Reasoning
Q39Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Why is it necessary to control the pH when aldehydes and ketones react with ammonia derivatives?
ExplanationThe reaction needs both things at once: a weakly acidic medium protonates the carbonyl oxygen, which makes the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic. But if the medium is strongly acidic, the H2N–G reagent is protonated too — its nitrogen lone pair is tied up and it can no longer act as a nucleophile. So there is an optimum pH, around 3.5.Source · Reasoning
Q40Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Carboxylic acids are higher boiling than alcohols of comparable molecular mass because:
ExplanationA carboxylic acid has both a hydrogen-bond donor (–OH) and a strong acceptor (C=O) in the same group, so two molecules lock together through two hydrogen bonds into a closed eight-membered dimer. It effectively boils as a molecule of twice the mass. An alcohol forms only single hydrogen bonds in a chain.Source · Reasoning
