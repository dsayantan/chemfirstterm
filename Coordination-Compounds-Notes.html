C1Complex compound or double salt?NCERT p. 120

C1Complex compound or double salt?
Double saltComplex compound
Behaviour in waterdissociates completely into all its simple ionsthe coordination sphere stays intact; only the ions outside it are released

ExampleMohr's salt, FeSO4·(NH4)2SO4·6H2O[Cu(NH3)4]SO4
Hold on to this  · This is the standard two-mark question, and it is always set the same way. FeSO4 mixed with (NH4)2SO4 in a 1:1 ratio gives Mohr's salt, a double salt, which dissociates fully and so does give the test for Fe2+. CuSO4 with aqueous ammonia in a 1:4 ratio gives the complex [Cu(NH3)4]SO4, in which Cu2+ is locked inside the coordination sphere and so gives no test for Cu2+.

  • A ligand that binds through two or more sites forms a ring with the metal and is called a chelating ligand; the ring is a chelate. Chelation makes a complex markedly more stable.
  • An ambidentate ligand can bind through either of two different atoms — NO2− through N (nitro) or through O (nitrito), and SCN− through S (thiocyanato) or N (isothiocyanato). This is what gives rise to linkage isomerism (C7).
Hold on to this  · EDTA is hexadentate and forms a very stable octahedral chelate. That is why it is used in the treatment of lead poisoning — it wraps around Pb2+, and the resulting complex is soluble, unreactive and excreted by the kidneys. It is also used to estimate Ca2+ and Mg2+ in hard water.

C3Oxidation number and coordination numberNCERT p. 122

C3Oxidation number and coordination number
  • Coordination number (C.N.) — the number of ligand donor atoms bonded to the central metal. Count donor sites, not ligand molecules: in [Co(en)3]3+ there are three ligands but each ethane-1,2-diamine donates through two nitrogens, so the C.N. is 6, not 3.
  • Oxidation number — let it be x, add the charges of all the ligands, and set the total equal to the charge on the complex ion.
[Co(en)3]3+ :   x + 3(0) = +3  ⇒  x = +3,   C.N. = 6[Fe(CN)6]4− :   x + 6(−1) = −4  ⇒  x = +2,   C.N. = 6[Ni(CO)4] :   x + 4(0) = 0  ⇒  x = 0,   C.N. = 4

C4IUPAC nomenclature — the rules in orderNCERT p. 122

C4IUPAC nomenclature — the rules in order
  1. Cation first, anion second, as in any salt.
  2. Within the complex, name the ligands first, in alphabetical order, then the metal. Alphabetise by the ligand name, ignoring the multiplying prefix.
  3. Anionic ligands end in -o: chlorido, cyanido, hydroxido, oxalato, nitrito. Neutral ligands keep their names, except aqua (H2O), ammine (NH3), carbonyl (CO) and nitrosyl (NO).
  4. Use di-, tri-, tetra- for simple ligands; use bis-, tris-, tetrakis- with brackets for ligands whose names already contain a multiplier, e.g. bis(ethane-1,2-diamine).
  5. Give the oxidation state of the metal in Roman numerals in brackets, immediately after the metal, with no space.
  6. If the complex is an anion, the metal takes the suffix -ate — and often its Latin stem: ferrate, cuprate, argentate, aurate, plumbate, stannate.
FormulaIUPAC name
[Co(NH3)4(H2O)2]Br3tetraamminediaquacobalt(III) bromide
K4[Fe(CN)6]potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II)
[Pt(NH3)4Cl2][PtCl4]tetraamminedichloridoplatinum(IV) tetrachloridoplatinate(II)
[CoCl(ONO)(en)2]+chloridobis(ethane-1,2-diamine)(nitrito-O)cobalt(III) ion

Common slip  · Alphabetical order is by the ligand name, so ammine comes before aqua (m before q) — hence tetraamminediaqua, never the reverse. And in the fourth example, chlorido (c) precedes ethane-1,2-diamine (e), which precedes nitrito (n).

C5Werner's theory and the AgNO₃ testNCERT p. 119

C5Werner's theory and the AgNO₃ test
  • Werner proposed two kinds of valency: primary (ionisable, satisfied by negative ions, shown outside the square brackets) and secondary (non-ionisable, satisfied by ligands, inside the brackets).
  • Only the ions outside the coordination sphere are free in solution. So the number of moles of AgCl precipitated by excess AgNO3 counts the chloride ions outside the brackets.
ComplexIons in solutionmol AgCl per mol complex
[Co(NH3)6]Cl34 (1:3 electrolyte)3
[Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl23 (1:2 electrolyte)2
[Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl2 (1:1 electrolyte)1
[Co(NH3)3Cl3]0 — a non-electrolyte0
Hold on to this  · More ions ⇒ higher molar conductivity. So the conductivity order is exactly the order of the table read upwards: [Co(NH3)3Cl3] < [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl < [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2 < [Co(en)3]Cl3.
Careful with K2[PtCl4]: it ionises to 2K+ and [PtCl4]2−. All four chlorides are inside the sphere, so it gives no precipitate with AgNO3.

C7Stereoisomerism — geometrical and opticalNCERT p. 126

C7Stereoisomerism — geometrical and optical

Geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism needs at least two different kinds of ligand, arranged so that two placements are genuinely distinct.

        [Co(NH3)4Cl2]+
Shape and typeGeometrical isomerism?
Octahedral MA4B2yes — cis and trans
Octahedral MA3B3yes — facial (fac) and meridional (mer)
Octahedral M(AA)2B2, e.g. [Co(en)2Cl2]+yes — cis and trans
Octahedral MA5Bno — only one arrangement exists
Square planar MA2B2, e.g. [Pt(NH3)2Cl2]yes — cis and trans
Tetrahedralno — all four positions are adjacent
  • Optical isomerism requires a non-superimposable mirror image. It is shown chiefly by octahedral complexes with bidentate ligands: [Co(en)3]3+ exists as a d and an l form, and cis-[Co(en)2Cl2]+ is optically active while the trans form is not (it has a plane of symmetry).
Common slip  · The one that is set as a “which does not show geometrical isomerism” distractor is the MA5B type, such as [Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2. With five identical ligands, every position for the sixth is equivalent.

C8Valence bond theory — the procedureNCERT p. 129

C8Valence bond theory — the procedure
  1. Find the oxidation state of the metal and write the d-electron count.
  2. Decide whether the ligands are strong field (they force pairing) or weak field (they do not) — see C10.
  3. If pairing occurs, inner (3d) orbitals become available and the complex uses d2sp3 — an inner orbital or low spin complex. If not, outer 4d orbitals are used and the hybridisation is sp3d2 — an outer orbital or high spin complex.
  4. Count the remaining unpaired electrons and get the magnetic moment.
HybridisationShapeCoordination number
sp3tetrahedral4
dsp2square planar4
d2sp3octahedral (inner orbital, low spin)6
sp3d2octahedral (outer orbital, high spin)6
Hold on to this  · Outer orbital complexes involve sp3d2 hybridisation and are high spin; inner orbital complexes are d2sp3 and low spin. That sentence is set verbatim as a fill-in-the-blanks.
Asked in Heritage 2024-25 Q1(A)(ii), Q15; Heritage 2016-17 Q5(c)

C10Crystal field theory and the spectrochemical seriesNCERT p. 131

C10Crystal field theory and the spectrochemical series
  • In an octahedral field the five d orbitals split into a lower t2g set of three and an upper eg set of two, separated by the crystal field splitting energy Δo.
  • In a tetrahedral field the order is inverted — e below t2 — and the splitting is much smaller, Δt = (4/9)Δo. Tetrahedral complexes are therefore almost always high spin.
  • Whether electrons pair up depends on Δo against the pairing energy P.

The rule that answers every CFT question

Δo > P  (strong field): electrons prefer to pair in t2g → low spin, fewer unpaired electrons.
Δo < P  (weak field): electrons prefer to occupy eg → high spin, more unpaired electrons.
Spectrochemical series (weak field ⟶ strong field)
I−<Br−<Cl−<F−<H2O<NH3<en<NO2−<CN−<CO
Memory hook  · Only the two ends really need memorising: the halides and water are weak field (high spin, often paramagnetic), while CN− and CO are strong field (low spin, often diamagnetic). NH3 sits in the middle and is treated as strong field for cobalt(III) and nickel(II).
Asked in Heritage 2024-25 Q15(ii); Heritage 2016-17 Q1(d)(i), Q5(b)

The same reasoning gives the d5, d6 and d7 cases. d6 is worth knowing separately: strong field gives t2g6eg0, zero unpaired electrons and a diamagnetic complex — which is why [Fe(CN)6]4− and [Co(NH3)6]3+ are both diamagnetic.

C12Colour — the d–d transitionNCERT p. 133

C12Colour — the d–d transition

A transition metal complex is coloured because Δo happens to correspond to the energy of visible light. An electron absorbs a photon and is promoted from t2g to eg — a d–d transition — and the colour seen is the complement of the colour absorbed.

  • A larger Δo means a higher absorbed frequency, so changing the ligand changes the colour. [Cu(H2O)4]2+ is pale blue; [Cu(NH3)4]2+ is deep blue.
  • A complex with no d electrons (d0, e.g. Sc3+, Ti4+) or a full d subshell (d10, e.g. Zn2+, Cu+) has no possible d–d transition and is therefore colourless.
Hold on to this  · The two-part answer that earns full marks: the d subshell is partially filled, so a d–d transition is possible. Both halves are needed — this is set as a fill-in-the-blanks in the specimen papers. Say partially filled, not unpaired electrons: [Fe(CN)6]4− and [Co(NH3)6]3+ are low-spin d6, so they have no unpaired electrons at all, and both are still coloured.

C13Stability of complexesNCERT p. 136

C13Stability of complexes

Stability is measured by the stability constant K for

M + nL  ⇌  MLn     K = [MLn][M][L]n
  • A larger K means a more stable complex.
  • Stability rises with the charge on the metal ion, with a smaller ionic radius, and strongly with chelation — which is why EDTA complexes are so stable.

C15Importance and usesNCERT p. 136

C15Importance and uses
  • Qualitative analysis — Fe3+ with SCN− gives a blood-red complex; Cu2+ with excess NH3 gives the deep blue [Cu(NH3)4]2+; Ni2+ with dimethylglyoxime gives a red precipitate.
  • Extraction of metals — silver and gold are leached as their cyanide complexes, [Ag(CN)2]−.
  • Purification — nickel by Mond's process, through the volatile [Ni(CO)4].
  • Biological systems — haemoglobin (Fe), chlorophyll (Mg), vitamin B12 (Co), carboxypeptidase (Zn).
  • Medicine — cis-platin, cis-[Pt(NH3)2Cl2], in cancer chemotherapy; EDTA in lead poisoning.

D1Oxidation number, coordination number and the number of ionsNCERT p. 122

D1Oxidation number, coordination number and the number of ions

Worked on [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2:

  1. Coordination number — count donor atoms inside the brackets: five NH3 plus one Cl = 6.
  2. Charge on the complex ion — two chlorides sit outside, so the ion must be [Co(NH3)5Cl]2+.
  3. Oxidation number — x + 5(0) + (−1) = +2, so x = +3.
  4. Ions in solution — one complex cation and two chlorides = 3 ions, a 1:2 electrolyte, giving 2 mol AgCl with excess AgNO3.

D2Valence bond treatment of [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻NCERT p. 129

D2Valence bond treatment of [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻
  1. x + 6(−1) = −4, so Fe is +2; Fe2+ is 3d6.
  2. CN− is a strong field ligand, so it forces the six d electrons to pair up in the three t2g-type orbitals.
  3. Two 3d orbitals are thereby vacated, so the metal hybridises as d2sp3 — an inner orbital complex.
  4. Shape octahedral; unpaired electrons n = 0; μ = 0, so the complex is diamagnetic.
Contrast [Fe(H2O)6]2+: water is weak field, so the d6 configuration stays high spin with n = 4, giving sp3d2 hybridisation, an outer orbital complex and μ = 4·90 BM.
■ determined

D3Crystal field treatment of a d⁴ ionNCERT p. 132

D3Crystal field treatment of a d⁴ ion
  1. Place the four electrons in the split levels, following the rule in C10.
Δo > P :   t2g4 eg0  ⇒  two electrons paired, n = 2, low spin, μ = 2·83 BMΔo < P :   t2g3 eg1  ⇒  all four unpaired, n = 4, high spin, μ = 4·90 BM
  1. Read the answer off: the strong-field complex is the less paramagnetic of the two, and the two are different colours, since the larger Δo absorbs at a higher frequency. Note that Δo fixes which colour is absorbed, not how intense the colour is — so do not write that the strong-field complex is ‘more deeply coloured’.

D4Deducing a molecular formula from conductivity dataNCERT p. 119

D4Deducing a molecular formula from conductivity data

“CoBr3·4NH3·2H2O has a molar conductivity corresponding to a 1:3 electrolyte. Write its molecular formula and IUPAC name.”

  1. A 1:3 electrolyte gives one cation and three anions, so four ions in all.
  2. Three of these must be the three Br−, so all three bromides lie outside the coordination sphere and the complex ion carries a charge of +3.
  3. Everything else — four NH3 and two H2O — must therefore be inside, giving a coordination number of 6, which is what cobalt(III) prefers.
[Co(NH3)4(H2O)2]Br3

Naming it: ligands alphabetically, ammine before aqua, then the metal with its oxidation state, then the counter-ion —

tetraamminediaquacobalt(III) bromide

T1Oxidation number and coordination number

T1Oxidation number and coordination number
Recognise it by“What is the oxidation number of the metal / the coordination number?”
MethodFollow D1. Count donor atoms, not ligand molecules — a bidentate ligand contributes 2 and EDTA contributes 6.
Worked example
For [Cr(en)2(H2O)Cl]2+, find the oxidation number of chromium and its coordination number.
  1. donor atoms: 2 en × 2 = 4, plus 1 H₂O, plus 1 Cl = 6
  2. x + 2(0) + 0 + (−1) = +2
Cr is +3; coordination number 6

T2Write the IUPAC name from the formula

T2Write the IUPAC name from the formula
Recognise it byA formula is given; the name is wanted.
MethodApply C4 in order: ligands alphabetically (by ligand name, ignoring prefixes), anionic ligands in -o, oxidation state in Roman numerals, -ate if the complex is an anion.
Worked example
Name (a) [Pt(NH3)4Cl2][PtCl4] and (b) [CoCl(ONO)(en)2]+.  (Heritage 2024–25)
  • (a) The cation is [Pt(NH3)4Cl2]2+, so Pt is +4; the anion is [PtCl4]2−, so Pt is +2. Name: tetraamminedichloridoplatinum(IV) tetrachloridoplatinate(II).
  • (b) x + (−1) + (−1) + 0 = +1, so Co is +3. Alphabetical order is chlorido, ethane-1,2-diamine, nitrito. Name: chloridobis(ethane-1,2-diamine)(nitrito-O)cobalt(III) ion.

T4Identify the type of isomerism

T4Identify the type of isomerism
Recognise it byA pair of formulae, or a single complex, with “state the type of isomerism”.
MethodAsk what has moved. Across the brackets ⇒ ionisation (or hydrate, if it is water). A different donor atom of the same ligand ⇒ linkage. Ligands swapped between two complex ions ⇒ coordination. Nothing moved, only the arrangement in space ⇒ geometrical or optical.
Worked example
State the type of isomerism shown by (a) [Co(NH3)5Cl]SO4 and (b) [Co(en)3]3+.  (Heritage 2024–25)
  • (a) Ionisation isomerism — its isomer is [Co(NH3)5SO4]Cl, in which the sulphate is the ligand and chloride the counter-ion. The two give different precipitation tests: the first gives a white precipitate with BaCl2, the second with AgNO3.
  • (b) Optical isomerism — three bidentate ligands about an octahedral centre give a propeller-shaped ion with no plane of symmetry, so d and l forms exist.

T6Which complex does not show geometrical isomerism?

T6Which complex does not show geometrical isomerism?
Recognise it byA list of four complexes, one of which is the exception.
MethodClassify each by type against the table in C7. The answer is almost always the MA5B member of the list, or a tetrahedral one.
Worked example
Which of the following will not show geometrical isomerism? (a) [Cr(NH3)4Cl2]Cl (b) [Co(en)2Cl2]Cl (c) [Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2 (d) [Pt(NH3)2Cl2]  (Heritage 2024–25)
Answer  · (c). It is of the MA5B type — five identical ammine ligands and one nitro — so every possible position for the odd ligand is equivalent and only one structure exists. (a) and (b) are MA4B2 types and (d) is square planar MA2B2; all three give cis and trans forms.

T7Valence bond theory: hybridisation, shape and magnetism

T7Valence bond theory: hybridisation, shape and magnetism
Recognise it by“State the hybridisation, shape and magnetic behaviour of …”
MethodFollow D2: oxidation state → d-count → strong or weak field → pairing → hybridisation → n → μ. Give all the parts the question asks for; each carries a mark.
Worked example
For [Fe(CN)6]4−: (a) the oxidation number of iron, (b) the number of unpaired electrons, (c) the hybridisation and magnetic behaviour.  (Heritage 2024–25)
  • (a) x + 6(−1) = −4, so Fe is +2, and Fe2+ is 3d6.
  • (b) CN− is strong field, so all six d electrons pair in t2g: n = 0.
  • (c) d2sp3, octahedral, inner orbital, and diamagnetic (μ = 0).

T11Precipitate with silver nitrate

T11Precipitate with silver nitrate
Recognise it by“Will it give a precipitate with AgNO3? How does it ionise?”
MethodOnly ionisable halide — outside the brackets — precipitates. Count it, and say how many moles of AgCl or AgBr result per mole of complex.
Worked example
How does K2[PtCl4] ionise in water? Will it give a precipitate with AgNO3?  (Heritage 2024–25)
K2[PtCl4]  →  2K+ + [PtCl4]2−
Answer  · No precipitate. All four chlorides are inside the coordination sphere, held by non-ionisable secondary valencies, so there are no free Cl− ions in solution for the silver ion to react with.
The related assertion–reason question is worth watching: [Cr(NH3)3Cl3] indeed gives no precipitate, but the reason “it contains neutral ligands only” is false — the three chlorides are anionic ligands. Assertion true, reason false.

T12Complex or double salt?

T12Complex or double salt?
Recognise it byTwo mixtures in stated molar ratios, one giving the metal ion test and one not.
MethodA 1:1 ratio of two simple salts usually means a double salt, which dissociates completely and gives the normal tests. A ratio matching a coordination number (1:4, 1:6) means a complex, in which the metal ion is not free.
Worked example
FeSO4 mixed with (NH4)2SO4 in a 1:1 molar ratio gives the test for Fe2+, but CuSO4 with aqueous ammonia in a 1:4 molar ratio does not give the test for Cu2+. Explain.  (Heritage 2024–25)
Answer  · The first gives Mohr's salt, FeSO4·(NH4)2SO4·6H2O, a double salt. In water it dissociates completely into Fe2+, NH4+ and SO42−, so free Fe2+ is present and gives its usual test. The second gives the complex [Cu(NH3)4]SO4, in which Cu2+ is bound inside the coordination sphere by non-ionisable secondary valencies. It does ionise — into [Cu(NH3)4]2+ and SO42− — but the complex ion itself does not dissociate, so no free Cu2+ is available and the test fails.

T13EDTA and chelation

T13EDTA and chelation
Recognise it byA passage about EDTA in medicine or water treatment, then short parts.
MethodFour facts answer nearly every version: EDTA is hexadentate; it forms a very stable octahedral chelate; in lead poisoning it sequesters Pb2+ into a soluble complex that is excreted; and in water analysis it is used to estimate Ca2+ and Mg2+.
Worked example
(i) What type of ligand is EDTA? (ii) Why is EDTA used in the treatment of lead poisoning? (iii) What is the geometry of the EDTA complex?  (Heritage 2024–25)
  • (i) A hexadentate chelating ligand — it donates through two nitrogen atoms and four carboxylate oxygens.
  • (ii) It chelates the Pb2+ ion, forming a complex of very high stability constant. The lead is thereby removed from the tissues, and the water-soluble complex is excreted through the kidneys.
  • (iii) Octahedral, since the coordination number is 6.

T14Explaining colour or magnetism

T14Explaining colour or magnetism
Recognise it by“Why is [Ti(H2O)6]3+ coloured but [Sc(H2O)6]3+ colourless?”
MethodBoth answers rest on the d-electron count. Colour requires unpaired electrons in the d subshell and a possible d–d transition; a d0 or d10 ion has neither. Magnetism requires unpaired electrons, so count n and use μ = √[n(n+2)].
Worked example
Why is [Ti(H2O)6]3+ violet while [Sc(H2O)6]3+ is colourless?
Answer  · Ti3+ is 3d1. Its single d electron can absorb visible light and be promoted from t2g to eg — a d–d transition — and the complex appears in the complementary colour. Sc3+ is 3d0: with no d electron at all, no d–d transition is possible, so it absorbs nothing in the visible region and is colourless. Zn2+ (d10) is colourless for the opposite reason — the subshell is full, so there is no vacancy to be promoted into.
