Question 1 (A)  ·  Fill in the blanks4 × 1 = 4

1(A)(i)Solutions[1]
van't Hoff factor for sucrose is ______ one while that for potassium ferrocyanide is ______ one.
Answer  · equal to one;  greater than one.
Why  · i counts the particles one formula unit actually gives in solution. Sucrose is a non-electrolyte — it dissolves as whole molecules, so i = 1. Potassium ferrocyanide is K4[Fe(CN)6], which gives 4K+ and one [Fe(CN)6]4− — five particles, so i = 5.
NCERT  · 
1(A)(ii)Coordination Compounds[1]
The geometry of [Ni(CN)4]2− and [NiCl4]2− are ______ and ______ respectively.
Answer  · square planar  and  tetrahedral.
Why  · Both are Ni(II), d8. CN− is a strong-field ligand: it pairs the two unpaired d electrons, freeing a 3d orbital for dsp2 hybridisation — square planar and diamagnetic. Cl− is weak-field and cannot pair them, so Ni(II) uses sp3 — tetrahedral and paramagnetic.
NCERT  · 
1(A)(iii)Aldehydes and Ketones[1]
Urotropine is formed by the action of ______ with ______
Answer  · formaldehyde  with  ammonia.
Why  · Urotropine (hexamethylenetetramine) is (CH2)6N4, made from six HCHO and four NH3: 6HCHO + 4NH3 → (CH2)6N4 + 6H2O. The word bank offers both formaldehyde and acetaldehyde — only formaldehyde works, because it has no α-hydrogen.
NCERT  ·  — (not in the NCERT Class XII book) Urotropine is not named in the Class XII book.
1(A)(iv)Coordination Compounds[1]
Outer orbital complexes involve ______ hybridisation and are ______ spin complexes.
Answer  · sp3d2  and  high spin.
Why  · ‘Outer orbital’ means the metal uses the nd orbitals (4d for a 3d metal) rather than the inner (n−1)d. That happens with weak-field ligands, which cannot force pairing, so the electrons stay spread out — maximum unpaired spins, i.e. high spin. Inner orbital complexes are d2sp3 and low spin.

Question 1 (B)  ·  Multiple choice7 × 1 = 7

1(B)(i)Aldehydes and Ketones[1]
Cyanohydrin of which compound gives lactic acid on hydrolysis? (a) Acetaldehyde  (b) Formaldehyde  (c) Acetone  (d) Propanal
CH3CHO  →HCN  CH3CH(OH)CN  →H3O+  CH3CH(OH)COOH
Answer  · (a) Acetaldehyde.
Why  · Work backwards from the target. Lactic acid is CH3–CH(OH)–COOH. Hydrolysis turns –CN into –COOH, so the cyanohydrin must have been CH3–CH(OH)–CN, which comes from CH3CHO + HCN. Formaldehyde would give glycolic acid, acetone would give 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanoic acid.

1(B)(ii)Solutions[1]
Phenol dimerises in benzene having van't Hoff factor 0.54. What is the degree of association? (a) 0.46  (b) 0.54  (c) 0.27  (d) 0.92
Formula used  · Degree of association α from the van't Hoff factor, for a dimer (n = 2)i = 1 − α(1 − 1/n)  ⇒  i = 1 − α2  ⇒  α = 2(1 − i) = 2(1 − 0·54)
Answer  · (d) 0·92
Why  · Two molecules becoming one halves the particle count, so i < 1 is the signature of association. For a dimer i = 1 − α/2, so α = 2(1 − i) = 2(1 − 0·54) = 0·92. Option (a) is the trap: 1 − i = 0·46, which forgets the factor of two.

1(B)(iii)Haloalkanes[1]
The reactivity order of 1° alkyl halides follows the order: (a) CH3CH2X < CH3X < CH3CH2CH2X  (b) CH3X < CH3CH2X < CH3CH2CH2X  (c) CH3CH2X < CH3CH2CH2X < CH3X  (d) CH3CH2CH2X < CH3CH2X < CH3X
Answer  · (d) CH3CH2CH2X < CH3CH2X < CH3X
Why  · Primary halides react by SN2, in which the nucleophile must reach the carbon from behind. The less crowded that carbon, the faster the reaction — so reactivity increases as the chain gets shorter, and CH3X is fastest of all. The listed order runs from least to most reactive, which is (d).
NCERT  · 
1(B)(iv)Biomolecules[1]
Rapid conversion of α-D-glucose and β-D-glucose in solution is known as: (a) racemization  (b) asymmetric induction  (c) fluxional induction  (d) mutarotation
Answer  · (d) mutarotation.
Why  · In solution the cyclic hemiacetal opens to the straight-chain aldehyde and re-closes, so the two anomers interconvert through it. The specific rotation drifts from +111° (α) or +19° (β) to the equilibrium +52·5° — the rotation mutates, hence the name. It is not racemisation, which would need the mirror image of the whole molecule, and the anomers differ at only one carbon.

1(B)(v)Coordination Compounds[1]
Which of the following will not show geometrical isomerism? (a) [Cr(NH3)4Cl2]Cl  (b) [Co(en)2Cl2]Cl  (c) [Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2  (d) [Pt(NH3)2Cl2]
Answer  · (c) [Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2
Why  · Geometrical isomerism needs at least two of each of two kinds of ligand, so that they can be arranged cis or trans. (a), (b) and (d) are all of the type MA4B2 or MA2B2 and have cis/trans forms. (c) is MA5B — with only one NO2 there is nothing for it to be cis or trans to, and all six positions of an octahedron are equivalent.
NCERT  · 
(c) does show linkage isomerism, because NO2− can bind through N or O — but that is a different kind of isomerism from the one asked about.
1(B)(vi)Haloalkanes[1]
Assertion: AgCN reacts with ethyl bromide to give ethyl cyanide. Reason: CN− is an ambident nucleophile.
Answer  · (d) Assertion is false but Reason is true.
Why  · CN− really is ambident — it can attack through carbon or through nitrogen — so the Reason is true. But which end attacks depends on the cation. KCN is largely ionic, the free CN− attacks through carbon and gives the cyanide (nitrile). AgCN is covalent, so only the nitrogen lone pair is available and the product is the isocyanide, C2H5NC — ethyl isocyanide, not ethyl cyanide. So the Assertion is false.
NCERT  · 
Common slip  · The names are treacherous: ethyl cyanide is C2H5CN (from KCN) and ethyl isocyanide is C2H5NC (from AgCN). Reading the assertion too quickly makes it look true.
1(B)(vii)Coordination Compounds[1]
Assertion: The complex [Cr(NH3)3Cl3] does not give a precipitate with AgNO3. Reason: The given complex contains neutral ligands only.
Answer  · (c) Assertion is true but Reason is false.
Why  · Only ions outside the coordination sphere are free in solution and can be precipitated. Here all three Cl are inside the square brackets, bonded to chromium, so there is no free Cl− and no AgCl — the Assertion is true. But the Reason is false: the complex contains three NH3 (neutral) and three Cl− (anionic) ligands, so it is not made of neutral ligands only.

Question 1 (C)  ·  Kinetics passage3

The data  ·  For 2A + B + C → D + 2F the rate law is rate = k[A][C]2 with k = 2·0 × 10−5 mol−2 L2 s−1, and initially [A] = 0·1, [B] = 0·2, [C] = 0·4 mol L−1. Note that B does not appear in the rate law at all — that single fact answers two of the four parts.
1(C)(i)Chemical Kinetics[1]
What is the order of reaction?
Formula used  · Overall order is the sum of the powers in the experimental rate lawrate = k[A]1[C]2  ⇒  order = 1 + 2
Answer  · Order = 3 — first order in A, zero order in B, second order in C.
Why  · Read the powers straight off the rate law. B is absent, which means its power is zero, not that it is missing. The stoichiometric coefficients (2, 1, 1) play no part — order is experimental.
NCERT  · 
1(C)(ii)Chemical Kinetics[1]
How will the rate be affected when the concentration of all reactants A, B and C is doubled?
Formula used  · Multiply each concentration by its factor, raised to its orderrate ratio = (2)1 × (2)0 × (2)2
Answer  · The rate becomes 8 times the original.
Why  · A contributes 21 = 2 and C contributes 22 = 4; B contributes 20 = 1, i.e. nothing. 2 × 4 = 8.
NCERT  · 
1(C)(iii)Chemical Kinetics[2]
a. How will the rate be affected when the concentration of B alone is doubled?   b. How will the rate be affected when the concentration of C alone is increased to 1·2 mol L−1?
Answer  · a. No change at all — the rate is unaffected.
b. The rate becomes 9 times the original.
Why  · a. B is zero order, so its concentration simply does not enter the rate law: 20 = 1. b. [C] goes from 0·4 to 1·2, a factor of 3, and C is second order, so the rate changes by 32 = 9.
NCERT  · 
1(C)(iv)Chemical Kinetics[1]
Calculate the initial rate of reaction after 0·04 mol L−1 of A has been reacted.
Formula used  · The rate law, with the concentrations left after the reaction has run; amounts consumed are in the ratio of the stoichiometric coefficientsrate = k[A][C]2,   Δ[C] = ½ × Δ[A]  (since 2A consumes 1C)
  1. [A] = 0·10 − 0·04 = 0·06 mol L−1
  2. [C] = 0·40 − (0·04 ÷ 2) = 0·40 − 0·02 = 0·38 mol L−1
  3. rate = 2·0 × 10−5 × 0·06 × (0·38)2 = 2·0 × 10−5 × 0·06 × 0·1444
Answer  · rate = 1·73 × 10−7 mol L−1 s−1
Why  · The step everyone misses is that C is consumed too. The equation is 2A + B + C → …, so for every 2 mol of A that react, 1 mol of C reacts: 0·04 of A means 0·02 of C.
NCERT  · 
Common slip  · Leaving [C] at 0·40 gives 1·92 × 10−7 — close enough to look right, which is exactly why the error survives.

Q2Chemical Kinetics[2]
i. k (the rate constant), Ea (activation energy) and A (Arrhenius constant) are 3 × 10−4 s−1, 104·4 kJ mol−1 and 6·0 × 1014 s−1 respectively. What is the value of k when T → ∞?   ii. What type of molecules undergo effective collisions?
Formula used  · The Arrhenius equation, taken to its high-temperature limitk = Ae−Ea/RT; as T → ∞, Ea/RT → 0, so e0 = 1 and k → A
Answer  · i. k = A = 6·0 × 1014 s−1.
ii. Molecules that collide with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy and with the correct orientation.
Why  · i. The exponential is the fraction of collisions energetic enough to react; at infinite temperature every collision clears the barrier, so that fraction reaches 1 and the rate constant rises to its ceiling A. The value of Ea and the given k are there to be ignored. ii. Energy alone is not enough — properly oriented collisions are what the steric factor P accounts for.

Q3Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers[2]
An alcohol (A) with molecular formula C4H10O gives instant turbidity with the Lucas test. It reacts with P and I2 to give an alkyl iodide (B) and with Na metal it gives a sodium salt (C). When (B) and (C) are heated together, a new compound (D) with molecular formula C4H8 along with (A) is formed. Identify A to D with chemical equations.
  1. (CH3)3COH + P/I2 → B = (CH3)3C–I
  2. 2(CH3)3COH + 2Na → 2C = (CH3)3C–ONa + H2
  3. (CH3)3CI + (CH3)3CONa → D = (CH3)2C=CH2 + (CH3)3COH (A) + NaI
Answer  · A = (CH3)3C–OH, 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tert-butyl alcohol)  ·  B = (CH3)3C–I  ·  C = (CH3)3C–ONa, sodium tert-butoxide  ·  D = (CH3)2C=CH2, 2-methylpropene.
Why  · Instant turbidity with Lucas reagent is the giveaway: only a tertiary alcohol reacts at once, because it ionises to a stable 3° carbocation. C4H10O with a tertiary –OH can only be tert-butyl alcohol. The last step looks like Williamson's ether synthesis but is not: a tertiary halide with a bulky alkoxide cannot do SN2, so the alkoxide acts as a base and eliminates instead — which is why D is an alkene and A comes back.
NCERT  · 
Common slip  · Expecting di-tert-butyl ether here is the trap the question is built on. SN2 cannot reach a tertiary carbon, so elimination wins.
Q4Carboxylic Acids / Aldehydes[2]
i. The pKa value of 4-nitrobenzoic acid is lower than that of benzoic acid. Explain.   ii. Dialkyl cadmium is considered superior to a Grignard reagent for the preparation of a ketone from an acid chloride. Explain.
R–COCl  →R′2Cd  R–CO–R′  (stops here)     R–COCl  →R′MgX  R–CO–R′  →R′MgX  R–C(OH)R′2
Answer  · i. –NO2 is strongly electron-withdrawing (−I and −R). From the para position it delocalises the negative charge of the carboxylate anion, stabilising it. A more stable anion means a stronger acid, and a stronger acid has a lower pKa.
ii. R2Cd is a much weaker nucleophile than RMgX, so it stops cleanly at the ketone. A Grignard reagent is so reactive that it attacks the ketone it has just made and goes on to a tertiary alcohol.
Why  · Both parts turn on the same idea from opposite ends: acid strength is decided by the stability of the anion left behind, and selectivity is decided by whether a reagent is reactive enough to attack its own product.
NCERT  ·  — for the acidity half; dialkyl cadmium is not in the Class XII book
Q5Chemical Kinetics[2]
A first order reaction is 50% completed in 30 minutes at 300 K and in 10 minutes at 320 K. Calculate the activation energy of the reaction.
Formula used  · ‘50% completed’ is the half-life, and for first order t½ = 0·693/k, so k ∝ 1/t½. Then the two-temperature Arrhenius equationlog k2k1 = Ea2·303 R × T2 − T1T1T2
  1. log 3 = 0·4771
  2. Ea = 2·303 × 8·314 × 0·4771 × 300 × 32020
  3. Ea = 9·136 × 4800 = 43850 J mol−1
Answer  · Ea = 43850 J mol−1 ≈ 43·85 kJ mol−1
Why  · You never have to find either rate constant — only their ratio, which is the inverse ratio of the half-lives: k2/k1 = 30/10 = 3.

Common slip  · T1T2 is a product (96000), not a sum, and T2 − T1 is 20, not 620.
Q6Alcohols / Carbonyls[2]
How can the following conversions be brought about? i. Ethanal to propan-2-ol   ii. Phenol to benzoic acid.
CH3CHO  →CH3MgBr  CH3CH(OMgBr)CH3  →H3O+  CH3CH(OH)CH3
Answer  · i. CH3CHO + CH3MgBr in dry ether, then hydrolyse with H3O+ → CH3CH(OH)CH3.
ii. Phenol → (Zn dust, distil) benzene → (CH3Cl / anhyd. AlCl3) toluene → (KMnO4/KOH, then H3O+) benzoic acid.
Why  · i. Count carbons: ethanal has 2, propan-2-ol has 3, so exactly one carbon must be added — a methyl Grignard. The classic route removes the –OH first with zinc dust, because a phenolic –OH would otherwise interfere with the Friedel–Crafts step.

Q7Coordination Compounds[2]
i. FeSO4 solution mixed with (NH4)2SO4 solution in 1:1 molar ratio gives the test of Fe2+, but CuSO4 solution mixed with aqueous ammonia in 1:4 molar ratio does not give the test of Cu2+. Explain why.   ii. Arrange the following complexes in increasing order of conductivity in solution: [Co(NH3)3Cl3], [Co(en)3]Cl3, [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2, [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl
Answer  · i. Q8Carbonyls / Phenols[2]
An organic compound (A) has a characteristic odour; on treatment with NaOH it forms two compounds (B) and (C). Compound (B) has molecular formula C7H8O which on partial oxidation gives back (A). The compound (C) is a sodium salt of an acid. When (C) is treated with soda lime it yields an aromatic hydrocarbon (D). Deduce the structures of (A), (B), (C) and (D).
  1. 2C6H5CHO + NaOH → C6H5CH2OH (B) + C6H5COONa (C)  — Cannizzaro
  2. C6H5COONa + NaOH  →CaO, Δ  C6H6 (D) + Na2CO3
Answer  · A = C6H5CHO, benzaldehyde  ·  B = C6H5CH2OH, benzyl alcohol  ·  C = C6H5COONa, sodium benzoate  ·  D = C6H6, benzene.
Why  · ‘One compound + NaOH → an alcohol and a salt of an acid’ is the Cannizzaro reaction, the disproportionation that aldehydes with no α-hydrogen undergo. C7H8O for B fixes the ring size, and B oxidising back to A confirms A is the aldehyde. Soda lime then decarboxylates the salt.

(i) Write the main product formed in each case: a. C6H5OH + FeCl3 →  b. C6H5OH + CHCl3 + NaOH →   (ii) Arrange in increasing order of boiling point: C2H5–O–C2H5, C4H9COOH, C4H9OH
Answer  · (i) a. A violet-coloured complex, [Fe(OC6H5)6]3− — the standard test for a phenol.
(i) b. Salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde) — the Reimer–Tiemann reaction.
(ii) C2H5OC2H5 < C4H9OH < C4H9COOH

Q10Chemical Kinetics[2]
In a reaction mA + nB → products, when the concentration of A is doubled the rate of reaction is also doubled. When the concentration of B is doubled the rate becomes four times. Find the overall order of the reaction.
Formula used  · Order in each reactant from the factor by which the rate changesif [X] × 2 changes the rate by 2p, then the order in X is p; overall order = sum of the orders
Answer  · Order in A = 1, order in B = 2, so the overall order is 3. The rate law is rate = k[A][B]2.
Why  · Doubling A doubles the rate: 2p = 2 gives p = 1. Doubling B quadruples it: 2q = 4 gives q = 2.
NCERT  · 
Common slip  · m and n are not the orders. Order comes from experiment, never from the balanced equation — which is precisely why the question writes the coefficients as letters.
Q11Carboxylic Acids[2]
Write the chemical test to distinguish between the following pairs of compounds: (i) Benzoic acid and phenol   (ii) Formic acid and acetic acid.
C6H5COOH + NaHCO3 → C6H5COONa + H2O + CO2↑
Answer  · (i) NaHCO3 test. Benzoic acid gives brisk effervescence of CO2; phenol gives none. (Equally good: neutral FeCl3 — phenol gives a violet colour, benzoic acid a buff precipitate.)
(ii) Tollens' reagent. Formic acid gives a silver mirror; acetic acid gives no reaction.
Why  · (i) A carboxylic acid is strong enough to liberate CO2 from bicarbonate; an ordinary phenol is not. (ii) Formic acid, HCOOH, uniquely contains an –CHO group as well as –COOH, so it is the one carboxylic acid that behaves as a reducing agent.

Q12Organic conversions[3]
i. C6H5Br → A → B   ii. HCHO → C + D   iii. CH3CHO → E → F
Answer  · Read as the standard Heritage scheme, with the reagents the arrows imply:
i. C6H5Br →Mg / dry ether A = C6H5MgBr →CO2, then H3O+ B = C6H5COOH.
ii. HCHO has no α-hydrogen, so with conc. NaOH it undergoes Cannizzaro: C = CH3OH and D = HCOONa.
iii. CH3CHO has an α-hydrogen, so with dilute NaOH it undergoes the aldol reaction: E = CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO (3-hydroxybutanal), which on heating loses water to give F = CH3CH=CH–CHO (but-2-enal).
Why  · The pair (ii) and (iii) is the point of the question: the same reagent gives completely different chemistry depending on whether the aldehyde has an α-hydrogen. No α-H → Cannizzaro; α-H present → aldol.

Q13Solutions[3]
A solution containing 0·5 g of KCl dissolved in 100 g of water freezes at −0·24 °C. Calculate the degree of dissociation of the salt. [Kf for water = 1·86 K kg mol−1]
Formula used  · Depression of freezing point with the van't Hoff factor, then the degree of dissociation for an electrolyte giving n ionsΔTf = i Kf m   and   α = i − 1n − 1
  1. Molar mass of KCl = 39 + 35·5 = 74·5 g mol−1
  2. molality m = 0·5 / 74·50·100 kg = 0·0671 mol kg−1
  3. i = ΔTfKf m = 0·241·86 × 0·0671 = 1·923
  4. α = 1·923 − 12 − 1 = 0·923
Answer  · α = 0·923, i.e. the KCl is 92·3% dissociated.
Why  · ΔTf is bigger than Kfm would predict, so i > 1 — the salt is splitting into more particles. KCl gives two ions, so n = 2 and the denominator n − 1 is simply 1, which makes α = i − 1.
NCERT  · 
Common slip  · ΔTf is the magnitude of the drop, 0·24 K — pure water freezes at 0 °C, so ΔTf = 0 − (−0·24). Putting −0·24 in gives a negative i.
Q14Carbonyls[3]
Give balanced chemical equations with conditions where necessary for the following name reactions: i. Rosenmund's reduction  ii. Perkin reaction  iii. Benzoin condensation.
Answer  · i. Rosenmund's reduction — acid chloride to aldehyde, over a poisoned catalyst:
R–COCl + H2  →Pd/BaSO4, S or quinoline  R–CHO + HCl
ii. Perkin reaction — an aromatic aldehyde with an acid anhydride and its sodium salt:
C6H5CHO + (CH3CO)2O  →CH3COONa, Δ  C6H5CH=CH–COOH + CH3COOH
iii. Benzoin condensation — two benzaldehydes joined by cyanide ion:
2C6H5CHO  →KCN, ethanol  C6H5CH(OH)–CO–C6H5
Why  · All three are about stopping at the right place or joining two carbons. The whole point of Rosenmund's is the poison: without sulphur or quinoline the palladium would reduce the aldehyde straight on to the alcohol. Perkin gives cinnamic acid, and benzoin condensation needs CN− because it is the only ion that can act as the leaving group and the stabilising group at once.
NCERT  ·  — Rosenmund only — Perkin and benzoin condensation are not in the Class XII book

i. [Fe(CN)6]4− is a coordination complex ion. a. What is the oxidation number of iron? b. How many unpaired electrons are there? c. State the hybridisation and magnetic behaviour.   ii. On the basis of CFT, write the electronic configuration of a d4 ion if a. Δo > P  b. Δo < P.

Q17Biomolecules[3]
i. Give a reason for each: (a) Except glycine, all other naturally occurring α-amino acids are optically active. (b) A zwitter ion is also known as a dipolar ion.   ii. From the structures of protein shown: a. Which type of structures do A and B represent? b. How can these structures be converted to the primary structure?
Answer  · i.(a) An α-amino acid has four groups on its α-carbon — –NH2, –COOH, –H and –R. When R is anything but hydrogen all four differ, so the carbon is a stereocentre and the molecule is chiral. In glycine R = H, so two of the groups are the same and the molecule is achiral.
i.(b) In the zwitter ion the –COOH has given its proton to the –NH2, so the molecule carries both a positive (–NH3+) and a negative (–COO−) centre while remaining overall neutral — two poles, hence dipolar.
ii.a. A and B are secondary structures — the α-helix and the β-pleated sheet.
ii.b. By denaturation: heating, or adding acid, alkali or urea, breaks the hydrogen bonds that hold the coil or sheet together and the chain uncoils to its primary structure. The peptide bonds are not broken, so the sequence survives.
Why  · The thread through the whole question is which bonds are strong and which are weak. Secondary structure is held by hydrogen bonds, which heat can undo; primary structure is held by covalent peptide bonds, which it cannot. Coagulation of egg white on boiling is the everyday example.

Q20Coordination Compounds[5]
i. Which type of ligand is EDTA?  ii. Why is EDTA used in the treatment of lead poisoning?  iii. What is the geometry of the EDTA complex?  iv. The complex CoBr3·4NH3·2H2O has molar conductivity corresponding to a 3:1 electrolyte. Write the molecular formula and its IUPAC name.  v. Draw the geometrical isomers of [Co(NH3)4Cl2]+.
Answer  · i. EDTA is a hexadentate ligand — it binds through four carboxylate oxygens and two nitrogens — and therefore a chelating ligand.
ii. Because it forms an exceptionally stable, water-soluble chelate with Pb2+. Wrapping six donor atoms round one ion gives a large positive entropy of chelation, so the lead is locked up and excreted in the urine instead of binding to enzymes.
iii. Octahedral — six donor atoms, six coordination positions.
iv. A 3:1 electrolyte gives four ions, one of them a tripositive complex cation and three bromides, so all four NH3 and both H2O must be inside: [Co(NH3)4(H2O)2]Br3, tetraamminediaquacobalt(III) bromide.
v. Two geometrical isomers: the cis form, with the two Cl adjacent (90° apart), and the trans form, with the two Cl opposite (180° apart).
Why  · Part iv is the whole of Werner's method in one line: the conductivity tells you how many ions, which tells you how many groups are outside the coordination sphere, which fixes the formula. Ligands are then named alphabetically — ammine before aqua.

  2. k = 2·30350 log 10060 = 2·30350 × 0·2218 = 0·01022 min−1
