Question 1 (a)  ·  Fill in the blanks5

1(a)(i)Ionic equilibrium[1]
Solubility of calcium oxalate is ______ in the presence of ammonium oxalate because of ______.
Answer  · decreased;  because of the common ion effect.
Why  · Ammonium oxalate supplies extra C2O42−, an ion the calcium oxalate equilibrium already contains. By Le Chatelier's principle the equilibrium CaC2O4(s) ⇌ Ca2+ + C2O42− shifts to the left, so less dissolves — the ionic product must stay equal to Ksp.
NCERT  ·  — (not in the NCERT Class XII book) Ionic equilibrium is a Class XI topic and is not in the Class XII book.
1(a)(ii)Alcohols and Phenols[1]
Ethanol is ______ to litmus while phenol is ______ to litmus.
Answer  · neutral;  acidic.
Why  · Phenol's conjugate base, the phenoxide ion, is stabilised by delocalisation of the negative charge into the benzene ring, so phenol gives up its proton (pKa ≈ 10). An ethoxide ion has no such delocalisation — the alkyl group is even +I and pushes charge onto the oxygen — so ethanol (pKa ≈ 16) is effectively neutral.

1(a)(iii)Solutions[1]
The ______ pressure of an aqueous solution of 0·1 M cane sugar is ______ than that of pure water measured at the same temperature.
Answer  · vapour pressure;  lesser than.
Why  · Raoult's law: the vapour pressure of the solvent is proportional to its mole fraction. Dissolving a non-volatile solute lowers that mole fraction and, on the surface picture, sugar molecules occupy part of the surface so fewer water molecules can escape. Hence the lowering of vapour pressure, the colligative property from which the other three follow.

1(a)(iv)Amines[1]
On treating a ______ with ______ nitrogen gas is evolved.
R–NH2 + HNO2 → [R–N2+Cl−] → R–OH + N2↑ + HCl
Answer  · a primary amine  with  nitrous acid.
Why  · An aliphatic primary amine gives a diazonium salt that is far too unstable to survive, so it decomposes at once, releasing N2 quantitatively and leaving the alcohol. Secondary amines give a nitrosamine and tertiary amines a salt — neither evolves nitrogen, which is what makes this a test.

1(a)(v)Electrochemistry[1]
The more ______ the reduction potential of a metal, the ______ is its ability to displace hydrogen from acids.
Answer  · the more negative;  the greater.
Why  · Displacing hydrogen means the metal is oxidised while H+ is reduced. For that to be spontaneous the metal must be the better reducing agent of the two, i.e. sit below hydrogen in the series — which is exactly what a more negative E°red means. Zinc (−0·76 V) displaces hydrogen; copper (+0·34 V) does not.

Question 1 (c)  ·  Explain the following facts5

1(c)(i)Phenols[1]
Unlike most phenolic compounds, 2,4,6-trinitrophenol is soluble in sodium bicarbonate solution.
Answer  · 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid) is a genuinely strong acid, pKa ≈ 0·4 — comparable with a mineral acid. It is therefore strong enough to liberate CO2 from NaHCO3 and dissolve as its sodium salt. An ordinary phenol (pKa ≈ 10) is far too weak to do so and dissolves only in the stronger base NaOH.
Why  · The three –NO2 groups are each −I and −R, and two of them are ortho and one para, so all three can delocalise the phenoxide's negative charge by resonance. That is an enormous stabilisation of the anion, and a well-stabilised anion means a strong acid.
NCERT  · 
This is the standard way of separating a carboxylic acid from a phenol — NaHCO3 dissolves the acid only. Picric acid is the exception that proves the rule.
1(c)(ii)Electrochemistry[1]
Specific conductance of a solution decreases with dilution.
Answer  · Specific conductance (conductivity, κ) is the conductance of one unit volume of solution — a 1 cm cube. On dilution the number of ions in that fixed volume falls, so κ falls.
Why  · The contrast with molar conductivity is the point. Λm = κ/c refers to all the ions from one mole, however much solvent they are spread through, and that increases on dilution because more of the electrolyte dissociates. So the two quantities move in opposite directions, and the question is testing whether you know which is which.
NCERT  · 
1(c)(iii)d- and f-Block[1]
Transition metals form complex compounds.
Answer  · Because their ions are small and highly charged, so they have a high charge-to-size ratio and polarise ligands strongly; and because they have vacant d orbitals of the right energy to accept lone pairs from ligands.
Why  · Both conditions are needed. A small, highly charged ion attracts electron pairs; the vacant (n−1)d, ns and np orbitals lie close enough in energy to hybridise and actually accommodate them. Alkali metal ions are large and singly charged with no accessible d orbitals, which is why they form almost no complexes.
NCERT  · 
1(c)(iv)p-Block[1]
Interhalogen compounds are more reactive than their constituent elements.
Answer  · The X–X′ bond in an interhalogen is weaker than the X–X bond in either halogen (fluorine excepted), because the two atoms differ in size and electronegativity, so the bond is polar and the overlap is poorer. A weaker bond breaks more easily, so the compound is more reactive.
Why  · Reactivity here means how readily the molecule dissociates to give halogen atoms or ions. The polarity also makes the larger, less electronegative atom a ready electrophilic centre.

1(c)(v)Ionic equilibrium[1]
Zinc sulphide is precipitated from an ammoniacal solution in the presence of hydrogen sulphide.
Answer  · Ammonia removes H+ from the solution, which drives the weak-acid equilibrium H2S ⇌ 2H+ + S2− to the right and raises [S2−] sharply. The ionic product [Zn2+][S2−] then exceeds the Ksp of ZnS, so ZnS precipitates.
Why  · In acidic solution the opposite happens: the added H+ suppresses the ionisation of H2S by the common ion effect, [S2−] stays tiny, and ZnS — which has a relatively large Ksp — does not precipitate. CuS, with a far smaller Ksp, precipitates even then. That difference is the whole basis of the Group II / Group IV separation in qualitative analysis.

1(d)(i)Coordination Compounds[1]
The number of unpaired electrons in the complex ion [CoF6]3− will be: (a) 2  (b) 3  (c) 4  (d) 0
Answer  · (c) 4
Why  · Co3+ is d6. F− is a weak-field ligand, so Δo < P and the electrons spread out before pairing: t2g4eg2, which leaves four unpaired. The complex is high spin, outer orbital (sp3d2) and paramagnetic. With a strong-field ligand such as NH3 the same d6 ion would be t2g6 and diamagnetic.

Q2(a)(i)Solutions[2]
A solution containing 8 g of compound per 100 mL was found to have an osmotic pressure of 500 cm of Hg at 27 °C. Calculate the molecular mass of the compound.
Formula used  · The van't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure, rearranged for molar massπV = nRT = wMRT   ⇒   M = wRTπV
  1. π = 50076 = 6·579 atm
  2. V = 0·1 L,  T = 273 + 27 = 300 K,  w = 8 g
  3. M = 8 × 0·0821 × 3006·579 × 0·1 = 197·040·6579 = 299·5 ≈ 300 g mol−1
Answer  · M ≈ 300 g mol−1
Why  · Everything is unit conversion. 500 cm Hg must become atmospheres (76 cm Hg = 1 atm), 100 mL must become 0·1 L, and 27 °C must become 300 K, so that R = 0·0821 dm3 atm K−1 mol−1 can be used.

What happens to the boiling point of water when a small amount of sodium chloride is dissolved in it?
ΔTb = i Kb m
Answer  · The boiling point rises — elevation of boiling point.
Why  · NaCl is non-volatile, so it lowers the vapour pressure of the water. The liquid must then be heated above 100 °C before its vapour pressure again equals atmospheric pressure. Q2(b)(i)Solid State[2]
The density of A+B−, which has the CsCl (bcc) structure, is 4·4 g/cm3. Calculate the edge length of the crystal. [Formula mass = 213]
Formula used  · Density of a cubic unit cell; for the CsCl structure there is one formula unit per cell, so Z = 1d = Z × Ma3 × NA   ⇒   a3 = Z × Md × NA
  1. a3 = 1 × 2134·4 × 6·022 × 1023 = 8·04 × 10−23 cm3
  2. a = (8·04 × 10−23)1/3 = 4·32 × 10−8 cm

Why  · The only thing to get right is Z. The CsCl arrangement looks body-centred, but the body-centre and the corners are different ions, so it is a simple cubic lattice of each ion and the cell contains one Cs+ (8 × ⅛) and one Cl− (1 × 1) — one formula unit. Taking Z = 2 as for a true bcc metal gives 544 pm, which is wrong.

Q2(b)(ii)Solid State[1]
What is the coordination number of copper in its crystal?
Answer  · 12.
Why  · Copper crystallises in the face-centred cubic (cubic close-packed) lattice. In any close-packed arrangement each sphere touches six in its own layer, three above and three below — twelve in all. That is the maximum possible for equal spheres, and the packing efficiency is 74%.

Q2(b)(iii)Ionic equilibrium[1]
An aqueous solution of acetic acid and sodium acetate having pH = 4·2 is diluted to half the initial concentration. What will be the pH of the new solution?
Formula used  · The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for an acidic bufferpH = pKa + log [salt][acid]
Answer  · pH = 4·2, unchanged.
Why  · Dilution divides the salt and the acid concentrations by the same factor, so their ratio is unchanged — and only the ratio appears in the Henderson equation. This is precisely the buffer property the question is testing: a buffer resists change in pH on dilution as well as on addition of small amounts of acid or alkali.

Q2(c)(ii)Solid State[1]
Explain the term Ferrimagnetic.
Answer  · A ferrimagnetic substance is one whose magnetic moments (domains) are aligned in opposite directions but in unequal numbers, so the opposing moments do not cancel and the substance has a small net magnetic moment. Examples: Fe3O4 (magnetite) and the ferrites MFe2O4.
Why  · It sits between the two extremes. In a ferromagnetic solid all the moments point the same way, giving a large moment; in an anti-ferromagnetic solid they are equal and opposite and cancel exactly, giving none. Ferrimagnetics are weakly attracted, and lose their magnetism on heating, becoming paramagnetic.

Q3(a)(i)Electrochemistry[2]
The values of λ°m for HCl, NaCl and CH3COONa are 426·1, 126·5 and 91 ohm−1 cm2 mol−1 respectively. Calculate the value of λ°m for acetic acid.
Formula used  · Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions — combine the three known electrolytes so that the unwanted ions cancelΛ°(CH3COOH) = Λ°(HCl) + Λ°(CH3COONa) − Λ°(NaCl)
Λ° = 426·1 + 91 − 126·5 = 390·6 ohm−1 cm2 mol−1
Answer  · Λ°m(CH3COOH) = 390·6 ohm−1 cm2 mol−1
Why  · Check the bookkeeping and you can see why the combination works: (H+ + Cl−) + (CH3COO− + Na+) − (Na+ + Cl−) leaves exactly H+ + CH3COO−. This is the only way to get Λ° for a weak electrolyte, because its conductivity cannot be extrapolated to zero concentration directly.

Q3(a)(ii)Chemical Kinetics[3]
The temperature coefficient for the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate by sodium hydroxide solution is 1·75. Calculate the energy of activation for the reaction.
Formula used  · The temperature coefficient is the ratio of rate constants ten degrees apart, conventionally at 298 K and 308 K; feed it into the two-temperature Arrhenius equationlog k308k298 = Ea2·303 R × T2 − T1T1T2
  1. log 1·75 = 0·2430
  2. Ea = 2·303 × 8·314 × 0·2430 × 298 × 30810
  3. Ea = 4·653 × 9178·4 = 42712 J mol−1
Answer  · Ea ≈ 42712 J mol−1 = 42·7 kJ mol−1
Why  · ‘Temperature coefficient’ is simply k308/k298, so the ratio you need is handed to you as 1·75 and no concentrations are involved at all. For most reactions the coefficient is between 2 and 3, so 1·75 is a slightly sluggish reaction and a moderate Ea is the expected answer.
NCERT  · 
If 293 K and 303 K are used instead, Ea comes to 41·3 kJ mol−1. Either is acceptable provided the two temperatures are stated.
Q3(b)Ionic equilibrium[2]
Deduce the equation to calculate the pH of an acid buffer solution.
Answer  · For a weak acid HA with its salt NaA:
Ka = [H+][A−] / [HA]  ⇒  [H+] = Ka × [HA]/[A−]
Taking −log of both sides gives the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:
pH = pKa + log ([salt] / [acid])
Why  · Two approximations make it work, and both are justified by the buffer's composition: the weak acid is so little ionised (further suppressed by the common ion from the salt) that [HA] equals the acid taken, and the salt is fully ionised so [A−] equals the salt taken. It follows immediately that pH = pKa when salt and acid are equal, which is where a buffer works best.
NCERT  ·  — (not in the NCERT Class XII book) Buffers are a Class XI topic and are not in the Class XII book.
Q3(c)Electrochemistry[1]
AgNO3 solution cannot be stored in a copper vessel — explain.
Answer  · Copper is a stronger reducing agent than silver (E°Cu2+/Cu = +0·34 V against E°Ag+/Ag = +0·80 V), so it displaces silver from the solution, dissolving the vessel and depositing silver:
Cu(s) + 2AgNO3(aq) → Cu(NO3)2(aq) + 2Ag(s)
Why  · E°cell = 0·80 − 0·34 = +0·46 V, which is positive, so ΔG° is negative and the reaction is spontaneous. The blue colour of Cu2+ appearing in the solution is the visible sign.

Q4(b)(i)Chemical Kinetics[2]
What is the Arrhenius equation? How can it be used graphically to calculate the energy of activation for a reaction?
Formula used  · The Arrhenius equation, and its linear formk = Ae−Ea/RT  ⇒  log k = log A − Ea2·303 R · 1T
Answer  · The equation relates the rate constant to temperature: k = Ae−Ea/RT, where A is the frequency (Arrhenius) factor, Ea the activation energy and R the gas constant.
Graphically: plot log k against 1/T. The linear form above shows this is a straight line of slope −Ea/2·303 R and intercept log A. Measuring the slope therefore gives Ea = −2·303 R × slope.
Why  · The exponential term is the fraction of molecules with energy at or above Ea. Taking logarithms is what turns an awkward exponential into a straight line, which is why the log k against 1/T plot is the standard experimental method. The slope is negative and Ea positive — the two minus signs cancel.

Q4(b)(ii)Ionic equilibrium[2]
The solubility product of BaSO4 is 1·5 × 10−9. Find its solubility in 0·1 M BaCl2 solution.

Q4(c)Solid State[3]
(i) How many formula units of sodium chloride are present in a unit cell of sodium chloride? (ii) To what type of cubic system does this cell belong? (iii) What is the geometry of sodium ions around chloride ions?
Formula used  · Counting atoms in a cubic cell — a corner is shared by 8 cells, a face centre by 2, an edge centre by 4, a body centre by 1Cl−: 8 × ⅛ + 6 × ½ = 4     Na+: 12 × ¼ + 1 = 4
Answer  · (i) 4 formula units.  (ii) Face-centred cubic (rock-salt structure) — the Cl− ions form an fcc lattice with Na+ in all the octahedral holes.  (iii) Octahedral — six Na+ around each Cl−, so the coordination is 6:6.
Why  · Do the counting rather than remembering it. Chloride sits at 8 corners (⅛ each) and 6 face centres (½ each) = 4; sodium sits at 12 edge centres (¼ each) and the body centre (1) = 4. Equal numbers, as the formula NaCl requires, and 4 formula units per cell.

Write the formulae of the following coordination compounds: (i) Potassium dicyanoargentate(I)  (ii) Hexaaquairon(III) chloride

Q5(b)Coordination Compounds[1]
Using C.F.T., determine the number of unpaired electrons for the FeCl4− complex.
Answer  · 5 unpaired electrons.
Why  · Charge balance: x + 4(−1) = −1 gives Fe(III), which is d5. The complex is tetrahedral (four ligands), and in a tetrahedral field Δt is only about 4/9 of Δo — always smaller than the pairing energy. So tetrahedral complexes are invariably high spin: e2t23, all five electrons unpaired. Cl− is a weak-field ligand as well, which points the same way.

Q5(c)Coordination Compounds[2]
Explain why tetrahedral nickel complexes are paramagnetic but square planar Ni(II) complexes are diamagnetic.
Answer  · Ni(II) is d8 in both. In a tetrahedral complex the splitting Δt is small, the ligands are weak-field, and the last two electrons stay unpaired in the higher set — two unpaired electrons, so paramagnetic (sp3). In a square planar complex a strong-field ligand pushes the dx2−y2 orbital very high, so all eight electrons pair up in the four lower orbitals leaving it empty — no unpaired electrons, so diamagnetic (dsp2).
Why  · The geometry is not the cause but the consequence: it is the strength of the ligand field that decides both. A strong-field ligand such as CN− forces pairing, which frees an inner d orbital for dsp2 and gives square planar; a weak-field ligand such as Cl− cannot, so the metal falls back on sp3 and tetrahedral.

Q6(a)(i)d- and f-Block[2]
Explain: silver bromide is used in photography.
Answer  · AgBr is light-sensitive. Light falling on the emulsion reduces the silver ions to metallic silver, forming the invisible latent image, which the developer then amplifies:
2AgBr  →hν  2Ag + Br2
Unexposed grains are washed away by the fixer (hypo) as the soluble complex Na3[Ag(S2O3)2], leaving a permanent image.
Why  · The photo-decomposition works because Ag+ is easily reduced and the Ag–Br bond has enough covalent character to absorb visible light. AgCl behaves similarly but is less sensitive; AgI is used in the fastest emulsions.

Q6(a)(ii)d- and f-Block[2]
Explain: in a given transition series there is no significant change in the atomic radii of the elements with increase in atomic number.
Answer  · Across a transition series, each added proton increases the nuclear charge and pulls the shell in, but each added electron goes into the inner (n−1)d subshell, which screens the outermost ns electrons from that extra charge. The two effects very nearly cancel, so the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons — and hence the radius — changes very little.
Why  · d electrons screen poorly compared with s and p electrons, but they screen well enough for the balance to hold across the middle of the series. The radius does fall slightly at the start and rise again near the end, where electron–electron repulsion in the filling d orbitals begins to win.

Q6(b)p-Block[1]
Draw the structure of xenon oxyfluoride molecules.
Answer  · XeOF4 — sp3d2, six electron pairs (5 bond + 1 lone) → square pyramidal, with the O at the apex, four F in the plane and the lone pair below.
XeO2F2 — sp3d, five electron pairs (4 bond + 1 lone) → see-saw (trigonal bipyramidal with one equatorial position occupied by the lone pair); the two F are axial and the two O equatorial.
XeO3F2 — sp3d, five bond pairs and no lone pair → trigonal bipyramidal, with the two F axial and the three O equatorial.
Why  · Count with VSEPR each time: total electron pairs decide the arrangement, and the lone pairs then decide the shape by taking the roomiest positions — equatorial in a trigonal bipyramid, because an axial lone pair would suffer three 90° repulsions instead of two.

Q7(b)Metallurgy[2]
How is silver extracted from its sulphide ore by the cyanide process? Give only the reactions.
Answer  · Leaching — the ore is treated with dilute NaCN in the presence of air, which dissolves the silver as a soluble dicyanoargentate(I) complex:
Ag2S + 4NaCN ⇌ 2Na[Ag(CN)2] + Na2S
Displacement — the silver is then precipitated with the more electropositive zinc:
2Na[Ag(CN)2] + Zn → Na2[Zn(CN)4] + 2Ag↓
Why  · The first equilibrium is unfavourable on its own; air is bubbled through to oxidise the Na2S away, which pulls the equilibrium to the right. The second step works because zinc is above silver in the electrochemical series. It is the same Mac Arthur–Forrest process used for gold.

Q8(a)Organic conversions[6]
How can the following conversions be brought about? (i) Benzoic acid to phenol  (ii) Acetaldehyde to acetamide  (iii) Aniline to fluorobenzene  (iv) Propionic acid to acetic acid
Answer  · (i) Benzoic acid → phenol (four steps, through the diazonium salt):
C6H5COOH  →NH3, Δ  C6H5CONH2  →Br2/KOH  C6H5NH2  →NaNO2/HCl, 273–278 K  C6H5N2+Cl−  →H2O, Δ  C6H5OH
(ii) Acetaldehyde → acetamide:
CH3CHO  →[O], KMnO4/H+  CH3COOH  →NH3  CH3COONH4  →Δ, −H2O  CH3CONH2

C6H5NH2  →NaNO2/HCl, 273–278 K  C6H5N2+Cl−  →HBF4  C6H5N2+BF4−  →Δ  C6H5F + N2 + BF3

C2H5COOH  →NH3, Δ  C2H5CONH2  →Br2/KOH  C2H5NH2  →HNO2  C2H5OH  →[O]  CH3COOH
Why  · Count the carbons first — it tells you which tool you need. (i) and (iv) both lose one carbon, and the standard way to do that is the Hofmann bromamide degradation, which converts an amide to an amine with one carbon fewer. (iii) cannot be done by direct fluorination, which is far too violent; the diazonium fluoroborate route is the only practical way to put F on a ring.

Q8(b)Amines / Nitriles[2]

Q9(b)Structure determination[4]
An organic compound [A] with molecular formula C3H6O, which reduces Tollens' reagent, gives [B] with hydroxylamine. [B] on reduction with LiAlH4 gives [C]. [C] on warming with chloroform and potassium hydroxide gives [D] with an unpleasant smell. [D] on reduction with Na/C2H5OH gives [E], which gives an insoluble compound with benzenesulphonyl chloride and NaOH. Give the sequence of reactions and suggest structures of [A] to [E].
  1. CH3CH2CHO + NH2OH → CH3CH2CH=NOH (B) + H2O
  2. B + 4[H]  →LiAlH4  CH3CH2CH2NH2 (C) + H2O
  3. C + CHCl3 + 3KOH → CH3CH2CH2NC (D) + 3KCl + 3H2O  — carbylamine
  4. D + 4[H]  →Na/C2H5OH  CH3CH2CH2NHCH3 (E)
Answer  · A = CH3CH2CHO, propanal  ·  B = CH3CH2CH=N–OH, propanal oxime  ·  C = CH3CH2CH2NH2, propan-1-amine  ·  D = CH3CH2CH2NC, propyl isocyanide  ·  E = CH3CH2CH2NHCH3, N-methylpropan-1-amine.
Why  · Each clue pins one structure. Reduces Tollens' → A is an aldehyde, and C3H6O leaves only propanal (the isomer acetone is a ketone and would not). Hydroxylamine gives an oxime; LiAlH4 reduces C=N to CH–NH2, giving a primary amine. Chloroform and KOH with an unpleasant smell is the carbylamine test, which only primary amines give — confirming C. Reducing an isocyanide gives a secondary amine, and the last clue closes the loop: in the Hinsberg test a 2° amine gives a sulphonamide with no N–H left, so it cannot dissolve in alkali — the insoluble compound.

Q9(c)Chemical tests[3]
Give one chemical test to distinguish between: (i) Formic acid and oxalic acid  (ii) Glucose and fructose
Answer  · (i) Add calcium chloride solution (to the neutralised acid): oxalic acid gives a white precipitate of calcium oxalate, which is insoluble in acetic acid; formic acid gives none. Alternatively, warm with Tollens' reagent: formic acid gives a silver mirror, oxalic acid does not.
(ii) Bromine water: glucose, an aldose, is oxidised to gluconic acid and decolourises it; fructose, a ketose, does not. (Seliwanoff's test works the other way — fructose gives a red colour rapidly.)
Why  · (i) Formic acid, HCOOH, is the only carboxylic acid that also contains a –CHO group, so it alone reduces Tollens'. Oxalic acid is distinguished instead by the insolubility of its calcium salt. (ii) Bromine water is a mild oxidant: it oxidises an aldehyde group but not a ketone. This is exactly why bromine water — and not Tollens' or Fehling's — distinguishes the two sugars: in alkaline Tollens' or Fehling's, fructose isomerises to glucose and gives a positive test too.
NCERT  · 
Common slip  · Naming Tollens' or Fehling's for (ii) is the standard error. Both are alkaline, and alkali converts fructose to glucose, so both sugars respond.

Q10(b)Biomolecules[2]
On electrolysis in acidic solution amino acids migrate towards the cathode, while in alkaline solution they migrate towards the anode. Explain.
Answer  · An amino acid exists as the zwitter ion, +H3N–CHR–COO−, which is overall neutral. In acid the excess H+ protonates the –COO−, leaving a net positive cation, which moves to the cathode. In alkali the OH− removes a proton from –NH3+, leaving a net negative anion, which moves to the anode.
Why  · The amino acid is amphoteric — it has an acidic and a basic group on the same molecule, so it responds to whichever is offered. At the intermediate pH where the two cancel exactly, the isoelectric point, the molecule is electrically neutral and does not migrate at all; that pH is also where its solubility is at a minimum, which is how proteins are separated by electrophoresis.

Deficiency of which vitamin will cause: (i) night blindness (ii) rickets?
Answer  · (i) Vitamin A   (ii) Vitamin D

Q10(d)Polymers[2]
Name the type of polymerisation and the monomers in each of the following polymers: (i) Dacron  (ii) Nylon-66
Answer  · (i) Dacron (Terylene) — condensation (step-growth) polymerisation; monomers ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid. It is a polyester.
(ii) Nylon-66 — condensation (step-growth) polymerisation; monomers hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid. It is a polyamide.
Why  · Both are condensation polymers because each linkage forms with the loss of a small molecule (water). The difference is only in which groups meet: –OH with –COOH gives an ester link, –NH2 with –COOH gives an amide link. Contrast addition polymers such as polythene, where nothing is lost.
