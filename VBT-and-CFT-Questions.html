The orbitals themselves

Both theories are about what the five d orbitals do when ligands come near, so start with their shapes. Everything later follows from where the lobes point.

1Shapes of s, p and d — ISC pattern — assumed by every VBT question
Draw the shapes of an s orbital and of the three p orbitals. Why can one s and three p orbitals be mixed into four equivalent hybrid orbitals?
How to see it  · Hybridisation mixes orbitals of similar energy. What matters for shape is that the s orbital is spherical and contributes no direction of its own, while the three p orbitals are identical to each other and differ only in which axis they lie along.
  1. An s orbital is a sphere centred on the nucleus. It has no lobes and no preferred direction.
  2. Each p orbital is two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, with a node at the nucleus itself; px, py and pz are the same shape along the three axes.
  3. Mixing one spherical orbital with three identical mutually perpendicular ones gives four orbitals of equal energy and equal shape, pointing at the corners of a tetrahedron — 109°28′ apart.
Answer  · s is spherical; each p is a two-lobed dumbbell along one axis. Because s is directionless and the three p orbitals are equivalent, the four sp³ hybrids come out identical, which is what a regular tetrahedral shape requires.
2The five d orbitals — ISC pattern — the basis of CFT
Draw the five d orbitals. Divide them into two groups according to where their lobes point, and name the two groups.
How to see it  · Read each name as an instruction. dxy lies in the xy plane; dx²−y² lies in the same plane but along the axes themselves. That single difference — between the axes or along them — is what divides the five.
  1. Lobes between the axes: dxy, dyz, dzx. Each lies in the plane of the two axes it is named for, with its four lobes at 45° to both. These three are the t2g set.
  2. Lobes along the axes: dx²−y² (four lobes on the x and y axes) and dz² (two lobes on z plus a collar in the xy plane). These two are the eg set.
  3. In a free ion all five have exactly the same energy — they are degenerate. It takes an external field to tell them apart.
Answer  · Three point between the axes (dxy, dyz, dzx — the t2g set) and two point along them (dz², dx²−y² — the eg set).
Watch out  · dz² looks unlike the other four, but it belongs with dx²−y²: its lobes lie on the z axis, so it too points straight at a ligand.
3Why the degeneracy is lifted — ISC pattern — the central idea of CFT
The five d orbitals of a free metal ion are degenerate. Explain, with a diagram, why they are no longer degenerate when six ligands approach octahedrally.
How to see it  · A ligand is a lone pair — a concentration of negative charge. An electron in a metal d orbital is repelled by it. So ask, for each d orbital, how close its lobes come to the incoming ligands.
  1. The six ligands come in along the x, y and z axes.
  2. The lobes of dx²−y² and dz² point straight at them. An electron in either is repelled strongly, so those two orbitals are raised in energy.
  3. The lobes of dxy, dyz and dzx point between the ligands. Repulsion is weaker, so those three are lowered.
  4. The average energy is unchanged — the five orbitals split about a barycentre.
Answer  · The ligands approach along the axes, so the two orbitals whose lobes lie along the axes are pushed up and the three whose lobes lie between them are pushed down. The degeneracy is lifted by unequal electrostatic repulsion, not by bonding.
4Reading the labels — ISC pattern
What do the labels t2g and eg stand for, and why is the tetrahedral pair written t2 and e with no g?
How to see it  · The letter counts the orbitals; the subscript g is a symmetry label.
  1. t means a set of three orbitals of the same energy; e means a set of two.
  2. The subscript g (German gerade, meaning even) says the orbital is symmetric about a centre of inversion.
  3. An octahedron has a centre of symmetry, so the g may be written: t2g and eg.
  4. A tetrahedron has no centre of symmetry, so the label is meaningless there and is dropped: the sets are written t2 and e.
Answer  · t = a set of three, e = a set of two; g marks symmetry about a centre of inversion. An octahedron has such a centre and a tetrahedron does not, so the tetrahedral labels carry no g.
Watch out  · Writing ‘t2g’ for a tetrahedral complex is a standard slip, and it is visible to an examiner at a glance.

Valence Bond Theory

VBT asks one question: which empty metal orbitals accept the ligands' lone pairs? Answer it and the hybridisation, the shape and the magnetic behaviour all follow. The routine below works for every complex ISC sets.

5The routine — ISC pattern — the method behind the rest of this section
Set out the steps by which valence bond theory predicts the hybridisation, shape and magnetic behaviour of a complex.
How to see it  · Every VBT question is the same five steps in the same order. Learn the order and the individual complexes become bookwork.
  1. Find the oxidation number of the metal from the charge on the complex ion and the charges on the ligands.
  2. Write the dn configuration of that ion. Remember the 4s electrons leave first, so Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶ and not 3d⁴4s².
  3. Decide whether the ligand is strong field (CN⁻, CO, NH₃, en, NO₂⁻) or weak field (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, H₂O, C₂O₄²⁻). A strong field forces the d electrons to pair up and vacate inner d orbitals.
  4. Count the empty orbitals now available and hybridise as many as there are ligands: six ligands using two inner (n−1)d orbitals gives d²sp³; six using outer nd gives sp³d²; four gives dsp² (square planar) or sp³ (tetrahedral).
  5. Count the electrons left unpaired. n = 0 is diamagnetic; n > 0 is paramagnetic, with μ = √n(n+2) BM.
Answer  · Oxidation number → dn → strong or weak field → count the empty orbitals and hybridise → count the unpaired electrons.
Watch out  · Step 2. The configuration is that of the ion, not the atom. Co is 3d⁷4s², but Co³⁺ is 3d⁶.
6[Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ — Heritage 2024–25 First Term, Q17
For [Fe(CN)6]⁴⁻ state (a) the oxidation number of iron, (b) the number of unpaired electrons, (c) the hybridisation and the magnetic behaviour. (At. no. Fe = 26)
How to see it  · CN⁻ is the strongest of the ordinary ligands, so expect pairing and an inner orbital complex.
  1. x + 6(−1) = −4, so iron is +2, and Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶.
  2. CN⁻ is strong field, so the six d electrons pair into the three t2g-type orbitals: n = 0.
  3. That vacates two 3d orbitals, which hybridise with 4s and the three 4p to give d²sp³ — an inner orbital complex.
  4. μ = √0(0+2) = 0, so the complex is diamagnetic.
Answer  · (a) +2   (b) 0   (c) d²sp³, octahedral, inner orbital, diamagnetic.
Watch out  · [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ is the other question of this pair: there iron is +3 and d⁵, so t2g⁵ leaves one unpaired electron and the complex is paramagnetic. One electron changes the answer to (b) and (c) both.
7[Cr(NH₃)₆]³⁺ — ISC Specimen 2027, Q4(i) — CISCE keyCISCE key
For the complex ion [Cr(NH3)6]³⁺: (a) what is the hybridisation state of the central metal atom? (b) comment on the magnetic nature of the complex ion. (At. no. Cr = 24)
How to see it  · Cr³⁺ is d³, and d³ is the easy case: three electrons go singly into the three t2g orbitals whatever the ligand, so there is no strong field / weak field decision to make.
  1. NH₃ is neutral, so x = +3. Cr is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, so Cr³⁺ is 3d³.
  2. Three electrons occupy three of the five 3d orbitals singly (Hund's rule), leaving two 3d orbitals empty without any pairing being needed.
  3. Those two, with 4s and three 4p, give d²sp³ — octahedral, inner orbital.
  4. Three electrons are unpaired, so μ = √3(5) = √15 = 3·87 BM.
Answer  · (a) d²sp³   (b) paramagnetic, three unpaired electrons, μ = 3·87 BM.
Watch out  · CISCE's key gives the reason as “there are three unpaired electrons” — state the number, not just the word ‘paramagnetic’.
8[Fe(en)₂Cl₂]Cl — ISC Specimen 2026, Q(ii)(a) — CISCE keyCISCE key
For the coordination compound [Fe(en)2Cl2]Cl identify: (1) the oxidation state, (2) the hybridisation of the central metal atom, (3) the magnetic behaviour, (4) the geometry.
How to see it  · One chloride is outside the square brackets, so it is a counter-ion and takes the charge off the complex ion. Count the donor atoms carefully: en is bidentate.
  1. The ion is [Fe(en)₂Cl₂]⁺. en is neutral and each Cl is −1, so x + 0 + (−2) = +1 and iron is +3; Fe³⁺ is 3d⁵.
  2. Donor atoms: 2 en × 2 = 4, plus 2 Cl = 6, so the coordination number is 6 and the geometry is octahedral.
  3. en is a strong field ligand; the five d electrons pair into t2g as t2g⁵, leaving two 3d orbitals empty → d²sp³.
  4. One electron is left unpaired, so the complex is paramagnetic, μ = √1(3) = 1·73 BM.
Answer  · (1) +3   (2) d²sp³   (3) paramagnetic   (4) octahedral.
Watch out  · Counting en as one donor atom gives a coordination number of 4 and the wrong geometry. Two nitrogen atoms, two bonds.
9[Ni(CO)₄] by the valence bond approach — ISC 2020 Board, Q17(b)(ii)
Using the valence bond approach, predict the shape, hybridisation and magnetic behaviour of [Ni(CO)4]. (At. no. Ni = 28)
How to see it  · CO is neutral, so nickel is in oxidation state zero — this is the one common complex where the metal is not a cation. Start from the atom, 3d⁸4s².
  1. CO is a neutral ligand and the complex is neutral, so Ni is 0: 3d⁸4s².
  2. CO is a very strong field ligand. The two 4s electrons are driven into the 3d set, which becomes 3d¹⁰ — completely filled and all paired.
  3. The 3d set is now full, so no d orbital is available. The empty 4s and three 4p orbitals hybridise: sp³.
  4. Four sp³ hybrids accept four CO lone pairs → tetrahedral. No unpaired electrons remain, so it is diamagnetic.
Answer  · sp³ hybridisation, tetrahedral shape, diamagnetic.
Watch out  · Nickel here is Ni(0), not Ni(II). Starting from 3d⁸ instead of 3d⁸4s² loses the two electrons that make the 3d subshell full.
10Same metal, same oxidation state, two shapes — Heritage 2024–25 First Term — also ISC pattern
The geometries of [Ni(CN)4]²⁻ and [NiCl4]²⁻ are ______ and ______ respectively. Explain the difference.
How to see it  · Nickel is +2 in both and d⁸ in both, so the metal cannot be the reason. Only the ligand differs — so the answer must be the ligand's field strength.
  1. Ni²⁺ is 3d⁸ in both: four orbitals paired-or-single as 3d⁸ leaves two unpaired electrons.
  2. CN⁻ is strong field. It forces those two to pair up, emptying one 3d orbital. One 3d + 4s + two 4p = dsp² → square planar, and with every electron paired, diamagnetic.
  3. Cl⁻ is weak field. No pairing occurs, so no 3d orbital is freed. Hybridisation uses 4s and three 4p: sp³ → tetrahedral, two unpaired electrons, paramagnetic, μ = 2·83 BM.
Answer  · [Ni(CN)4]²⁻ is square planar (dsp², diamagnetic); [NiCl4]²⁻ is tetrahedral (sp³, paramagnetic).
Watch out  · Both are four-coordinate, so the coordination number tells you nothing here. The shape is decided by whether the ligand is strong enough to pair the d electrons.
11Inner and outer orbital complexes — ISC 2020 Board Q1 — also Heritage 2024–25 Q1(iv)
Fill in the blanks: Outer orbital complexes involve ______ hybridisation and are ______ spin complexes. Then explain the difference between an inner and an outer orbital complex, using [Co(NH3)6]³⁺ and [CoF6]³⁻.
How to see it  · ‘Inner’ and ‘outer’ refer to which d orbitals the hybridisation uses — the inner (n−1)d or the outer nd.
  1. An inner orbital complex uses two (n−1)d orbitals: d²sp³. Getting them empty requires pairing, so it is low spin.
  2. An outer orbital complex uses two nd orbitals: sp³d². No pairing is needed, so it is high spin.
  3. Co³⁺ is 3d⁶ in both complexes. With NH₃ (strong field) the six electrons pair as t2g⁶, two 3d orbitals empty, d²sp³, inner, diamagnetic.
  4. With F⁻ (weak field) there is no pairing: four electrons stay unpaired, no 3d orbital is free, so the 4d orbitals are used — sp³d², outer, paramagnetic, μ = 4·90 BM.
Answer  · Outer orbital complexes involve sp³d² hybridisation and are high spin complexes. [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ is inner (d²sp³, diamagnetic); [CoF₆]³⁻ is outer (sp³d², four unpaired electrons).
Watch out  · Both complexes are octahedral. Inner against outer is not a question about shape.
12Which is paramagnetic? — ISC 2024 Board, Q1(iii)
The metal complex ion that is paramagnetic is: (a) [Fe(CN)4]²⁻ (b) [Co(NH3)6]³⁺ (c) [Ni(CN)4]²⁻ (d) [Cu(NH3)4]²⁺   (At. no. Fe = 26, Co = 27, Ni = 28, Cu = 29)
How to see it  · Work out dn for each and ask whether the electrons can all be paired. One of them cannot pair however strong the ligand — and that is the answer.
  1. (b) Co³⁺ is d⁶ with strong-field NH₃: t2g⁶, all paired — diamagnetic.
  2. (c) Ni²⁺ is d⁸ with strong-field CN⁻: dsp², square planar, all paired — diamagnetic.
  3. (d) Cu²⁺ is d⁹. Nine is odd, so one electron must remain unpaired no matter how the orbitals are arranged — paramagnetic, μ = 1·73 BM.
  4. (a) Fe²⁺ is d⁶ with CN⁻ and pairs up.
Answer  · (d) [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ — Cu²⁺ is d⁹, an odd number of electrons, so it is paramagnetic whatever the ligand does.
Watch out  · An odd dn count (d¹, d³, d⁵, d⁷, d⁹) is always paramagnetic. Checking parity first answers many of these in one line.
13What VBT cannot do — ISC pattern — set regularly as a 2-mark question
State any four limitations of valence bond theory as applied to coordination compounds.
How to see it  · VBT is a bonding model only. Every limitation is something it was never built to describe — energy differences between orbitals, and anything that depends on them.
  1. It gives no explanation of colour. Colour needs energy levels within the d set, and VBT has none.
  2. It cannot predict whether a complex will be inner or outer orbital — the hybridisation is chosen after the magnetic moment has been measured, which makes the argument circular.
  3. It offers no account of the spectrochemical series: nothing in the theory says why CN⁻ is a stronger field ligand than F⁻.
  4. It does not explain the temperature dependence of magnetic data, or the observed relative stabilities and rates of reaction of complexes.
Answer  · No account of colour; the inner/outer choice is assumed from the magnetism rather than predicted; no basis for the spectrochemical series; no explanation of thermal variation of magnetic behaviour or of relative stability.
Watch out  · “It does not explain colour” on its own is one mark. The question usually asks for four.

Crystal Field Theory — the splitting

CFT throws the bonding away and keeps only the electrostatics: the ligand is a point negative charge, and the question is how hard it pushes on each d orbital. That is enough to explain colour and magnetism, which is exactly what VBT could not do.

14Octahedral splitting — ISC Specimen 2027 Q4(ii) prints this diagram
Draw a labelled diagram to show the splitting of the d orbitals in an octahedral crystal field. Mark Δo and the energy of each set relative to the barycentre.
How to see it  · Two levels, with three orbitals below and two above, and the gap between them is Δo. The numbers on the two levels are fixed by the barycentre rule.
  1. The three t2g orbitals are stabilised by −0·4 Δo each.
  2. The two eg orbitals are destabilised by +0·6 Δo each.
  3. Check the barycentre: 3(−0·4) + 2(+0·6) = −1·2 + 1·2 = 0. The average energy is unchanged, which is what ‘splitting about the barycentre’ means.
  4. Δo is also written 10Dq; its size is fixed by the ligand and by the metal's oxidation state.
Answer  · Three t2g orbitals at −0·4 Δo and two eg orbitals at +0·6 Δo, separated by Δo (= 10Dq).
Watch out  · The larger fraction goes with the smaller set. Two orbitals go up by 0·6, three come down by 0·4 — that is what keeps the average unchanged.
15Tetrahedral splitting — ISC Specimen 2026, Q(i)(c) — CISCE keyCISCE key
Draw a diagram to show the splitting of d orbitals in a tetrahedral crystal field, and state how Δt compares with Δo.
How to see it  · In a tetrahedron no ligand lies on an axis. So the set that was worst off in the octahedron is now best off — the diagram turns upside down.
  1. The four ligands approach between the axes, so they come closer to dxy, dyz, dzx. Those three (t2) are now the upper set, at +0·4 Δt.
  2. dz² and dx²−y² (e) are the lower set, at −0·6 Δt.
  3. Barycentre check: 3(+0·4) + 2(−0·6) = 0. ✓
  4. There are only four ligands instead of six, and none points directly at any orbital, so the splitting is much smaller: Δt = (4/9)Δo.
Answer  · e (dz², dx²−y²) lies below t2 (dxy, dyz, dzx), separated by Δt = (4/9)Δo — roughly half the octahedral splitting.
Watch out  · No g subscripts, and the e set is at the bottom. Drawing the octahedral diagram and relabelling it is the usual error.
16Why tetrahedral complexes are always high spin — ISC pattern
Explain why tetrahedral complexes are always high spin, whatever the ligand.
How to see it  · Compare Δt with the pairing energy P. Electrons pair only if the splitting is worth more than the cost of pairing.
  1. Δt = (4/9)Δo, i.e. less than half the octahedral value, for two reasons: there are four ligands instead of six, and none of them points directly at a d orbital.
  2. The pairing energy P is a property of the metal ion and is essentially the same whatever the geometry.
  3. For every known ligand Δt < P.
  4. So electrons always spread out over all five orbitals before pairing — the high-spin arrangement.
Answer  · Because Δt = (4/9)Δo is smaller than the pairing energy P for every known ligand, so pairing is never worthwhile. No low-spin tetrahedral complex is known.
Watch out  · The reason is not ‘tetrahedral ligands are weak’. CN⁻ is a strong field ligand in any geometry; it is the geometry that makes the splitting small.
17Square planar — ISC pattern — explains the d⁸ complexes
Show how the square planar splitting pattern follows from the octahedral one, and use it to explain why square planar d⁸ complexes such as [Ni(CN)4]²⁻ are diamagnetic.
How to see it  · Get to square planar by taking the octahedron and pulling the two ligands on the z axis away to infinity. Then ask what happens to each orbital.
  1. Withdrawing the two z ligands relieves every orbital with a z component: dz², dxz and dyz all fall.
  2. The four remaining ligands move in slightly, so the two orbitals in the xy plane rise — and dx²−y², which points straight at all four, rises furthest.
  3. The order becomes dxz = dyz < dz² < dxy « dx²−y².
  4. Eight electrons fill the lower four orbitals as four pairs and leave dx²−y² empty, because the gap to it is large. No unpaired electron remains.
Answer  · dx²−y² is left far above the other four, so a d⁸ ion puts all eight electrons into those four as four pairs — hence diamagnetic.
18Δo and ligand strength — ISC Specimen 2027, Q4(ii)(b) — CISCE keyCISCE key
Comment on the relationship between the crystal field splitting energy Δo and the strength of the ligand.
How to see it  · Δo is the measure of ligand strength — the two statements are the same statement.
  1. A ligand that repels the d electrons hard produces a large separation between t2g and eg.
  2. So a larger Δo means a stronger field ligand; a smaller Δo means a weaker one.
  3. Once Δo exceeds the pairing energy P, electrons pair rather than occupy eg — so a strong field ligand gives a low spin complex, and a weak field ligand a high spin one.
  4. Arranging ligands in order of increasing Δo is precisely what the spectrochemical series does.
Answer  · Δo increases with the strength of the ligand. A large Δo (strong ligand) gives a low spin complex; a small one gives high spin.
19Crystal field stabilisation energy — ISC pattern
Define crystal field stabilisation energy and write the expression used to calculate it for an octahedral complex.
How to see it  · CFSE measures how much energy the complex gains purely by having its electrons in the split levels rather than in five degenerate ones.
  1. Count the electrons in each set: n(t2g) and n(eg).
  2. Each t2g electron is worth −0·4 Δo; each eg electron +0·6 Δo.
  3. CFSE = [−0·4 n(t2g) + 0·6 n(eg)] Δo, to which the pairing energy of any extra pairs is added.
  4. A negative value means the complex is stabilised. The more negative the CFSE, the more stable the arrangement.
Answer  · CFSE is the net lowering of energy of the d electrons caused by the splitting, CFSE = [−0·4 n(t2g) + 0·6 n(eg)]Δo.
Watch out  · Signs. t2g electrons contribute a negative term (stabilising) and eg electrons a positive one.

High spin, low spin and magnetic moment

The commonest questions in the chapter. All of them are the same calculation: fill the split levels according to Δo against P, count what is left unpaired, and turn that into a magnetic moment.

20The d⁴ ion, both ways — ISC Specimen 2025 Q(ii) and Specimen 2026 Q(ii)(c) — CISCE keyCISCE key
On the basis of crystal field theory, write the electronic configuration of a d⁴ ion in an octahedral field if (a) Δo > P and (b) Δo < P.
How to see it  · The fourth electron has a choice: pair up in t2g, costing P, or go up alone into eg, costing Δo. It takes the cheaper option.
  1. Three electrons go singly into the three t2g orbitals in either case.
  2. (a) Δo > P — pairing is cheaper than promotion, so the fourth pairs up: t2g⁴ eg⁰, low spin, strong field, n = 2.
  3. (b) Δo < P — promotion is cheaper, so the fourth goes up alone: t2g³ eg¹, high spin, weak field, n = 4.
  4. Magnetic moments: μ = 2·83 BM (low spin) and 4·90 BM (high spin).
Answer  · (a) t2g⁴ eg⁰ — low spin, 2 unpaired. (b) t2g³ eg¹ — high spin, 4 unpaired.
Watch out  · Read the inequality the right way round: Δo > P is the strong field and gives the low spin state.
21The d⁵ ion — ISC Specimen 2027, Q4(ii)(a)
Identify the electronic configuration of a d⁵ ion in an octahedral field if Δo < P.
How to see it  · Δo < P is the weak field, so Hund's rule wins throughout: every orbital takes one electron before any takes a second.
  1. Five electrons, five orbitals, weak field — one electron each, all with parallel spins.
  2. That is t2g³ eg².
  3. n = 5, the largest number of unpaired electrons any ion can have, so μ = √5(7) = 5·92 BM.
  4. For contrast, the strong field case would be t2g⁵ with n = 1.
Answer  · t2g³ eg² — high spin, five unpaired electrons, μ = 5·92 BM.
22[Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ against [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ — ISC Specimen 2026, Q(i)(b) — CISCE keyCISCE key
Predict the number of unpaired electrons in [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺ and [Fe(CN)6]³⁻ on the basis of crystal field theory.
How to see it  · Same metal, same oxidation state, same geometry — only the ligand differs. This is the standard question testing whether you know the spectrochemical series.
  1. Iron is +3 in both, so both are d⁵.
  2. H₂O is a weak field ligand: Δo < P, so the five electrons spread out — t2g³ eg², n = 5.
  3. CN⁻ is a strong field ligand: Δo > P, so they pair into t2g — t2g⁵ eg⁰, n = 1.
  4. μ = 5·92 BM and 1·73 BM respectively.
Answer  · [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ has 5 unpaired electrons (t2g³ eg²); [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ has 1 (t2g⁵).
Watch out  · Both are Fe(III). Assuming H₂O makes Fe(II) because it is neutral is a common slip — the charge on the complex fixes the oxidation state.
23Spin-only magnetic moment — ISC pattern — asked in most papers
Write the spin-only formula for magnetic moment and use it to complete the table for n = 0 to 5. What does a measured moment of 4·90 BM tell you about a d⁶ octahedral complex?
How to see it  · The formula converts a count of unpaired electrons into a measurable number, so it is the bridge between the diagram and the experiment — and it runs both ways.
  1. μ = √n(n + 2) BM, with n the number of unpaired electrons.
  2. n = 0 → 0  ·  1 → 1·73  ·  2 → 2·83  ·  3 → 3·87  ·  4 → 4·90  ·  5 → 5·92 BM.
  3. 4·90 BM corresponds to n = 4.
  4. For d⁶ the only arrangement with four unpaired electrons is t2g⁴ eg² — so the complex is high spin, the ligand is weak field, and by VBT it would be an outer orbital sp³d² complex.
Answer  · μ = √n(n+2) BM. A d⁶ complex measuring 4·90 BM has n = 4, so it is high spin with a weak field ligand — t2g⁴ eg².
Watch out  · ‘Spin only’ means orbital contribution is ignored. For the first transition series that is a good approximation and is what ISC expects.
24CFSE of a high spin d⁴ complex — ISC Specimen 2024, Q1
The crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE) for a high spin d⁴ octahedral complex is: (a) −1·6 Δo (b) −1·2 Δo (c) −0·8 Δo (d) −0·6 Δo
How to see it  · Write the configuration first, then apply the formula. Do not try to do it in your head — the two terms have opposite signs.
  1. High spin d⁴ is t2g³ eg¹.
  2. CFSE = [−0·4(3) + 0·6(1)]Δo
  3. = [−1·2 + 0·6]Δo
  4. = −0·6 Δo. No extra pairing is involved in the high spin case, so there is no P term.
Answer  · (d) −0·6 Δo
Watch out  · −1·2 Δo is what you get by forgetting the eg electron altogether — which is why it is offered as option (b).
25Where the choice exists at all — ISC pattern
For which dn configurations does an octahedral complex have a choice between high spin and low spin? Justify your answer.
How to see it  · The choice exists only when an electron could either pair in t2g or go up alone into eg. Count where that is possible.
  1. d¹, d², d³ — the electrons go singly into the three t2g orbitals. No pairing is needed and no promotion is tempting, so there is only one arrangement.
  2. d⁴ to d⁷ — the next electron must either pair in t2g (cost P) or occupy eg (cost Δo). Here the choice is real.
  3. d⁸, d⁹, d¹⁰ — t2g is full and the remaining electrons have nowhere to go but eg. One arrangement again.
  4. So exactly four configurations admit both a high spin and a low spin form.
Answer  · Only d⁴, d⁵, d⁶ and d⁷. For d¹–d³ and d⁸–d¹⁰ the filling is forced, so high spin and low spin describe the same arrangement.

Colour and the spectrochemical series

The reason CFT displaced VBT. A gap of the right size between t2g and eg happens to match visible light, so the splitting that explains magnetism explains colour as well.

26Why complexes are coloured — ISC pattern — set in almost every paper
Explain, on the basis of crystal field theory, why most transition metal complexes are coloured. Why is [Ti(H2O)6]³⁺ violet while [Sc(H2O)6]³⁺ is colourless?
How to see it  · Colour needs an electron to absorb a photon and jump. Here the jump is from t2g to eg — a d–d transition — so it needs an electron in t2g and a vacancy in eg.
  1. Δo for a typical complex corresponds to a photon in the visible range.
  2. An electron absorbs that photon and is promoted t2g → eg. The light we see is what is left over, so the complex appears in the complementary colour to the light absorbed.
  3. Ti³⁺ is 3d¹. Its one electron can be promoted, absorbing in the yellow-green, so the complex looks violet.
  4. Sc³⁺ is 3d⁰. With no d electron at all there is nothing to promote, no d–d transition, and no absorption in the visible — so it is colourless.
Answer  · Colour arises from a d–d transition across Δo, and the complex shows the complementary colour. Ti³⁺ (d¹) has an electron to promote; Sc³⁺ (d⁰) has none, so it is colourless.
Watch out  · A d¹⁰ ion such as Zn²⁺ is colourless for the opposite reason — the subshell is full, so there is no vacancy to be promoted into. Both d⁰ and d¹⁰ are colourless.
27The spectrochemical series — ISC 2026 Board, Q1 — also Specimen 2026
What is the spectrochemical series? Arrange the following in order of increasing field strength: CN⁻, H2O, NH3, F⁻, Cl⁻, en, CO.
How to see it  · The series is the experimental ordering of ligands by the Δo they produce — measured from absorption spectra, which is where the name comes from.
  1. The halides sit at the weak end, and within them the order follows size: Cl⁻ < F⁻.
  2. Then the oxygen and nitrogen donors: H₂O < NH₃ < en.
  3. The carbon donors are strongest of all: CN⁻ < CO.
  4. So: Cl⁻ < F⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO.
  5. The fuller series worth knowing: I⁻ < Br⁻ < S²⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO.
Answer  · The spectrochemical series is ligands arranged in order of increasing Δo. Here: Cl⁻ < F⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO.
Watch out  · The order is experimental, not derived. Nothing in CFT predicts that neutral CO should outrank the anion F⁻ — which is itself one of the theory's limitations.
28Changing the ligand changes the colour — ISC pattern
A solution of [Cu(H2O)4]²⁺ is pale blue; adding excess ammonia turns it deep blue. Explain in terms of crystal field theory.
How to see it  · The metal and its oxidation state do not change, so Δ must. Use the spectrochemical series.
  1. Ammonia displaces water: [Cu(H₂O)₄]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ + 4H₂O.
  2. NH₃ is higher in the spectrochemical series than H₂O, so it produces a larger Δ.
  3. A larger Δ means the d–d transition absorbs a photon of higher energy, i.e. shorter wavelength.
  4. The absorption shifts, and so does the complementary colour that we see — from pale blue to deep blue.
Answer  · NH₃ is a stronger field ligand than H₂O, so Δ increases, the d–d absorption moves to higher energy, and the transmitted colour changes to deep blue.
Watch out  · Say which way Δ moves and why that changes the colour. ‘Ammonia forms a complex’ earns nothing on its own.

The two theories compared

A favourite two- and three-mark question. The short version: VBT explains shape and magnetism but stops there; CFT gives up on covalent bonding and buys colour and the high/low spin distinction with what it saves.

29What CFT cannot do — ISC pattern — set as a 2-mark question
State three limitations of crystal field theory.
How to see it  · CFT's one assumption is that the metal–ligand interaction is purely electrostatic. Every limitation is a consequence of that being an approximation.
  1. It treats the bond as purely ionic and ignores the covalent character that is certainly present.
  2. It cannot account for the spectrochemical series itself: on an electrostatic argument the anion F⁻ should outrank neutral CO, and the opposite is observed.
  3. It takes no account of π-bonding, which is exactly what makes CO and CN⁻ such strong field ligands.
  4. It considers only the metal's d orbitals, ignoring the ligand orbitals altogether.
Answer  · It assumes a purely electrostatic, ionic interaction; it cannot explain the order of the spectrochemical series (neutral CO above the anion F⁻); and it ignores π-bonding and the ligand orbitals entirely.
Watch out  · These are the limitations that led to ligand field theory and molecular orbital theory, which put the covalency back.
30VBT against CFT — ISC pattern
Compare valence bond theory and crystal field theory on: the nature of the metal–ligand bond, what each explains about magnetism, and colour.
How to see it  · One table, three rows. The contrast is sharpest on colour, which is the reason the second theory was needed at all.
  1. The bond. VBT: covalent — a ligand lone pair is donated into an empty hybrid orbital. CFT: purely electrostatic — the ligand is a point negative charge and no bond is formed at all.
  2. Magnetism. VBT explains it after the fact: it fits the hybridisation to the observed moment, so it cannot predict inner against outer. CFT predicts it, from Δo against P.
  3. Colour. VBT offers no explanation whatever. CFT explains it directly, as a d–d transition across Δo.
  4. Shape. VBT gives it straight from the hybridisation; CFT assumes the geometry at the start and works out the consequences.
Answer  · VBT treats the bond as covalent and accounts for shape and, retrospectively, magnetism, but says nothing about colour. CFT treats the interaction as purely electrostatic and predicts both magnetic behaviour and colour from Δo, but assumes the geometry and ignores covalency.
Watch out  · The honest summary is that neither is complete: VBT keeps the bond and loses the energy levels; CFT keeps the energy levels and loses the bond.

The one idea to keep. Both theories start from the same picture — six ligands coming in along the axes — and differ in what they do with it. Valence bond theory asks which empty orbitals accept the lone pairs, and gets shape and magnetism. Crystal field theory asks how hard the ligands push on each d orbital, and gets the high / low spin distinction and colour. The orbital shapes at the top of this page are what both are reasoning about.
