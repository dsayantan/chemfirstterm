Q1Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which of o- and p-dibromobenzene has the higher melting point, and why?
ExplanationMelting point is about how well molecules fit into a crystal, not about polarity. The para isomer is highly symmetrical, so its molecules stack neatly and a great deal of energy is needed to break the lattice apart. Note this runs opposite to boiling point, where the more polar ortho isomer wins — boiling depends on attraction between molecules, melting on packing.Source · Reasoning
Q2Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Electrophilic substitution in haloarenes occurs slowly compared with benzene because:
ExplanationA halogen has two opposing effects. Its −I effect pulls electron density out of the ring, making it poorer at attracting an electrophile — so the ring is deactivated and reacts slowly. Its +R effect donates a lone pair back, but only to the ortho and para positions, which is why the reaction is slow yet still o,p-directing.Source · Reasoning
Q3Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
A solution of KOH hydrolyses CH3CH(Cl)CH2CH3 and CH3CH2CH2CH2Cl. Which is hydrolysed more easily, and why?
Explanation2-Chlorobutane ionises to a secondary carbocation, stabilised by the +I effect and hyperconjugation of two alkyl groups; 1-chlorobutane would have to form a much less stable primary one. Since the SN1 rate is set entirely by carbocation stability, the secondary halide is hydrolysed the more easily.Source · Reasoning
Q4Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which of C6H5CH(CH3)Br and C6H5CH(C6H5)Br is more reactive in an SN1 substitution?
ExplanationBoth ionise to a benzylic carbocation, so the question is which cation is better stabilised. The diphenyl compound gives a cation flanked by two rings, so the positive charge is delocalised over both by resonance — far more effective than one ring plus the weak hyperconjugation of a methyl group.Source · Reasoning
Q5Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Which reacts faster in an SN2 displacement?
ExplanationSN2 needs the nucleophile to attack the carbon from directly behind the leaving group, so the less crowded that carbon, the faster the reaction: 1° > 2° > 3°. Note this is the exact reverse of the SN1 order, which depends on carbocation stability instead — always check which mechanism is named before quoting an order.Source · Reasoning
Q6Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Why can aryl halides not be prepared by reacting phenol with HCl in the presence of ZnCl2?
ExplanationThe Lucas-type route works by protonating the –OH and losing water to give a carbocation, which the chloride then captures. For phenol that would mean a phenyl cation, with the positive charge in an sp2 orbital lying in the plane of the ring, where the π system cannot stabilise it at all. It does not form, so no aryl halide is produced. The C–O bond in phenol also has partial double-bond character and is unusually strong.Source · CFQ
Q7Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
What is the role of a Lewis acid in preparing aryl chlorides and bromides in the dark?
ExplanationA Lewis acid is electron deficient, so it induces heterolytic fission of the halogen molecule and produces the electrophile (X+, or a strongly polarised complex) that the electron-rich ring can attack. In the dark there are no radicals, so substitution goes on the ring; in light the reaction would be a free-radical one on the side chain instead.Source · CFQ
Q8Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
A racemic mixture is optically inactive because:
ExplanationThe two enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light through equal angles in opposite directions, and in an equimolar mixture they cancel exactly — this is called external compensation. Note the molecules themselves are still chiral and still optically active; it is only the mixture that is not. In a meso compound, by contrast, the cancellation is internal, within one molecule.Source · Reasoning
Q9Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
The C–O–H bond angle in alcohols is slightly less than 109°28′, whereas the C–O–C angle in ethers is slightly greater. Why?
ExplanationBoth oxygens are sp3, so both start near the tetrahedral angle. In an alcohol the two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than the pairs repel each other, squeezing the angle below 109°28′. In an ether the two bulky alkyl groups repel each other more strongly still, pushing the angle above it.Source · Reasoning
Q10Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Although the phenoxide ion has more resonating structures than the carboxylate ion, a carboxylic acid is a stronger acid than phenol. Why?
ExplanationCounting resonance structures is not enough — what matters is how much each contributes. In the carboxylate the charge is spread over two electronegative oxygens in two equivalent structures, which is maximally effective. In the phenoxide the charge is pushed onto carbon atoms of the ring, which are far less able to bear it. So the carboxylate is better stabilised despite having fewer structures.Source · Reasoning
Q11Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
(CH3)3C–O–CH3 with HI gives CH3OH and (CH3)3C–I, not (CH3)3C–OH and CH3I, because:
ExplanationHI first protonates the ether oxygen, so that the leaving group is a neutral alcohol rather than an alkoxide. The question is then which C–O bond breaks — and the tert-butyl side breaks on its own because it gives a stable tertiary carbocation, which I− captures. Attack at the methyl carbon by SN2 is possible in principle but far slower, so it does not compete.Source · Reasoning
Q12Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
p-Nitrophenol is more acidic than o-nitrophenol because:
ExplanationBoth isomers are acidified by the −I and −R effects of –NO2, which reach the ortho and para positions alike. What separates them is that in the ortho isomer the –OH is locked into an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the adjacent nitro group, so that proton is less readily given up. Note this is the same chelation that makes o-nitrophenol the more volatile of the two.Source · Reasoning
Q13Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Which reagent converts a primary alcohol to an aldehyde without going further?
ExplanationAlcohol to aldehyde is an oxidation that must be stopped half way. Pyridinium chlorochromate in CH2Cl2 is the standard mild oxidant, and having no water present means the aldehyde is never hydrated and so cannot be oxidised on. The strong acidic oxidants carry straight through to the carboxylic acid, and LiAlH4 runs the reaction the wrong way altogether — it reduces.Source · CFQ
Q14Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
In alcohols, the boiling point decreases with increase in branching because:
ExplanationAll the isomers can hydrogen bond equally through their single –OH, so that is not what separates them. What changes is the shape: a branched molecule is more compact and more nearly spherical, so neighbouring molecules touch over a smaller area and the van der Waals attraction between them falls.Source · Reasoning
Q15Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Arrange in decreasing order of acidity: H2O, ROH, HC≡CH.
ExplanationRead it off the displacement reactions. Treating a sodium alkoxide with water gives back the alcohol, so water is the stronger acid; treating sodium ethynide with water or with an alcohol gives back acetylene, so both are stronger acids than ethyne. The alkyl group's +I effect destabilises the alkoxide, which is why ROH falls below water.Source · CFQ
Q16Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Which chemical test distinguishes phenol from cyclohexanol?
ExplanationNeutral FeCl3 is the test for a phenolic –OH: it forms an intensely violet iron–phenoxide complex. Cyclohexanol is an ordinary secondary alcohol and gives no colour. Bromine water works too — phenol gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol — as does NaOH, in which phenol dissolves as the phenoxide and cyclohexanol does not.Source · CFQ
Q17Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Aliphatic and ______ aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent to give a ______.
ExplanationTollens' reagent, ammoniacal AgNO3, is reduced by both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes, depositing metallic silver as a mirror. That is worth contrasting with Fehling's solution, which gives a red precipitate of Cu2O but is reduced by aliphatic aldehydes only — benzaldehyde does not respond to it.Source · CFQ
Q18Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of acid strength: 4-nitrobenzoic acid, benzoic acid, 3,4-dinitrobenzoic acid, 4-methoxybenzoic acid.
ExplanationAcid strength follows the stability of the carboxylate anion. –NO2 is strongly electron withdrawing, so it disperses the negative charge and strengthens the acid — and two nitro groups do so more than one. –OCH3 is electron releasing by resonance from the para position, so it destabilises the anion and gives the weakest acid of the four.Source · CFQ
Q19Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of reactivity towards hydrolysis: CH3COCl, CH3CONH2, CH3COOCH3, (CH3CO)2O.
ExplanationReactivity of an acid derivative is set by how good the leaving group is and how much the attached atom donates electrons back into the carbonyl. Cl− is the best leaving group and chlorine donates least, so the acid chloride is the most reactive. –NH2 donates its lone pair strongly into the C=O and NH2− is a terrible leaving group, so the amide is the least.Source · CFQ
Q20Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Cyclohexanone forms a cyanohydrin in good yield but 2,4,6-trimethylcyclohexanone does not, because:
ExplanationNucleophilic addition needs CN− to reach the carbonyl carbon, approaching roughly perpendicular to the C=O plane. Three methyl groups placed around the ring block that approach. Note the methyls are also +I, which reduces the positive charge on the carbonyl carbon, but here the dominant effect is steric.Source · Reasoning
Q21Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Monochloroethanoic acid has a higher pKa than dichloroethanoic acid because:
ExplanationChlorine is −I, so it pulls electron density away from the carboxylate and disperses the negative charge, stabilising the anion. Two chlorines do this more than one, so dichloroethanoic acid is the stronger acid. A stronger acid has the lower pKa — so the weaker monochloro acid has the higher one. The inverse relation between strength and pKa is what this question is really testing.Source · Reasoning
Q22Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Would benzaldehyde be more or less reactive than propanal in nucleophilic addition?
ExplanationThe ring is conjugated with the C=O, and resonance feeds electron density towards the carbonyl carbon. That reduces its partial positive charge, so a nucleophile is attracted less strongly and benzaldehyde is less reactive. Having no α-hydrogen is true of benzaldehyde but is irrelevant here — it governs aldol and Cannizzaro, not addition.Source · Reasoning
Q23Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Why is it necessary to control the pH when aldehydes and ketones react with ammonia derivatives?
ExplanationThe reaction needs both things at once: a weakly acidic medium protonates the carbonyl oxygen, which makes the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic. But if the medium is strongly acidic, the H2N–G reagent is protonated too — its nitrogen lone pair is tied up and it can no longer act as a nucleophile. So there is an optimum pH, around 3.5.Source · Reasoning
Q24Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Only one of the two –NH2 groups of semicarbazide takes part in forming a semicarbazone, because:
ExplanationSemicarbazide is H2N–CO–NH–NH2. The nitrogen attached to the C=O has its lone pair drawn into that carbonyl by resonance — the N–C bond takes on double-bond character — so it is no longer available to attack. The terminal –NH2, well away from the carbonyl, keeps its lone pair and is the one that reacts.Source · Reasoning
Q25Aldehydes, Ketones & Carboxylic Acids1 mark
Why are the α-hydrogen atoms of aldehydes and ketones acidic?
ExplanationRemoving an α-hydrogen leaves a carbanion on the carbon next to the C=O. That negative charge is delocalised onto the carbonyl oxygen — an electronegative atom well able to carry it — giving the resonance-stabilised enolate. A stabilised conjugate base means the proton comes off relatively easily. Note the α-carbon is not bonded to oxygen; that is the common misreading, and it is why aldol, iodoform and halogenation all happen at this position.Source · CFQ
