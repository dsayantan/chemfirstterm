Q1Chemical Kinetics1 mark
For A + B → products: doubling [A] at fixed [B] doubles the rate; raising [B] from 0·15 to 0·20 at fixed [A] raises the rate by the same factor of 1·33. The rate law is:
ExplanationDoubling [A] doubles the rate, so 2a = 2 and a = 1. Raising [B] by 1·33 raises the rate by the same 1·33, so b = 1 too. Overall order 2. Note the worksheet's table misprints experiment 3 as [A] = 3; with that value no order fits, and the answer key's own working uses [A] = 1.Source · Kinetics worksheet Q1
Q2Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The rate of a reaction becomes 4 times when the temperature rises from 293 K to 313 K. The activation energy is about:
Explanationlog(k2/k1) = Ea(T2−T1)/(2·303 R T1T2). With log 4 = 0·6021, Ea = 2·303 × 8·314 × 0·6021 × (293×313)/20 = 52 863 J mol−1. Note T1T2 is a product, not a sum — using the sum is what gives the small wrong answer.Source · Kinetics worksheet Q2
Q3Chemical Kinetics1 mark
For the decomposition of ozone, O3 ⇌ O2 + [O] (fast) followed by O3 + [O] → 2O2 (slow), the rate law is:
ExplanationThe slow step gives rate = k2[O3][O]. From the fast pre-equilibrium [O] = k1[O3]/[O2]. Substituting gives second order in ozone and order −1 in oxygen — the reaction is slowed by its own product, which is the standard example of a negative order.Source · Kinetics worksheet Q3
Q4Chemical Kinetics1 mark
A first order reaction has k = 1·15 × 10−3 s−1. The time for 5 g of reactant to fall to 3 g is about:
Explanationt = (2·303/k) log(a/(a−x)) = (2·303/1·15×10−3) log(5/3) = 2002·6 × 0·2218 = 444 s. The masses can be used directly — only their ratio enters, so no conversion to moles is needed.Source · Kinetics worksheet Q4
Q5Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The slope of log k against 1/T is −5841 K. The activation energy is:
Explanationslope = −Ea/2·303R, so Ea = −2·303 × 8·314 × (−5841) = 111 838 J mol−1. The two minus signs cancel: a negative slope must give a positive activation energy, and reporting it negative is the usual slip.Source · Kinetics worksheet Q5
Q6Electrochemistry1 mark
Given E°(Cu2+/Cu) = 0·337 V and E°(Cu2+/Cu+) = 0·153 V, E°(Cu+/Cu) is:
ExplanationPotentials are not additive — free energies are. ΔG°3 = ΔG°1 + ΔG°2 gives −1·F·E° = −2F(0·337) + 0·153F, so E° = 2(0·337) − 0·153 = 0·521 V. Simply adding the two potentials gives 0·490 V, which is the trap.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q1
Q7Electrochemistry1 mark
Λ°m for Ba(OH)2, BaCl2 and NH4Cl are 523·28, 280·0 and 129·8. Λ°m for NH4OH is:
ExplanationBy Kohlrausch's law, Λ°(NH4OH) = ½Λ°(Ba(OH)2) + Λ°(NH4Cl) − ½Λ°(BaCl2) = 261·64 + 129·8 − 140·0 = 251·44. The halving matters: Ba(OH)2 and BaCl2 each carry two anions.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q2
Q8Electrochemistry1 mark
0·01 M CH3COOH has resistance 2220 Ω in a cell of cell constant 0·366 cm−1. With Λ°m = 390, the degree of dissociation is about:
Explanationκ = (1/R) × cell constant = 0·366/2220 = 1·65 × 10−4. Λm = κ×1000/M = 16·5. α = Λm/Λ°m = 16·5/390 = 0·042. A weak acid at this dilution being a few per cent dissociated is the expected size of answer.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q3
Q9Electrochemistry1 mark
For that same acetic acid solution (C = 0·01 M, α = 0·0423), the dissociation constant is:
ExplanationKa = Cα2/(1−α) ≈ Cα2 = 0·01 × (0·0423)2 = 1·78 × 10−5. This is the accepted Ka of acetic acid, which is a useful check that the chain of steps has not gone astray.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q3(d)
Q10Electrochemistry1 mark
On dilution, the specific conductance of an electrolyte solution:
ExplanationSpecific conductance is the conductance of one unit volume, and dilution puts fewer ions in that fixed volume. Molar conductivity does the opposite — it counts all the ions from one mole however much solvent they occupy, so it rises on dilution. Confusing the two is exactly what this question tests.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q4
Q11Electrochemistry1 mark
For Cu + 2Ag+ → Cu2+ + 2Ag with E° = 0·46 V at 298 K, the equilibrium constant is about:
Explanationlog K = nE°/0·0591 = 2(0·46)/0·0591 = 15·57, so K = 1015·57 ≈ 4 × 1015. Forgetting the factor n = 2 gives the 107-sized answer.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q5
Q12Electrochemistry1 mark
A cell with n = 2 has E° = 0·295 V at 25 °C. Its equilibrium constant is:
ExplanationAt equilibrium Ecell = 0, so E° = (0·059/n) log K. Then log K = 0·295 × 2/0·059 = 10, giving K = 1010.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q6
Q13Electrochemistry1 mark
4·5 g of Al (atomic mass 27) is deposited from Al3+. The same charge liberates a volume of H2 at STP of:
ExplanationBy Faraday's second law wAl/wH = EAl/EH = (27/3)/1 = 9, so wH = 0·5 g. Since 2 g of H2 occupies 22·4 L at STP, 0·5 g occupies 5·6 L. The equivalent mass of aluminium is 9, not 27 — that is the step usually missed.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q7
Q14Electrochemistry1 mark
A hydrogen electrode dips in a solution of pH = 10 with H2 at 1 atm. Its oxidation potential is:
ExplanationFor the oxidation ½H2 − e− → H+, E = −0·059 log[H+] = −0·059(−10) = +0·591 V. The reduction potential is the negative of this. Note the worksheet says a solution of HCl at pH 10, which is impossible — HCl is a strong acid. Read it as simply a solution of pH 10.Source · Electrochemistry worksheet Q8
Q15Solutions1 mark
12·48 g of BaCl2 (M = 208·34) in 1 kg of water boils at 373·0832 K (Kb = 0·52). The degree of dissociation is about:
ExplanationΔTb = 0·0832 K gives Mobs = 0·52×12·48×1000/(1000×0·0832) = 78. Then i = 208·34/78 = 2·67, and for n = 3, α = (i−1)/(n−1) = 1·67/2 = 0·835. Dividing by n instead of n−1 gives the 66·8% distractor.Source · Solutions worksheet Q1
Q16Solutions1 mark
20 g of phenol (M = 94) in 1 kg of benzene lowers the freezing point by 0·69 K (Kf = 5·1). The fraction dimerised is:
ExplanationMobs = 5·1×1000×20/(0·69×1000) = 147·8, so i = 94/147·8 = 0·635. For 2A → A2, α = (i−1)/(½−1) = 0·73. i < 1 is the signature of association; using the dissociation formula gives a negative α, which is the signal you have picked the wrong one.Source · Solutions worksheet Q2
Q17Solutions1 mark
Which has the higher boiling point, 1 M glucose or 2 M glucose, and why?
ExplanationΔTb = iKbm is colligative, so the more concentrated solution has the greater elevation. Glucose is a non-electrolyte (i = 1) so the comparison turns on concentration alone — it does not dissociate, which rules out the last option.Source · Solutions worksheet Q3
Q18Solutions1 mark
Molecular masses of polymers are found by osmotic pressure rather than by other colligative properties mainly because:
ExplanationA polymer solution is necessarily dilute in moles, because the molar mass is enormous. Its freezing-point depression might be a thousandth of a degree — unmeasurable — while its osmotic pressure is easily read. It is also measured at room temperature, so the polymer is not heated and degraded.Source · Solutions worksheet Q4
Q19Solutions1 mark
What mass of a solute of molar mass 60 must dissolve in 180 g of water to reduce the vapour pressure to 4/5 of that of pure water? (exact treatment)
ExplanationRelative lowering = 1/5. With n1 = 10 mol of water, the exact relation 1/5 = n2/(n1+n2) gives n2 = 2·5 and 150 g. The school key answers 120 g, which is what the dilute approximation n2/n1 yields — but the solution is not dilute here, the solute being a fifth of all molecules present.Source · Solutions worksheet Q5
Q20Solutions1 mark
KH for N2 in water at 298 K is 105 atm and the mole fraction of N2 in air is 0·8. At 5 atm, the moles of N2 dissolved in 10 mol of water is:
ExplanationPartial pressure of N2 = 0·8 × 5 = 4 atm. x2 = p/KH = 4 × 10−5. Since x2 is tiny, n2 ≈ 10 × x2 = 4 × 10−4 mol. Note the mole fraction itself is the 10−5 distractor.Source · Solutions worksheet Q6
Q21Solutions1 mark
50 g of ethylene glycol (M = 62) in 200 g of water is cooled to −9·3 °C (Kf = 1·86). The mass of ice separating is about:
ExplanationAt that temperature the solution still liquid must have ΔTf = 9·3, which fixes w1 = 1·86×1000×50/(62×9·3) = 161·3 g of water remaining. Ice = 200 − 161·3 = 38·7 g. 161·3 g is the water left, not the ice — the commonest slip.Source · Solutions worksheet Q7
Q22Solutions1 mark
An aqueous solution is 5% urea (M = 60) and 10% glucose (M = 180) by weight. Its freezing point (Kf = 1·86) is about:
ExplanationIn 100 g of solution there are 5 g urea, 10 g glucose and 85 g of water. Depressions add: 1·824 (urea) + 1·216 (glucose) = 3·04 K, so Tf = −3·04 °C. Taking the water as 100 g rather than 85 g gives the wrong answer.Source · Solutions worksheet Q8
Q23Solutions1 mark
Adding mercuric iodide to aqueous KI raises the freezing point because:
ExplanationHgI2 + 2KI → K2[HgI4]: four particles (2K+ + 2I−) become three (2K+ + one complex anion). Depression of freezing point is colligative, so fewer particles means less depression and the freezing point rises.Source · Solutions worksheet Q9(i)
Q24Solutions1 mark
A person with high blood pressure is advised to take little common salt. The osmotic reason is that the extra salt:
Explanationπ = iCRT, so salt raises the osmotic pressure of the body fluid and water is retained to balance it — raising blood volume and pressure. The school key ends ‘causing rupture of blood cells’, which is the wrong way round: a salty plasma is hypertonic, so cells shrink. Rupture needs a hypotonic medium.Source · Solutions worksheet Q9(ii)
Q25Solutions1 mark
Dissolution of NH4Cl in water is endothermic, yet it dissolves readily, because:
ExplanationThe ions held in the lattice become free to move in all directions, so ΔS is large and positive. Since ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, when TΔS > ΔH the free energy change is negative and dissolution is spontaneous despite absorbing heat — which is why the solution feels cold.Source · Solutions worksheet Q10
Q26Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Two isomers of C4H9Br are hydrolysed by aqueous KOH. For A the rate depends on [A] only; for B, which is optically active, it depends on both. A and B are:
ExplanationFirst-order kinetics means SN1, which needs a tertiary halide, so A is tert-butyl bromide. Second-order means SN2, and B must also be chiral — 2-bromobutane has four different groups on C-2, while 1-bromobutane has two hydrogens there and is achiral. B is the one that gives inversion.Source · Halides worksheet Q1
Q27Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Toluene derivatives with Br2 in the presence of heat or UV light give:
ExplanationHeat or light homolyses Br2 into radicals, and a radical attacks the weakest C–H — the benzylic one. A Lewis acid such as Fe or AlCl3 would instead polarise Br2 into an electrophile and give ring substitution. Light means side chain; a Lewis acid means the ring.Source · Halides worksheet Q2(i)/(vi)
Q28Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
3-Methylbut-1-ene + HBr in the presence of peroxide gives mainly:
ExplanationPeroxide switches the addition to a free-radical mechanism, so HBr adds anti-Markovnikov and the bromine goes to the less substituted carbon — the terminal one. Note the peroxide effect is shown by HBr only, not by HCl or HI.Source · Halides worksheet Q2(iii)
Q29Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Benzene with 1-chloropropane and anhydrous AlCl3 gives mainly:
ExplanationThe primary carbocation formed first rearranges by a 1,2-hydride shift to the more stable secondary one before it attacks the ring, so the product is isopropylbenzene (cumene), not propylbenzene. Carbocation rearrangement is the classic limitation of Friedel–Crafts alkylation.Source · Halides worksheet Q2(viii)
Q30Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
C2H5Br gives one product with KCN and a different one with AgCN. They are respectively:
ExplanationCN− is ambident. KCN is largely ionic, so the free ion attacks through carbon and gives the cyanide (nitrile). AgCN is largely covalent, so only the nitrogen lone pair is available and the product is the isocyanide. The names are treacherously similar — read the cation, not the anion.Source · Halides worksheet Q4(i)/(ii)
Q31Haloalkanes & Haloarenes1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of SN2 reactivity: CH3Cl, CH3CH2Cl, (CH3)2CHCl.
ExplanationSN2 needs the nucleophile to reach the carbon from behind, so the less crowded that carbon the faster: methyl > 1° > 2°. This is the exact reverse of the SN1 order, which depends on carbocation stability — always check which mechanism is named.Source · Halides worksheet Q5(i)
Q32Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
CH3CH2Cl →NaCN X →Ni/H2 Y →(CH3CO)2O Z. Z is:
ExplanationNaCN is ionic, so CN− attacks through carbon and the chain gains a carbon: X = propanenitrile. Catalytic hydrogenation reduces the nitrile to the primary amine Y = propan-1-amine, and acetic anhydride acetylates it. Note the product has three carbons in the chain, not two.Source · Alcohols worksheet Q1
Q33Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
(CH3)3C–O–C2H5 with HI gives mainly:
ExplanationHI protonates the ether oxygen; the question is then which C–O bond breaks. The tert-butyl side ionises on its own because it gives a stable tertiary carbocation, which iodide captures. Attack at the ethyl carbon by SN2 is possible in principle but far slower, so it does not compete.Source · Alcohols worksheet Q4
Q34Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
C6H5–O–C2H5 with HBr gives:
ExplanationThe aryl C–O bond has partial double-bond character from delocalisation of the oxygen lone pair into the ring, so it never breaks. Bromide therefore attacks the ethyl carbon, giving phenol and ethyl bromide. Bromobenzene is never a product of ether cleavage.Source · Alcohols worksheet Q4(ii)
Q35Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
Which reagent oxidises a primary alcohol to the aldehyde and no further?
ExplanationPyridinium chlorochromate is the standard mild oxidant, and having no water present means the aldehyde is never hydrated and so cannot be oxidised on. The two strong acidic oxidants carry through to the carboxylic acid; NaBH4 runs the reaction the wrong way — it reduces.Source · Alcohols worksheet Q5(i)
Q36Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers1 mark
An optically active compound A on mild oxidation gives B, which with I2/NaOH gives iodoform and an acid C. Vigorous oxidation of A gives the same C. A is:
ExplanationA is optically active, so it has a chiral carbon; it oxidises to a compound giving iodoform, so that product is a methyl ketone and A is a secondary alcohol with a CH3–CH(OH)– group. The smallest such alcohol that is chiral is butan-2-ol. Propan-2-ol gives iodoform but is achiral; the tertiary alcohol cannot be oxidised at all.Source · Alcohols worksheet Q6
Q37Aldehydes, Ketones & Acids1 mark
Benzophenone oxime, Ph2C=NOH, with concentrated H2SO4 gives:
ExplanationThis is the Beckmann rearrangement: a strong acid removes the –OH and the group anti to it migrates from carbon to nitrogen, turning a ketoxime into a substituted amide. It is how cyclohexanone oxime is converted industrially to caprolactam.Source · Carbonyls worksheet Q1(ii)
Q38Aldehydes, Ketones & Acids1 mark
Formaldehyde with ammonia gives:
Explanation6HCHO + 4NH3 → (CH2)6N4 + 6H2O. Hexamethylenetetramine (urotropine) is a cage molecule used as a urinary antiseptic. Formaldehyde is special here because it has no α-hydrogen, so it cannot instead undergo the aldol reaction.Source · Carbonyls worksheet Q2(i)
Q39Aldehydes, Ketones & Acids1 mark
Phenol with bromine in CS2 at low temperature gives:
ExplanationIn a non-polar solvent at low temperature the reaction stops at monobromination. In water the same reagents give the white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol, because the highly ionised phenoxide is far more activated. The solvent decides how far it goes — that is the whole point of the question.Source · Carbonyls worksheet Q2(ii)
Q40Aldehydes, Ketones & Acids1 mark
Arrange in decreasing order of acid strength: benzoic acid, p-methoxybenzoic acid, p-toluic acid, o-nitrobenzoic acid.
ExplanationAcidity tracks the stability of the carboxylate. –NO2 withdraws electrons strongly and stabilises it; –CH3 and –OCH3 donate and destabilise it. –OCH3 is weakest because from the para position it donates by resonance as well as induction.Source · Carbonyls worksheet Q3
Q41Aldehydes, Ketones & Acids1 mark
Formaldehyde with concentrated NaOH gives methanol and sodium formate. This reaction is possible because formaldehyde:
ExplanationThe Cannizzaro reaction is a disproportionation: one molecule is reduced to the alcohol and another oxidised to the acid salt. It is available only to aldehydes with no α-hydrogen — formaldehyde and benzaldehyde — because otherwise the aldol reaction takes over.Source · Carbonyls worksheet Q4(iii)
Q42Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Cu(NH3)4][PtCl4] and [Pt(NH3)4][CuCl4] are:
ExplanationBoth the cation and the anion are complexes, and the ligands have been exchanged between the two metal centres. That is coordination isomerism, and it can only arise when both ions of a salt are complexes.Source · Coordination worksheet Q1(i)
Q43Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Co(pn)2Cl2]+ and [Co(tn)2Cl2]+, where pn is propane-1,2-diamine and tn is propane-1,3-diamine, are:
Explanationpn and tn are different ligands that are isomers of each other, both C3H10N2 — that is ligand isomerism. The school key answers ‘linkage’, which is wrong: linkage isomerism needs one ambidentate ligand binding through different atoms, and neither pn nor tn is ambidentate — each binds through its two nitrogens and nothing else.Source · Coordination worksheet Q1(ii)
Q44Coordination Compounds1 mark
For [Cr(H2O)2(C2O4)2]−, the hybridisation and magnetism are:
ExplanationCharge balance x + 2(0) + 2(−2) = −1 gives Cr(III), which is d3. Three electrons singly occupy t2g whatever the ligand strength, so two inner 3d orbitals are always vacant and the complex is d2sp3 with three unpaired electrons. d3 is the case where high and low spin coincide.Source · Coordination worksheet Q2(i)
Q45Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2 exists in a nitro form and a nitrito form. These are:
ExplanationNO2− is ambidentate: bound through nitrogen it is nitrito-κN (the nitro form), through oxygen nitrito-κO. Two compounds differing only in which donor atom is used are linkage isomers — and they differ in colour, which is how they were first told apart.Source · Coordination worksheet Q3
Q46Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Co(NH3)3Cl3] shows two geometrical isomers called:
ExplanationFor an MA3B3 octahedral complex the three like ligands either occupy one triangular face (fac) or lie around a meridian (mer). cis and trans is the naming for MA4B2 and square planar MA2B2 instead.Source · Coordination worksheet Q4
Q47Coordination Compounds1 mark
For [Co(NH3)6]3+, the oxidation number, hybridisation and magnetic behaviour are:
ExplanationSix neutral ammine ligands on a +3 ion means Co is +3, which is d6. NH3 is a strong-field ligand for cobalt(III), so all six electrons pair into t2g: none unpaired, hence diamagnetic, with two inner 3d orbitals freed for d2sp3.Source · Coordination worksheet Q5
Q48Coordination Compounds1 mark
The formula of potassium trioxalatoaluminate(III) is:
ExplanationThe -ate ending means the complex is the anion, so potassium sits outside and is written first. Al is +3 and three oxalates give 3 × (−2) = −6, so the ion is −3 overall and needs three K+.Source · Coordination worksheet Q6(i)
