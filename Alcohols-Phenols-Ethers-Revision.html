1Three families, one oxygen

Every question in this chapter starts by asking, silently, what class is this? Get that wrong and nothing downstream works.

All three families are water with a hydrogen replaced by carbon. That single substitution is the whole chapter:

H—O—HWater the parent
R—O—HAlcohol one H replaced by an alkyl group
Ar—O—HPhenol one H replaced by an aryl group — OH on the ring
R—O—R′Ether both H atoms replaced
The commonest classification trap. Benzyl alcohol C6H5CH2OH is not a phenol. The OH sits on a CH2 that happens to be attached to a ring, not on the ring carbon itself. It is a primary alcohol and behaves like one — it gives no colour with FeCl3, it does not dissolve in NaOH, and it oxidises to benzaldehyde. A phenol needs the oxygen bonded directly to an sp2 ring carbon.

Then: how many carbons touch the carbinol carbon?

The carbinol carbon is the one carrying the OH. Count the other carbons bonded to it — nothing else matters, not the size of the molecule, not the total carbon count.

How many carbon atoms are attached to the C–OH carbon?
0 or 1 → primary (1°) CH3OH, CH3CH2OH, (CH3)2CHCH2OH — note the last one is 1° even though it is branched, because the branch is one carbon further along
2 → secondary (2°) (CH3)2CHOH, cyclohexanol
3 → tertiary (3°) (CH3)3COH, 2-methylbutan-2-ol
Also worth naming allylic CH2=CH–CH2OH and benzylic C6H5CH2OH — formally 1°, but their carbocations are resonance stabilised, so they react like 3° in SN1 and in the Lucas test
Why this matters. The class fixes the answer to almost every later question: which product oxidation gives, how fast the Lucas test turns cloudy, whether dehydration is easy or hard, whether SN1 or SN2 runs, and which Grignard you need to make it. Classify first, every time.

Degree of unsaturation — a 10-second sanity check

For CnH2n+2O the molecule is saturated and acyclic: it can only be an alcohol or an ether, never a phenol. C4H10O fits, so no ring is possible. But C7H8O has four degrees of unsaturation — a benzene ring — so cresol and anisole are candidates and butanol is not.

2Hydrogen bonding — boiling point and solubility

Almost every physical-property question is this one picture applied twice: once to boiling point, once to solubility.

The same molecular formula, a 102 K difference in boiling point. Only the alcohol can donate a hydrogen bond, so only the alcohol forms an extended network that has to be broken before it boils.

The two-part rule. To hydrogen bond with itself a molecule must both donate (needs H on O, N or F) and accept (needs a lone pair). An ether can only accept. That is why ethers boil near the alkane of the same mass — but still dissolve in water, because water supplies the H and the ether supplies the lone pair.

Ordering boiling points

Compare in this order and stop at the first difference:

Falling boiling point, C4H10O and relatives

2-Methylpropan-2-ol — 3°, OH buried in branches

Butane — no oxygen at all

  1. Can it hydrogen bond to itself? Alcohol › ether ≈ alkane of similar mass. This dominates everything else.
  2. Among alcohols, is the chain branched? Branching makes the molecule more spherical: less surface contact, weaker van der Waals forces, and the OH is partly shielded. So 1° › 2° › 3° for isomers.
  3. How many OH groups? Each one adds a hydrogen-bonding site. Ethane-1,2-diol boils at 470 K, glycerol at 563 K.
  4. Only then, molecular mass.
Ortho-nitrophenol boils lower than para-nitrophenol. This looks backwards until you see where the hydrogen bond goes. In the ortho isomer the OH and the NO2 are next to each other, so the hydrogen bonds inside the same molecule — chelation. That H is used up and unavailable to neighbours. In the para isomer the groups are too far apart to reach, so the bonding is intermolecular and the molecules cling to each other. Consequences: o-nitrophenol is more volatile (it steam-distils, which is how the two are separated) and less soluble in water; p-nitrophenol is the higher-boiling, more soluble solid.

Solubility in water

The OH end is hydrophilic and hydrogen bonds to water; the hydrocarbon tail is hydrophobic and gets in the way. Solubility is a tug of war between the two, and the tail wins as it grows:

AlcoholCarbonsSolubility in water
Methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol1–3 completely miscible
Butanol4partly soluble (~8 g/100 mL)
Hexanol and beyond≥6essentially insoluble

For isomers of the same formula the order flips relative to boiling point: 3° is the most soluble, because a compact branched tail disrupts water's own structure less than a long straight one. So butan-1-ol boils highest but 2-methylpropan-2-ol dissolves best.

3Counting isomers without missing any

"How many isomers of C4H10O?" is a counting question, not a chemistry one — but only if you count systematically.

Work down a fixed list. Skeletons first, then position, then function.

Alcohols — 4

  • Straight chain C–C–C–C, OH at position 1 → butan-1-ol (1°)
  • Straight chain, OH at position 2 → butan-2-ol (2°)
  • Branched skeleton, OH on the end → 2-methylpropan-1-ol (1°)
  • Branched skeleton, OH on the branch point → 2-methylpropan-2-ol (3°)

Ethers — 3

  • 1 + 3 split → methoxypropane (CH3OC3H7, n-propyl)
  • 1 + 3 split, branched → 2-methoxypropane (isopropyl)
  • 2 + 2 split → ethoxyethane
Answer: 7 in total — 4 alcohols + 3 ethers. Chain and position isomerism live inside each family; alcohol–ether is functional isomerism across them. A question that says "structural isomers" wants all 7; one that says "isomeric alcohols" wants 4.

The chirality check

A carbon is a stereocentre when it carries four different groups. Butan-2-ol qualifies — C2 holds H, OH, CH3 and C2H5 — so it is the only one of the seven that is optically active, and it exists as a pair of enantiomers. Butan-1-ol fails (two H on C1), and 2-methylpropan-2-ol fails (two identical CH3 groups). This is why SN1 versus SN2 questions so often use butan-2-ol: it is the smallest alcohol whose stereochemistry you can watch.

Worth memorising. C3H8O has 3 isomers (2 alcohols + 1 ether); C4H10O has 7 (4 + 3); C5H12O has 14 (8 + 6). C7H8O has 5 aromatic isomers: o-, m- and p-cresol, benzyl alcohol and anisole — of which only the three cresols are phenols.

4Acidity — the one idea worth most marks

More questions hang on this than on anything else in the chapter. The whole of it is: whatever stabilises the anion after the H+ leaves makes the acid stronger.

Acidity is never about the O–H bond you start with. It is about the conjugate base you end with. Spread the negative charge over more atoms and the anion is lower in energy, so the equilibrium sits further to the right.

Phenoxide: the lone pair on oxygen overlaps the ring π system, so the charge is shared with three ring carbons. This is resonance delocalisation, and it is the entire reason phenol is acidic while ethanol is not.

The two-reagent test, and why it works. NaOH (conjugate acid: water, pKa 15·7) deprotonates anything more acidic than water — so phenol dissolves in NaOH, ethanol does not. NaHCO3 (conjugate acid: H2CO3, pKa 6·4) deprotonates only acids stronger than that — so a carboxylic acid fizzes with CO2, but ordinary phenol does not. That gap between pKa 10 and 6·4 is what lets you tell a phenol from a carboxylic acid in one test tube. Picric acid is the exception that proves the rule: at pKa 0·4 it does fizz with bicarbonate, and it is stronger than acetic acid.

Substituents: which way does the group push?

Electron-withdrawing groups help the ring carry the negative charge, so they raise acidity. Electron-donating groups add charge to an already negative ion, so they lower it.

GroupEffectOn acidity of the phenol
–NO2, –CN, –CHO, –COOH −I and −R, strongly withdrawingincreases — strongest at ortho and para, where resonance can reach
–X (F, Cl, Br, I)−I dominates over its weak +R increases, but only mildly
–CH3, –C2H5 +I, weakly donatingdecreases — cresols are weaker than phenol
–OH, –OCH3, –NH2 +R dominates, strongly donatingdecreases most
Two traps in one topic. (i) Resonance only reaches ortho and para. m-Nitrophenol (pKa 8·4) is a weaker acid than o- or p-nitrophenol (~7·2) because from the meta position NO2 can only work through the inductive effect — which is real but far weaker. Always check the position, not just the group. (ii) Alcohols run the other way. Alkyl groups are +I, so more of them means a less stable alkoxide: acidity falls CH3OH › 1° › 2° › 3°. That is the reverse of the reactivity order you will meet in §6, and mixing the two up is the single commonest slip in this chapter.

5The four handles on an alcohol

An alcohol has exactly four bonds that reagents attack. Learn the reactions grouped by which bond breaks and the list stops being a list.

Every reaction of an alcohol in the syllabus breaks one of these four bonds. Identify which one a reagent goes for and the product follows.

Handle 1 — the O–H bond breaks

The alcohol acts as an acid or a nucleophile through its oxygen; the carbon skeleton is untouched.

2R–OH + 2Na → 2R–ONa + H2 Sodium metaleffervescence of H2; the general test for an active hydrogen. Rate falls 1° › 2° › 3°, matching acidity
R–OH + CH3COCl → CH3COOR + HClAcylation an ester. Use pyridine to mop up the HCl
R–OH + R′COOH ⇌ ester + H2OEsterification conc. H2SO4; reversible, so drive it by removing water

Handle 2 — the C–O bond breaks

Now the OH leaves and something takes its place. This is substitution, and it is where carbocations and §6 come in.

R–OH + HX → R–X + H2O Hydrogen halidethe basis of the Lucas test. Reactivity of HX: HI › HBr › HCl
3R–OH + PCl3 → 3R–Cl + H3PO3Phosphorus halides PBr3 and PI3 are generated in situ from red P + Br2/I2
R–OH + SOCl2 → R–Cl + SO2↑ + HCl↑Thionyl chloride — the bestboth by-products are gases and leave the flask, so the product needs no purification. Darzens' procedure

Handle 3 — a β C–H breaks, taking the OH with it

Elimination, giving an alkene. Conc. H2SO4 at 443 K, or Al2O3 at 623 K. Ease of dehydration is 3° › 2° › 1°, because the intermediate is a carbocation.

Saytzeff's rule. When more than one alkene is possible, the major product is the more substituted one — it has more hyperconjugative structures and so is more stable. Butan-2-ol therefore gives but-2-ene, not but-1-ene.

Handle 4 — the carbinol C–H breaks

Oxidation. The number of hydrogens on the carbinol carbon decides how far it can go — and a tertiary alcohol has none, which is why it resists.

ClassH on carbinol CMild oxidant (PCC, Cu/573 K) Strong oxidant (KMnO4, K2Cr2O7)
1°2aldehyde — stops there carboxylic acid, same number of carbons
2°1ketone ketone — further oxidation needs the C–C bond to break
3°0no reaction only under drastic conditions, with chain cleavage — fewer carbons
PCC is the reagent that stops. Pyridinium chlorochromate in CH2Cl2 is anhydrous, so a 1° alcohol gives the aldehyde and goes no further. Aqueous KMnO4 hydrates the aldehyde and takes it straight through to the acid. If a question wants "1° alcohol → aldehyde", the answer is PCC (or Cu at 573 K, which dehydrogenates). Note that Cu/573 K on a 3° alcohol gives an alkene by dehydration, not a carbonyl — there is no H to remove from the carbinol carbon.

6Carbocations decide almost everything

Lucas test, SN1 versus SN2, dehydration, ether cleavage — four separate topics, one underlying idea.

The Lucas test reads that ladder directly

Lucas reagent is conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl2. The zinc chloride is a Lewis acid: it grips the oxygen and turns the terrible leaving group OH− into a good one, water. Then the C–O bond breaks to a carbocation, and Cl− grabs it. The chloride is insoluble in the reagent, so turbidity is the signal.

Add Lucas reagent at room temperature. When does it go cloudy?
Immediately 3° — and also allylic or benzylic, because their cations are resonance stabilised too
In about 5 minutes 2°
Not at room temperature 1° — needs heating
Why the test is limited only works for alcohols with up to about 6 carbons — larger ones are not soluble in the reagent to begin with, so there is nothing to observe

The two orders that point opposite ways

Reactivity towards HX, dehydration, SN1

  • 3° › 2° › 1° › CH3
  • Because the C–O bond breaks first and the carbocation's stability sets the rate
  • Rate depends only on the substrate: rate = k[R–OH], first order
  • Planar cation → attack from both faces → racemisation

Acidity, and reactivity in SN2

  • CH3 › 1° › 2° › 3°
  • Because the nucleophile has to reach the carbon, and alkyl groups are in the way (steric hindrance)
  • One step, both species in the rate law: rate = k[R–X][Nu]
  • Backside attack → inversion of configuration
Rearrangement gives it away. If a reaction produces a skeleton that is not the one you started with — a methyl or hydride has shifted — a carbocation existed, so the mechanism was SN1. For example 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol dehydrates to 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene: the 2° cation shifts a methyl to become 3°. SN2 has no intermediate and therefore never rearranges.

The 413 K / 443 K fork — one reagent, two products

Ethanol with conc. H2SO4 gives different products depending on the temperature and the ratio, and questions exploit this constantly:

C2H5OH + conc. H2SO4. What comes out?
413 K, excess alcohol the protonated alcohol is attacked by a second alcohol molecule — substitution, giving ethoxyethane C2H5OC2H5
443 K, excess acid a β-hydrogen leaves instead — elimination, giving ethene CH2=CH2

Higher temperature favours elimination in general, because elimination has the larger ∆S (two molecules from one) and so gains more from the T∆S term.

7Phenol has two faces

Reagents attack phenol either at the O–H or on the ring. Sorting a reaction into one of those two boxes is most of the work.

Face 1 — the O–H (acidic)

  • NaOH → sodium phenoxide, dissolves. Ethanol does not react.
  • Na → phenoxide + H2
  • Zn dust, distil → benzene + ZnO — the C–O bond itself is removed
  • (CH3CO)2O → phenyl acetate
  • NaHCO3 → no reaction, no CO2 — this is the test that separates phenol from a carboxylic acid

Face 2 — the ring (activated)

  • Br2/water → white ppt of 2,4,6-tribromophenol
  • Br2/CS2, 273 K → mostly p-bromophenol only
  • dil. HNO3 → o- and p-nitrophenol; conc. HNO3 → picric acid
  • CO2, NaOH, 400 K, pressure → salicylic acid (Kolbe)
  • CHCl3 + NaOH → salicylaldehyde (Reimer–Tiemann)
  • benzene diazonium chloride, 273 K → orange p-hydroxyazobenzene

Why the ring reactions all land ortho and para, and why phenol is far more reactive than benzene: the oxygen lone pair feeds electron density specifically into those three positions.

Why bromine water gives the tri-substituted product but Br2/CS2 does not. Water is polar, so it ionises phenol slightly to phenoxide — which carries a full negative charge and activates the ring far more strongly than neutral phenol. All three available positions are then brominated at once, and no Lewis-acid catalyst is needed. CS2 is non-polar, there is no phenoxide, the ring is less activated, and at 273 K substitution stops after one. Solvent polarity is the variable being tested here, not the bromine.
Both Kolbe and Reimer–Tiemann start by making phenoxide. Neutral phenol is not nucleophilic enough to attack CO2 or dichlorocarbene. That is why NaOH appears in both. Reimer–Tiemann's actual electrophile is :CCl2, dichlorocarbene, made from CHCl3 + OH−; the intermediate is an o-benzal chloride, which hydrolyses to the aldehyde. Both reactions favour the ortho product, because the sodium ion chelates the phenoxide oxygen and holds the incoming electrophile next to it.
The FeCl3 colour. Phenol gives a violet colour with neutral FeCl3 — a coloured iron–phenoxide complex, [Fe(OC6H5)6]3−. Alcohols, ethers and benzyl alcohol give nothing. It is the single fastest way to identify a phenol.

The four routes to phenol

C6H5Cl →[NaOH, 623 K, 300 atm] → C6H5ONa →[H+] phenol Dow processthe forcing conditions are the point: the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene has partial double-bond character
C6H5N2Cl + H2O →[warm] phenol + N2 + HCl From a diazonium saltthe mildest lab route; comes from aniline + NaNO2/HCl at 273–278 K

8Ethers — making them and breaking them

Ethers are unreactive, which is why they are used as solvents. Only two things happen to them, and both are heavily examined.

Making them: Williamson synthesis

An alkoxide attacks an alkyl halide. It is a clean SN2, and that single fact settles every "which pair should you use?" question:

✓ Right pairing

  • (CH3)3C–ONa + CH3I
  • The bulky group is on the alkoxide; the halide is small, so SN2 is unhindered
  • Gives tert-butyl methyl ether in good yield

✗ Wrong pairing

  • CH3ONa + (CH3)3C–Br
  • The bulky group is on the halide, so SN2 cannot reach the carbon
  • The alkoxide acts as a base instead and eliminates → 2-methylpropene, not an ether
The rule in one line. In Williamson synthesis, put the bulk on the oxygen and keep the halide primary (methyl best). Aryl ethers such as anisole are made from sodium phenoxide + CH3I — never from phenol + chlorobenzene, because an aryl halide will not do SN2 at all.

Breaking them: cleavage by HI

Ethers are inert to bases, oxidants and reductants, but concentrated HI or HBr cleaves them on heating. The oxygen is protonated first — that is the only way to make the alkoxide a viable leaving group — and then I− attacks. Which side it attacks is the whole question.

Three cases, one decision: does the iodide attack directly (SN2, so it takes the less hindered side) or does a stable carbocation form first (SN1, so the more substituted side becomes the halide)?

EtherMechanismProducts with 1 mol HIReason
CH3–O–C2H5 SN2CH3I + C2H5OH attack at the less hindered carbon — the methyl
(CH3)3C–O–CH3 SN1(CH3)3CI + CH3OH a stable 3° carbocation forms and is captured
C6H5–O–CH3 (anisole) SN2 at the alkyl side C6H5OH + CH3I the C(aryl)–O bond has partial double-bond character and never breaks
Iodobenzene is never a product. Whatever the aryl alkyl ether, the ring keeps the oxygen and you get a phenol. With excess HI the alcohol formed in the first two rows goes on to its own iodide, so ethoxyethane + excess HI gives 2 C2H5I — but anisole still stops at phenol, because phenol does not react further with HI.
Why an ether dissolves in cold conc. H2SO4. The oxygen lone pair accepts a proton to form an oxonium salt — ethers are Lewis bases. This is used to separate an ether from an alkane, which is untouched. It is also why ethers form peroxides on standing in air, a genuine laboratory hazard: always test an old bottle of ether before distilling it.

9Telling them apart in one test tube

"How would you distinguish X from Y?" is worth a mark every year. Choose the test that gives an observation, not just a reaction.

Step 1 — add neutral FeCl3
Violet colour → a PHENOL the iron–phenoxide complex. Nothing else in this chapter does it
No colour → go to step 2 alcohol, ether, or benzyl alcohol
Step 2 — add sodium metal
H2 effervescence → an ALCOHOL it has an active hydrogen
Nothing → an ETHER no O–H to lose
Step 3 — which alcohol? Add Lucas reagent
Turbid at once / in 5 min / on heating 3° / 2° / 1° — see §6
Or oxidise with PCC and test the product aldehyde (Tollens' positive) → 1°; ketone (Tollens' negative) → 2°; no reaction → 3°

Two more tests that carry their own structural condition

Iodoform test — I2 + NaOH

  • Yellow crystals of CHI3, antiseptic smell
  • Needs the fragment CH3–CH(OH)– (or CH3CO–)
  • Positive: ethanol, propan-2-ol, butan-2-ol
  • Negative: methanol (no CH3 next to the carbinol carbon), propan-1-ol, all 3° alcohols except those fitting the pattern
  • So it distinguishes ethanol from methanol, and propan-2-ol from propan-1-ol

Bromine water — for phenol

  • White precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol; the bromine colour is discharged
  • Distinguishes phenol from benzyl alcohol and from anisole under these conditions
  • Aniline also gives a white precipitate — use FeCl3 if you need to separate those two

10Making an alcohol — and choosing the route

Preparation questions usually ask which route gives which regiochemistry, or which Grignard builds a named alcohol.

From an alkene — two routes, opposite orientations

Acid-catalysed hydration — Markovnikov

  • CH3CH=CH2 + H2O/H+ → propan-2-ol
  • Goes through a carbocation, which forms at the more substituted carbon
  • So the OH ends up on the more substituted carbon
  • Rearrangement is possible — a warning sign of a cation

Hydroboration–oxidation — anti-Markovnikov

  • (i) B2H6  (ii) H2O2/OH− → propan-1-ol
  • Boron adds to the less hindered carbon; the H goes to the other
  • One concerted step, no carbocation, so no rearrangement
  • Syn addition — H and OH add to the same face

From a carbonyl compound — the Grignard method

The Grignard carbon is nucleophilic: it attacks the carbonyl carbon, and hydrolysis of the alkoxide intermediate then gives the alcohol. Which class of alcohol you get is fixed by the carbonyl you start with, and this is the question that gets asked:

Carbonyl+ RMgX, then H3O+Note
Methanal HCHO1° alcoholadds one carbon to R
Any other aldehyde RCHO2° alcohol
Ketone R2C=O3° alcohol
Ester / acid chloride (2 mol RMgX)3° alcohol with two identical R groups
Epoxide (ethylene oxide)1° alcohol adds two carbons — RCH2CH2OH
Grignards must be bone dry. R–MgX is destroyed by any compound with an active hydrogen — water, alcohols, phenols, acids, amines — giving the alkane R–H and Mg(OH)X. That is why you cannot use a Grignard on a substrate that also carries an OH group, and why the reaction is run in dry ether. It also means RMgX + D2O is a clean way to make R–D.

Reduction routes

RCHO / RCOR′ →[NaBH4 or LiAlH4] alcoholCarbonyl reduction NaBH4 is mild and leaves esters and acids alone; LiAlH4 reduces everything
RCOOH →[LiAlH4] RCH2OH Acid to 1° alcoholNaBH4 will not do this — a standard distractor
RCOOR′ →[H2/catalyst or LiAlH4] RCH2OH + R′OH Ester reductiongives two alcohols
Anhydrous ZnCl2, PCC, SOCl2, NaBH4, LiAlH4, B2H6. Reagent-recognition questions are free marks. Learn what each one does and, just as important, what it refuses to do — NaBH4 not touching a carboxylic acid, PCC not going past the aldehyde, HI not making iodobenzene.
