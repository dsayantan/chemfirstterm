Chemical Kinetics

Worksheet of five questions, with its answer key.

1Order from initial rates
For A + B → products the following initial rates were measured. Find (i) the order with respect to A, (ii) the order with respect to B, (iii) the rate law.
Exp.[A][B]d[Products]/dt
110·154·20 × 10−6
220·158·40 × 10−6
310·205·60 × 10−6
  1. Experiments 1 and 2 hold [B] fixed and double [A]; the rate exactly doubles, so 2a = 2 and a = 1.
  2. Experiments 1 and 3 hold [A] fixed and raise [B] by 0·20/0·15 = 1·33; the rate rises by 5·60/4·20 = 1·33, the same factor, so b = 1.
Answer  · Order in A = 1, order in B = 1; rate = k[A][B], overall order 2.
2Arrhenius — activation energy
The rate of a reaction becomes 4 times when the temperature changes from 293 K to 313 K. Calculate the activation energy, assuming it does not change with temperature. (R = 8·314 J K−1 mol−1)
log k2k1 = Ea2·303 R × T2 − T1T1T2
  1. log 4 = 0·6021
  2. Ea = 2·303 × 8·314 × 0·6021 × 293 × 31320
  3. Ea = 11·53 × 4585·5 = 52 863 J mol−1
Answer  · Ea ≈ 52 863 J mol−1 = 52·86 kJ mol−1
3Mechanism and rate law
Suggest a mechanism for the decomposition of ozone, O3 into O2, and give the rate law equation.
O3  ⇌fast, k1  O2 + [O]      O3 + [O]  →slow, k2  2O2
  1. The slow step gives rate = k2[O3][O].
  2. From the fast pre-equilibrium, [O] = k1[O3]/[O2].
  3. Substituting gives rate = k[O3]2/[O2].
Answer  · rate = k[O3]2[O2]−1 — second order in ozone, and inhibited by the oxygen produced.
4First-order timing
A first order reaction has a rate constant of 1·15 × 10−3 s−1. How long will 5 g of the reactant take to reduce to 3 g?
t = 2·303k log aa − x
  1. t = 2·3031·15 × 10−3 log 53
  2. = 2002·6 × 0·2218 = 444 s
Answer  · t ≈ 444 s (about 7½ minutes).
5Activation energy from a graph
The slope of the line in a graph of log k against 1/T for a reaction is −5841 K. Calculate the activation energy.
slope = −Ea2·303 R  ⇒   Ea = −2·303 × R × slope
  1. Ea = −2·303 × 8·314 × (−5841) = 111 838 J mol−1
Answer  · Ea = 111·83 kJ mol−1

Electrochemistry

Worksheet of eight questions, with its answer key.

1Electrode potential from a thermodynamic cycle
What is the electrode potential for Cu+ + e− → Cu, given E°(Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu) = 0·337 V and E°(Cu2+ + e− → Cu+) = 0·153 V?
Potentials are not additive — free energies are. Convert each step to ΔG° = −nFE°, add, then convert back.
  1. Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu: ΔG°1 = −2F(0·337)
  2. Cu+ → Cu2+ + e−: ΔG°2 = −F(−0·153)  (the reverse of the given half-reaction)
  3. Adding gives Cu+ + e− → Cu, so −1·F·E° = −2F(0·337) + 0·153F
  4. −E° = −0·674 + 0·153 = −0·521, so E° = 0·521 V
Answer  · E° = 0·521 V
2Kohlrausch's law
Calculate Λ°m for NH4OH, given Λ°m for Ba(OH)2, BaCl2 and NH4Cl as 523·28, 280·0 and 129·8 S cm2 mol−1 respectively.
Λ°(NH4OH) = ½Λ°(Ba(OH)2) + Λ°(NH4Cl) − ½Λ°(BaCl2)
  1. = ½(523·28) + 129·8 − ½(280·0)
  2. = 261·64 + 129·8 − 140·0 = 251·44
Answer  · Λ°m(NH4OH) = 251·44 S cm2 mol−1
3Conductivity, dissociation and Ka
The resistance of 0·01 M CH3COOH is 2220 Ω in a cell of cell constant 0·366 cm−1. Given λ°(H+) = 349·1 and λ°(CH3COO−) = 40·9 S cm2 mol−1, calculate (a) conductivity, (b) molar conductivity, (c) degree of dissociation, (d) the dissociation constant.
  1. Λ°m = 349·1 + 40·9 = 390 S cm2 mol−1
  2. κ = 1R × cell constant = 0·3662220 = 1·65 × 10−4
  3. Λm = κ × 1000M = 1·65 × 10−4 × 10000·01 = 16·5
  4. α = Λm/Λ°m = 16·5/390 = 0·0423
  5. Ka = Cα21 − α ≈ Cα2 = 0·01(0·0423)2 = 1·78 × 10−5
Answer  · (a) κ = 1·65 × 10−4 S cm−1  (b) Λm = 16·5 S cm2 mol−1  (c) α = 0·0423 (4·23%)  (d) Ka = 1·78 × 10−5
4Conductance on dilution
Explain what happens to specific conductance on dilution.
Answer  · It decreases. Specific conductance is the conductance of one unit volume of solution, and on dilution the number of current-carrying ions in that fixed volume falls.
5Equilibrium constant from E°
Calculate the equilibrium constant for Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) → Cu2+(aq) + 2Ag(s), E° = 0·46 V at 298 K.
log K = nE°0·0591 = 2 × 0·460·0591 = 15·57
  1. K = 1015·57 = 3·7 × 1015 ≈ 4 × 1015
Answer  · K ≈ 4 × 1015
6Equilibrium constant from the cell emf
The standard emf of a galvanic cell involving a reaction with n = 2 is 0·295 V at 25 °C. Calculate the equilibrium constant.
  1. At equilibrium Ecell = 0, so 0 = E° − 0·059n log K
  2. 0·295 = 0·0592 log K  ⇒  log K = 10
  3. K = 1010
Answer  · K = 1010
7Faraday's second law
4·5 g of Al (atomic mass 27) is deposited at the cathode from Al3+ solution by a certain quantity of charge. Calculate the volume of hydrogen produced at STP from H+ ions by the same quantity of charge.
wAlwH = EAlEH
  1. Equivalent masses: Al = 27/3 = 9, H = 1/1 = 1
  2. 4·5wH = 91  ⇒  wH = 0·5 g
  3. 2 g of H2 occupies 22·4 L at STP, so 0·5 g occupies 22·42 × 0·5 = 5·6 L
Answer  · 5·6 litres of H2 at STP
8Hydrogen electrode
A hydrogen gas electrode is made by dipping a Pt wire in a solution of pH = 10 and passing H2 gas around the wire at 1 atm. Calculate the oxidation potential.
  1. ½H2 − e− → H+; pH = 10 so [H+] = 10−10 M
  2. E = 0 − 0·059 log [H+][H2]½ = −0·059 log(10−10)
  3. = 0·059 × 10 = +0·591 V
Answer  · Oxidation potential = +0·591 V (so the reduction potential is −0·591 V).

Solutions

Worksheet of ten questions, with its answer key.

1Degree of dissociation from ΔTb
An aqueous solution containing 12·48 g of BaCl2 in 1 kg of water boils at 373·0832 K. Calculate the degree of dissociation of BaCl2. [Kb = 0·52 K kg mol−1; molar mass 208·34 g mol−1]
  1. ΔTb = 373·0832 − 373 = 0·0832 K
  2. Mobs = Kb × w2 × 1000w1 × ΔTb = 0·52 × 12·48 × 10001000 × 0·0832 = 78 g mol−1
  3. i = Mtheo/Mobs = 208·34/78 = 2·67
  4. BaCl2 → Ba2+ + 2Cl−, so n = 3 and α = i − 1n − 1 = 1·672 = 0·835
Answer  · α = 0·835, i.e. 83·5% dissociated
2Degree of association from ΔTf
Phenol associates in benzene to form a dimer. A solution containing 20 g of phenol in 1 kg of benzene has its freezing point lowered by 0·69 K. Calculate the fraction of phenol that has dimerised. [Kf = 5·1 K kg mol−1]
  1. Mobs = Kf × 1000 × w2ΔTf × w1 = 5·1 × 1000 × 200·69 × 1000 = 147·82 g mol−1
  2. Mtheo(C6H5OH) = 94, so i = 94/147·82 = 0·635
  3. For 2A → A2, α = i − 1½ − 1 = −0·365−0·5 = 0·73
Answer  · α = 0·73, i.e. 73% dimerised
3Which has the higher boiling point?
Out of 1 M glucose and 2 M glucose, which has the higher boiling point, and why?
Answer  · 2 M glucose. Elevation of boiling point is a colligative property: ΔTb = Kbm, so the more concentrated solution has the greater elevation and therefore the higher boiling point.
4Why osmotic pressure for polymers
The molecular masses of polymers are determined by the osmotic pressure method and not by measuring other colligative properties. Give reasons.
Answer  · Two reasons. (i) Osmotic pressure is measured at or near room temperature, and uses molarity rather than molality, so the polymer is not heated and possibly degraded. (ii) Its magnitude is large compared with the other colligative properties even for very dilute solutions — and a polymer solution is necessarily dilute in moles, because the molar mass is enormous.
5Mass of solute from the lowering of vapour pressure
What is the weight of a solute (molar mass 60) required to dissolve in 180 g of water to reduce the vapour pressure to 4/5 of that of pure water?
  1. Relative lowering = p° − pp° = 1 − 4/5 = 1/5
  2. n1 = 180/18 = 10 mol of water; let n2 = w2/60
  3. Exact: 15 = n2n1 + n2  ⇒  10 + n2 = 5n2  ⇒  n2 = 2·5  ⇒  w2 = 150 g
  4. Approximate (dilute, n2 ≪ n1): 15 = n2n1  ⇒  n2 = 2  ⇒  w2 = 120 g
Answer  · 150 g, from the exact expression. (The school key gives 120 g, which is what the dilute approximation yields — see the errata.)
6Henry's law
KH for the solubility of N2 in H2O at 298 K is 105 atm. The mole fraction of N2 in air is 0·8. Calculate the number of moles of N2 from air dissolved in 10 moles of H2O at 298 K and 5 atm pressure.
  1. Partial pressure of N2 = 0·8 × 5 = 4 atm
  2. p = KHx2  ⇒  x2 = 4105 = 4 × 10−5
  3. nN2nN2 + 10 = 4 × 10−5, and since nN2 is tiny beside 10, nN2 ≈ 10 × 4 × 10−5 = 4 × 10−4 mol
Answer  · n(N2) = 4 × 10−4 mol
7Ice separating on cooling
What is the amount of ice that will separate out on cooling a solution containing 50 g of ethylene glycol in 200 g of water to −9·3 °C? [Kf = 1·86 K kg mol−1]
  1. At −9·3 °C the solution that remains must have ΔTf = 9·3 K, which fixes how much water can still be liquid.
  2. 9·3 = 1·86 × 1000 × 5062 × w1  ⇒  w1 = 161·3 g of water remaining liquid
  3. Ice separated = 200 − 161·3 = 38·7 g
Answer  · About 38·8 g of ice
8Freezing point of a mixed solution
An aqueous solution has 5% urea and 10% glucose by weight. What will be the freezing point of this solution? [Kf = 1·86 K kg mol−1]
  1. Take 100 g of solution: 5 g urea, 10 g glucose, and 100 − 15 = 85 g of water.
  2. Depressions are additive, since each solute contributes its own particles.
  3. ΔTf(urea) = 1000 × 1·86 × 560 × 85 = 1·824 K
  4. ΔTf(glucose) = 1000 × 1·86 × 10180 × 85 = 1·216 K
  5. Total = 3·04 K, so Tf = 0 − 3·04 = −3·04 °C
Answer  · Tf = −3·04 °C
9Two explanations
Explain: (i) when mercuric iodide is added to an aqueous solution of KI, the freezing point is raised. (ii) A person suffering from high blood pressure is advised to take a minimum quantity of common salt.
Answer  · (i) HgI2 and KI combine to form the complex K2[HgI4]. Four particles (2K+ + 2I−) become three (2K+ + [HgI4]2−), so the number of particles falls. Depression of freezing point is colligative, so it decreases — the freezing point rises.
(ii) Osmotic pressure rises with the concentration of solute. Salt raises the Na+ and Cl− concentration of the body fluid, so water is retained to balance it and the volume — and hence the pressure — of the blood rises.
10Endothermic yet spontaneous
The dissolution of NH4Cl in water is an endothermic process but still it dissolves in water readily. Why?
Answer  · Because the process is driven by entropy, not enthalpy. The ions held in the crystal lattice become free to move in all directions, so ΔS is large and positive. Since ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, when TΔS > ΔH the free energy change is negative and dissolution is spontaneous despite absorbing heat.

Compound A, C4H9Br, is treated with aqueous KOH and the rate depends on the concentration of A only. Another optically active isomer B of the same formula gives a rate depending on both A and KOH. (i) Write the structural formulae of A and B. (ii) Which is converted to a product with inverted configuration?
Answer  · A = (CH3)3C–Br (2-bromo-2-methylpropane) — rate depends on the halide alone, so SN1.

Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Worksheet of six questions, with its answer key.

1A three-step chain
CH3CH2Cl →NaCN X →Ni/H2 Y →(CH3CO)2O Z. Identify X, Y, Z.
Answer  · X = CH3CH2CN (propanenitrile)  ·  Y = CH3CH2CH2NH2 (propan-1-amine)  ·  Z = CH3CH2CH2NHCOCH3 (N-propylacetamide)
2Grignard with carbon dioxide
C6H5MgBr →(i) CO2 →(ii) H3O+ ?
C6H5MgBr + CO2 → C6H5COO–MgBr  →H3O+  C6H5COOH
Answer  · Benzoic acid, C6H5COOH (with Mg(OH)Br).
3Three conversions
(i) Propan-1-ol to propan-2-ol. (ii) Ethanol to propan-1-ol. (iii) 2-methylpropan-1-ol to 2-methylpropan-2-ol.
Answer  · (i) PCl5, then alcoholic KOH (gives propene), then HBr (Markovnikov → 2-bromopropane), then aqueous KOH.
(ii) PCl5, then Mg/dry ether, then HCHO and H3O+.
(iii) conc. H2SO4 at 443 K (gives 2-methylpropene), then HBr (Markovnikov → the tertiary bromide), then aqueous KOH.
4Ether cleavage
Give the products: (i) CH3CH2CH2–O–CH3 + HBr  (ii) C6H5–O–C2H5 + HBr  (iii) (CH3)3C–O–C2H5 + HI
Answer  · (i) CH3Br + propan-1-ol — both groups are primary, so SN2 attacks the less hindered carbon, the methyl.
(ii) C2H5Br + phenol — the aryl C–O bond has partial double-bond character and never breaks.
(iii) (CH3)3C–I + ethanol — the tertiary side ionises by SN1 to a stable carbocation.
5Reagents, and two aromatic chains
(i) Name the reagent for: (a) oxidation of a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid, (b) benzyl alcohol to benzoic acid, (c) butan-2-one to butan-2-ol, (d) oxidation of a primary alcohol to an aldehyde. (ii) (a) phenol →Zn dust A →H2SO4/SO3 B; (b) benzene →Cl2/AlCl3 A →aq. NaOH, 623 K B.
Answer  · (i) (a) acidified K2Cr2O7  (b) alkaline KMnO4 followed by acidification  (c) NaBH4 (or Ni/H2)  (d) PCC in CH2Cl2
(ii)(a) A = benzene, B = benzenesulphonic acid. (b) A = chlorobenzene, B = phenol.
6Structure determination
A compound A is optically active. On mild oxidation it gives B; on vigorous oxidation it gives C. Compound D, which is also formed from B by reaction with iodine and alkali, accompanies C. Deduce the structures of A, B, C and D.
  1. A is optically active, so it has a chiral carbon; mild oxidation to a compound that then gives iodoform means A is a secondary alcohol with a CH3–CH(OH)– group.
  2. The smallest such alcohol that is chiral is butan-2-ol.
  3. Mild oxidation gives the methyl ketone B; I2/NaOH then cleaves it to CHI3 (D) and propanoate, i.e. propanoic acid (C).
  4. Vigorous oxidation of A reaches the same acid C, which is the consistency check the question is built on.
Answer  · A = butan-2-ol, CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3  ·  B = butan-2-one, CH3CH2COCH3  ·  C = propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH  ·  D = iodoform, CHI3

Coordination Compounds

Worksheet of six questions, with its answer key.

1Name the isomerism
Name the type of isomerism exhibited by each pair: (i) [Cu(NH3)4][PtCl4] and [Pt(NH3)4][CuCl4]; (ii) [Co(pn)2Cl2]+ and [Co(tn)2Cl2]+.
Answer  · (i) Coordination isomerism — both ions are complexes and the ligands have been exchanged between the two metal centres.
(ii) Ligand isomerism — pn (propane-1,2-diamine) and tn (propane-1,3-diamine) are different ligands that are isomers of each other, both C3H10N2.
2Hybridisation, shape and magnetism
Describe the state of hybridisation, the shape and the magnetic behaviour of (i) [Cr(H2O)2(C2O4)2]− and (ii) [Co(NH3)2(en)2]3+.
  1. (i) x + 2(0) + 2(−2) = −1 ⇒ Cr(III), d3. Three electrons singly occupy t2g whatever the ligand, so two inner 3d orbitals are always free.
  2. (ii) x + 2(0) + 2(0) = +3 ⇒ Co(III), d6. NH3 and en are strong field, so all six pair into t2g.
Answer  · (i) Cr is +3, d3: d2sp3, octahedral, paramagnetic (3 unpaired).
(ii) Co is +3, d6 with strong-field ligands: d2sp3, octahedral, diamagnetic (0 unpaired).
3Structural isomers and their type
Draw the structural isomers of [Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2 and name the type of isomerism.
Answer  · Two isomers, differing in how the NO2 is attached: [Co(NH3)5(NO2-κN)]Cl2 (nitrito-N, the nitro form, bound through nitrogen) and [Co(NH3)5(ONO-κO)]Cl2 (nitrito-O, bound through oxygen). This is linkage isomerism.
4Geometrical isomers
Draw the geometrical isomers of [Co(NH3)3Cl3] and [CoCl2(en)2]+.
Answer  · [Co(NH3)3Cl3] is MA3B3: the three chlorides either occupy one triangular face (fac) or lie round a meridian (mer).
[CoCl2(en)2]+ is M(AA)2B2: the two chlorides are adjacent (cis, 90°) or opposite (trans, 180°).
5A complex ion in four parts
For [Co(NH3)6]3+: (i) give the IUPAC name, (ii) the oxidation number of cobalt, (iii) the hybridisation, (iv) the magnetic behaviour.
Answer  · (i) hexaamminecobalt(III) ion  (ii) +3  (iii) d2sp3  (iv) diamagnetic
6Write the formulae
Write the formula of (i) potassium trioxalatoaluminate(III), (ii) hexaaquairon(II) sulphate, (iii) tetraamminechromium(III)-μ-amido-μ-hydroxo-tetraaquacobalt(III) nitrate.
Answer  · (i) K3[Al(C2O4)3]  (ii) [Fe(H2O)6]SO4  (iii) [(NH3)4Cr(μ-NH2)(μ-OH)Co(H2O)4](NO3)4
