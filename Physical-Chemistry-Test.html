Q1Solutions1 mark
1·8 g of a non-electrolyte dissolved in 90 g of water lowered the freezing point by 0·465 K. The molar mass of the solute is (Kf for water = 1·86 K kg mol−1):
ExplanationM = Kf·wB·1000 / (ΔTf·wA) = (1·86 × 1·8 × 1000) / (0·465 × 90) = 3348 / 41·85 = 80 g mol−1. Keep the solvent mass in grams and the factor of 1000 converts it to kilograms; dropping the 1000 gives 0·08 and dropping the solvent mass gives 7200.
Q2Solutions1 mark
A 0·1 M solution of K3[Fe(CN)6] is 60% dissociated. Its van't Hoff factor is:
ExplanationK3[Fe(CN)6] → 3K+ + [Fe(CN)6]3−, so n = 4 particles — the complex ion counts as one, because ligands inside the coordination sphere do not ionise. Then i = 1 + (n − 1)α = 1 + 3(0·60) = 2·8. Taking n = 10 by splitting the complex gives 6·4.
Q3Solutions1 mark
The osmotic pressure of a 0·02 M aqueous solution of K2SO4 at 27 °C, assuming complete dissociation, is (R = 0·0821 L atm K−1 mol−1):
ExplanationK2SO4 → 2K+ + SO42−, so i = 3 and T = 300 K. π = iCRT = 3 × 0·02 × 0·0821 × 300 = 1·48 atm. Omitting i gives 0·49 atm — exactly one third, and the commonest error in this question.
Q4Solutions1 mark
30 g of urea (M = 60 g mol−1) is dissolved in 846 g of water at 298 K. If the vapour pressure of pure water at 298 K is 23·8 mm Hg, the vapour pressure of the solution is:
ExplanationUrea is non-volatile, so p = xwater × p°. n(urea) = 30/60 = 0·5 mol; n(water) = 846/18 = 47·0 mol. xwater = 47·0 / 47·5 = 0·9895. p = 0·9895 × 23·8 = 23·55 mm Hg. Using the mole fraction of the solute gives 0·25 mm Hg, which is the lowering, not the vapour pressure.
Q5Solutions1 mark
Four aqueous solutions, each 0·1 molal, are prepared from glucose, NaCl, BaCl2 and AlCl3. The solution with the highest boiling point is that of:
ExplanationElevation of boiling point is colligative: ΔTb = i Kb m. At equal molality the largest i wins. glucose 1 < NaCl 2 < BaCl2 3 < AlCl3 4. Note the direction: the greatest elevation gives the highest boiling point, whereas the greatest depression gives the lowest freezing point — the same ordering read two opposite ways.
Q6Solutions1 mark
Which pair of liquids shows a negative deviation from Raoult's law and forms a maximum boiling azeotrope?
ExplanationChloroform and acetone. A hydrogen bond forms between the chloroform hydrogen and the acetone carbonyl oxygen — a new interaction stronger than those it replaces, so escaping tendency falls, the observed pressure is below the predicted one, ΔmixH is negative, and the mixture boils at a maximum. Ethanol + water deviates positively (minimum boiling, 95% azeotrope); the last two pairs are essentially ideal.
Q7Solutions1 mark
The Henry's law constant for CO2 in water at 298 K is 1·67 × 108 Pa. The mole fraction of CO2 in a soda water bottle in which the partial pressure of CO2 is 2·5 atm is (1 atm = 1·013 × 105 Pa):
Explanationp = KHx, so x = p/KH. Convert first: p = 2·5 × 1·013 × 105 = 2·53 × 105 Pa. x = 2·53 × 105 / 1·67 × 108 = 1·52 × 10−3. The units of p and KH must match — using 2·5 atm directly against a constant in pascals is the trap.
Q8Electrochemistry1 mark
For the cell Zn(s) | Zn2+(0·1 M) ‖ Cu2+(0·01 M) | Cu(s), for which E°cell = 1·10 V, the emf at 298 K is:
ExplanationCell reaction Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu, so n = 2 and Q = [Zn2+]/[Cu2+] = 0·1/0·01 = 10. E = 1·10 − (0·0591/2) log 10 = 1·10 − 0·0296 = 1·07 V. The anode ion goes on top of Q; inverting it gives 1·13 V.
Q9Electrochemistry1 mark
A galvanic cell has E°cell = 0·46 V and its cell reaction involves 2 electrons. ΔG° for the reaction is (1 F = 96500 C):
ExplanationΔG° = −nFE°cell = −2 × 96500 × 0·46 = −88780 J = −88·78 kJ mol−1. Negative, as it must be for a cell with a positive emf — a positive answer here means the sign of the formula was dropped.
Q10Electrochemistry1 mark
Given λ°(Al3+) = 189 and λ°(SO42−) = 160 S cm2 mol−1, the molar conductivity at infinite dilution of Al2(SO4)3 is:
ExplanationBy Kohlrausch's law each ion is counted as many times as the formula unit releases it: Λ° = 2λ°(Al3+) + 3λ°(SO42−) = 2(189) + 3(160) = 378 + 480 = 858. Simply adding 189 + 160 = 349 ignores the stoichiometry, which is the whole point of the law.
Q11Electrochemistry1 mark
The molar conductivity of a 0·001 M acetic acid solution is 39·05 S cm2 mol−1 and its molar conductivity at infinite dilution is 390·5 S cm2 mol−1. Its degree of dissociation is:
Explanationα = Λm / Λ°m = 39·05 / 390·5 = 0·10, i.e. 10% dissociated. Λ°m for a weak electrolyte cannot be found by extrapolating its own graph — the curve rises too steeply near the axis — which is precisely why Kohlrausch's law is needed to supply it.
Q12Electrochemistry1 mark
A current of 5 A is passed through molten AlCl3 for 30 minutes. The mass of aluminium deposited is (Al = 27, 1 F = 96500 C):
ExplanationQ = It = 5 × (30 × 60) = 9000 C. For Al3+ + 3e− → Al the equivalent weight is 27/3 = 9. w = (E/F)Q = (9 × 9000)/96500 = 0·84 g. Leaving the time as 30 instead of 1800 seconds, or forgetting to divide by 3, are the two ways this goes wrong.
Q13Electrochemistry1 mark
Silver and copper voltameters are connected in series and a current is passed until 1·08 g of silver is deposited. The mass of copper deposited is (Ag = 108, Cu = 63·5):
ExplanationIn series the same charge passes through both — Faraday's second law. Moles of electrons = 1·08/108 = 0·01 (n = 1 for Ag+). For Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu, w = (63·5/2) × 0·01 = 0·318 g. Do not compute a fresh charge for the second cell.
Q14Electrochemistry1 mark
Given E°: Cu2+/Cu = +0·34 V, Ag+/Ag = +0·80 V, Zn2+/Zn = −0·76 V, Au3+/Au = +1·50 V. Which metal will liberate H2 gas from dilute HCl?
ExplanationHydrogen is the zero of the scale, so only a metal with a negative standard reduction potential lies above it and can reduce H+ to H2. Only Zn at −0·76 V qualifies: E°cell = 0·00 − (−0·76) = +0·76 V. Cu, Ag and Au all sit below hydrogen, which is why they do not dissolve in dilute HCl or dilute H2SO4.
Q15Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The unit of the rate constant of a reaction is mol L−1 s−1. The order of the reaction is:
ExplanationThe unit of k is (mol L−1)1−n s−1. Setting 1 − n = 1 gives n = 0. Put another way, only for a zero order reaction is k numerically the rate itself, so the two share a unit. First order would be s−1 and second order L mol−1 s−1.
Q16Chemical Kinetics1 mark
For the reaction 2A + B → products, doubling [A] doubles the rate, and doubling [B] makes the rate four times. The overall order of the reaction is:
ExplanationRate ∝ [A]1 because doubling gives 21, and rate ∝ [B]2 because doubling gives 4 = 22. So rate = k[A][B]2 and the overall order is 1 + 2 = 3. Note that the coefficient 2 in front of A does not enter — order is experimental, not read off the balanced equation.
Q17Chemical Kinetics1 mark
A first order reaction is 30% complete in 20 minutes. The time required for it to be 60% complete is approximately:
Explanationk = (2·303/t) log[a/(a − x)] = (2·303/20) log(100/70) = (2·303/20)(0·1549) = 0·01784 min−1. Then t = (2·303/k) log(100/40) = (2·303/0·01784)(0·3979) = 51·4 min. Doubling the time because the percentage doubled gives 40 min, and is wrong — first order kinetics is logarithmic, not proportional.
Q18Chemical Kinetics1 mark
For a first order reaction with k = 2·303 × 10−3 s−1, the time taken for the reactant concentration to fall to one tenth of its initial value is:
Explanationt = (2·303/k) log([R]0/[R]) = (2·303 / 2·303 × 10−3) log 10 = 1000 × 1 = 1000 s. The 2·303 cancels exactly, which is the hint that this is the intended route; note the answer is independent of the starting concentration, as it must be for first order.
Q19Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The rate constant of a reaction doubles when the temperature is raised from 300 K to 310 K. The activation energy of the reaction is approximately (R = 8·314 J K−1 mol−1):
Explanationlog(k2/k1) = Ea(T2 − T1) / (2·303 R T1T2). With k2/k1 = 2, log 2 = 0·3010: Ea = (2·303 × 8·314 × 300 × 310 × 0·3010)/10 = 53600 J = 53·6 kJ mol−1. This is the standard ‘rate doubles per 10 °C’ result, and it is worth remembering that it comes out near 50 kJ mol−1 around room temperature.
Q20Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The experimentally determined rate law for a reaction is rate = k[A]1/2[B]3/2. Which statement is correct?
ExplanationOrder is the sum of the exponents in the experimental rate law: ½ + 3/2 = 2. Molecularity is the number of species colliding in an elementary step; it is a whole number and is defined only for a single step. Fractional orders are the signature of a complex, multi-step reaction, so molecularity has no meaning for the overall reaction here.
Q21Solutions1 mark
Which of the following concentration terms is independent of temperature?
ExplanationMolality is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent — both are masses, and mass does not change with temperature. Molarity, normality and formality are all defined per litre of solution, and volume expands on heating, so all three fall as the temperature rises. This is why molality is used in the colligative-property formulae.
Q22Solutions1 mark
The vapour pressure of pure benzene at 300 K is 640 mm Hg. On dissolving 2·5 g of a non-volatile solute in 39 g of benzene (M = 78 g mol−1) it falls to 600 mm Hg. The molar mass of the solute is:
ExplanationRelative lowering = (p° − p)/p° = 40/640 = 0·0625, and by Raoult's law that equals the mole fraction of the solute. n(benzene) = 39/78 = 0·5 mol. From x2 = n2/(n1 + n2): n2 = 0·0333 mol, so M = 2·5/0·0333 = 75 g mol−1.
Q23Solutions1 mark
Which solution is isotonic with 0·1 M glucose at the same temperature? (Assume complete dissociation of the electrolytes.)
ExplanationIsotonic means equal osmotic pressure, and π = iCRT, so the effective particle concentration must match. Glucose does not ionise, so 0·1 M glucose gives 0·1 M particles. 0·05 M NaCl gives i = 2, so 0·05 × 2 = 0·1 M particles. ✓ 0·1 M NaCl gives 0·2 M and 0·1 M BaCl2 gives 0·3 M — both hypertonic.
Q24Solutions1 mark
A mixture of ethanol and water containing 95% ethanol boils at a constant 351·1 K and distils over unchanged in composition. This mixture is:
ExplanationEthanol and water deviate positively from Raoult's law — the ethanol–water hydrogen bonds are weaker than those they replace — so the observed vapour pressure is higher than predicted and the mixture boils at a minimum. Because the vapour has the same composition as the liquid, fractional distillation cannot enrich it further, which is why absolute alcohol cannot be made by distillation alone.
Q25Solutions1 mark
Benzoic acid dissolved in benzene is completely associated into dimers. Its van't Hoff factor is:
ExplanationFor association into an n-mer, i = 1 − α + α/n. With α = 1 and n = 2 this gives i = 0·5: two molecules become one particle, so the particle count halves. Association makes i < 1, a smaller colligative effect and an observed molar mass larger than the true one — here twice it, 244 against 122.
Q26Solutions1 mark
Which of the following is not a colligative property?
ExplanationA colligative property depends only on the number of solute particles, not their nature. The four are relative lowering of vapour pressure, elevation of boiling point, depression of freezing point and osmotic pressure. Viscosity depends very much on what the solute is — glycerol and salt at the same molality behave quite differently — so it is not colligative.
Q27Solutions1 mark
The molality of a solution containing 20% by mass of a solute of molar mass 40 g mol−1 is:
ExplanationTake 100 g of solution: 20 g of solute and 80 g of solvent. Moles of solute = 20/40 = 0·5. Mass of solvent = 0·080 kg. m = 0·5 / 0·080 = 6·25 molal. Dividing by 0·1 kg — i.e. using the mass of the solution instead of the solvent — gives 5·0 and is the standard slip.
Q28Electrochemistry1 mark
What happens to the voltage of a galvanic cell if the salt bridge is removed?
ExplanationThe salt bridge completes the circuit and keeps each half cell electrically neutral. Remove it and charge builds up immediately — positive in the anode compartment as cations form, negative in the cathode compartment as they are consumed — which stops the electron flow. The voltage falls to zero.
Q29Electrochemistry1 mark
On diluting an electrolyte solution, its specific conductance and its molar conductivity respectively:
ExplanationSpecific conductance κ is the conductance of 1 cm3 of solution: on dilution that fixed volume holds fewer ions, so κ decreases. Molar conductivity Λm = 1000κ/C is the conductance of the volume holding one mole: that volume grows, the ions move more freely, and for a weak electrolyte more of them form — so Λm increases.
Q30Electrochemistry1 mark
The quantity of charge required to liberate one mole of hydrogen gas at the cathode is:
ExplanationThe cathode reaction is 2H+ + 2e− → H2, so two moles of electrons give one mole of H2: 2 × 96500 = 193000 C. Note the question asks for a mole of the gas, not of H+ ions — that distinction is the whole question.
Q31Electrochemistry1 mark
For the cell Mg(s) | Mg2+ ‖ Ag+ | Ag(s), given E°(Mg2+/Mg) = −2·37 V and E°(Ag+/Ag) = +0·80 V, the standard cell potential is:
ExplanationE°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = 0·80 − (−2·37) = +3·17 V. Take both values as printed — they are standard reduction potentials — and subtract. Reversing the anode's sign first and then adding gives 1·57 V, which is the commonest error in the chapter.
Q32Electrochemistry1 mark
As a lead storage battery is discharged, the density of the sulphuric acid in it:
ExplanationOn discharge both electrodes are converted to PbSO4 and the overall reaction is Pb + PbO2 + 2H2SO4 → 2PbSO4 + 2H2O. The acid is consumed and water is produced, so the density decreases — which is exactly why measuring the acid's density is the standard test of a car battery's charge. Recharging reverses it.
Q33Electrochemistry1 mark
Blocks of magnesium are attached to the steel hull of a ship to prevent rusting. In this arrangement the magnesium acts as:
ExplanationThis is cathodic protection. Magnesium has the more negative electrode potential, so it is oxidised in preference to the iron: it becomes the anode and corrodes away, while the hull is forced to be the cathode and is protected. The block is replaced periodically, hence sacrificial anode. Zinc is used the same way, and galvanising works on the same principle.
Q34Electrochemistry1 mark
Kohlrausch's law is essential for finding the molar conductivity at infinite dilution of:
ExplanationFor a strong electrolyte Λm varies almost linearly with √C, so the plot can simply be extrapolated back to the axis. For a weak electrolyte the curve rises very steeply near infinite dilution and cannot be extrapolated — the ionisation is still changing fast. Kohlrausch's law supplies the value indirectly instead, by adding the ionic contributions or combining strong electrolytes.
Q35Chemical Kinetics1 mark
Which of the following is an example of a pseudo first order reaction?
ExplanationAcidic hydrolysis of ethyl acetate: CH3COOC2H5 + H2O → CH3COOH + C2H5OH is genuinely second order, but the water is in such vast excess that its concentration is effectively constant, so the observed rate depends only on the ester and the reaction behaves as first order. Inversion of cane sugar is the other standard example. NH3 on platinum is zero order, and N2O5 decomposition is truly first order.
Q36Chemical Kinetics1 mark
A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction because it:
ExplanationA catalyst offers an alternative mechanism with a lower Ea, so a much larger fraction of collisions clears the barrier — by the Arrhenius equation k = Ae−Ea/RT, a small fall in Ea raises k sharply. It does not change ΔH, and it lowers the barrier for the forward and reverse reactions equally, so it does not shift the equilibrium — it only reaches it sooner.
Q37Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The half-life of a first order reaction:
ExplanationFor first order, t½ = 0·693/k — the initial concentration does not appear at all, so it is independent of it. That is the defining signature of first order kinetics, and it is why radioactive decay has a fixed half-life. For zero order t½ = [R]0/2k, which is proportional to the initial concentration.
Q38Chemical Kinetics1 mark
For a zero order reaction, a plot of the concentration of the reactant [R] against time t is:
ExplanationFor zero order the integrated rate equation is [R] = [R]0 − kt — the equation of a straight line with intercept [R]0 and slope −k. The slope −k/2·303 belongs to the log[R] against t plot for a first order reaction, and the exponential curve is [R] against t for first order.
Q39Chemical Kinetics1 mark
Threshold energy is related to activation energy as:
ExplanationThreshold energy is the minimum total energy the colliding molecules must possess for a fruitful collision. Activation energy is the extra energy they must be given on top of what they already have. Hence Ethreshold = Eactivation + Eaverage of reactants. On an energy-profile diagram the threshold is the height of the peak above the zero, and Ea the height above the reactants.
Q40Chemical Kinetics1 mark
The half-life of a first order reaction is 10 minutes. The time required for the reaction to be 75% complete is:
ExplanationAfter one half-life 50% remains; after a second, half of that again, i.e. 25% remains and 75% has reacted. So the time is exactly two half-lives = 20 minutes. Checking with the formula: t = (2·303/k) log(100/25) with k = 0·693/10 = 0·0693 gives (2·303/0·0693)(0·602) = 20 min. ✓
