Q1Biomolecules1 mark
The pair of sugars that gives the same osazone with excess phenyl hydrazine is:
ExplanationOsazone formation involves only C-1 and C-2 of the sugar. Glucose and fructose differ only at those two carbons — glucose is an aldose, fructose the corresponding ketose — and are identical from C-3 onwards, so both give the same osazone. Mannose does too. Starch, cellulose and sucrose are not reducing sugars in the required sense and do not form osazones this way.Source · School worksheet Q1
Q2Biomolecules1 mark
Which of the following is not a monosaccharide?
ExplanationA monosaccharide cannot be hydrolysed to a simpler carbohydrate. Glycogen is a polysaccharide — the storage carbohydrate of animals, a branched polymer of α-glucose, often called animal starch. Ribose, galactose and fructose are all single sugar units.Source · School worksheet Q2
Q3Biomolecules1 mark
Which of the following is not a reducing sugar?
ExplanationA sugar reduces Tollens' or Fehling's only if it has a free aldehyde or ketone group, i.e. a free anomeric carbon. In sucrose the glycosidic link joins C-1 of glucose to C-2 of fructose, so both anomeric carbons are tied up and no free carbonyl can form. Maltose and lactose each keep one free anomeric carbon and are reducing.Source · School worksheet Q5
Q4Biomolecules1 mark
The main structural feature of a protein is the:
ExplanationProteins are polymers of α-amino acids joined by the peptide bond, –CO–NH–, formed between the –COOH of one residue and the –NH2 of the next with loss of water. Ester links occur in fats, glycosidic links in carbohydrates.Source · School worksheet Q6
Q5Biomolecules1 mark
The sugar present in DNA is:
ExplanationDNA is deoxyribonucleic acid: its pentose is 2-deoxy-D-ribose, which lacks the –OH at C-2 that ribose has. That missing hydroxyl is what makes DNA chemically more stable than RNA, and is why DNA is the molecule used for long-term storage of genetic information.Source · School worksheet Q7
Q6Biomolecules1 mark
Which vitamin contains cobalt?
ExplanationVitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin) is a coordination compound of cobalt — the only vitamin containing a metal ion. Its deficiency causes pernicious anaemia. It is also the only vitamin not obtainable from plant sources.Source · School worksheet Q8
Q7Biomolecules1 mark
Glycine is:
ExplanationIn glycine the side chain R is simply –H, so the α-carbon carries two hydrogens and therefore does not have four different groups. It is the one naturally occurring amino acid with no stereocentre, and so the only one that is optically inactive. Its side chain is non-polar.Source · School worksheet Q9
Q8Biomolecules1 mark
The nitrogenous bases common to both DNA and RNA are:
ExplanationDNA uses A, G, C and T; RNA uses A, G, C and U. The three shared bases are therefore adenine, guanine and cytosine. Thymine is unique to DNA and uracil to RNA — thymine is simply 5-methyluracil.Source · School worksheet Q10
Q9Biomolecules1 mark
The secondary structure of a protein is held together mainly by:
ExplanationThe secondary structure — the α-helix and the β-pleated sheet — is held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O of one peptide group and the N–H of another. Being weak, they are what heat or acid destroys on denaturation, while the covalent peptide bonds of the primary structure survive.Source · School worksheet Q11
Q10Biomolecules1 mark
Denaturation of a protein involves a change in:
ExplanationDenaturation breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak forces that hold the coil and the folded shape, so the secondary and tertiary structures are lost and the protein becomes a random coil. The peptide bonds are covalent and are not broken, so the sequence — the primary structure — survives. Boiling an egg is the everyday example.Source · School worksheet Q12
Q11Biomolecules1 mark
In living systems, enzymes:
ExplanationEnzymes are biological catalysts, almost all of them globular proteins. They are highly specific and enormously efficient, lowering the activation energy of a reaction. Energy is provided by carbohydrates and fats, oxygen is carried by haemoglobin and immunity is the work of antibodies.Source · School worksheet Q13
Q12Biomolecules1 mark
Pernicious anaemia is caused by deficiency of vitamin:
ExplanationVitamin B12. Its deficiency prevents normal maturation of red blood cells, giving pernicious anaemia. Compare: B1 (thiamine) deficiency causes beri-beri, B2 (riboflavin) causes cheilosis, and B6 causes convulsions.Source · School worksheet Q14
Q13Biomolecules1 mark
Nucleic acids are polymers of:
ExplanationA nucleoside is base + sugar. Adding a phosphate gives a nucleotide, and it is the nucleotide that is the repeating unit: the chain is built by phosphodiester links between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next. So a nucleic acid is a polynucleotide.Source · School worksheet Q15-16
Q14Biomolecules1 mark
Deficiency of vitamin D causes:
ExplanationVitamin D governs the absorption of calcium from the gut, so its deficiency gives soft, deformed bone — rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults. Scurvy is vitamin C, night blindness is vitamin A.Source · School worksheet Q4
Q15Biomolecules1 mark
The gradual change in specific rotation when α-D-glucose is dissolved in water is called:
ExplanationIn solution the cyclic hemiacetal opens to the open-chain aldehyde and re-closes, so the two anomers interconvert through it. The specific rotation drifts from +111° (α) or +19° (β) to the equilibrium value +52·5° — the rotation mutates. The two anomers differ at one carbon only, so this is not racemisation.Source · ISC board · mutarotation
Q16Biomolecules1 mark
Sucrose on hydrolysis gives an equimolar mixture of glucose and fructose which is called invert sugar because:
ExplanationSucrose is dextrorotatory, +66·5°. Hydrolysis gives glucose (+52·5°) and fructose (−92·4°); since the products are equimolar the mixture rotates by the mean, about −19·9°. The sign has gone from + to −, so the rotation is inverted — hence invert sugar, and the enzyme is called invertase.Source · ISC board · invert sugar
Q17Biomolecules1 mark
An amino acid exists in aqueous solution largely as a zwitter ion, which means it:
ExplanationThe –COOH gives its proton to the –NH2 of the same molecule, giving +H3N–CHR–COO−. There are two poles but they cancel, so the ion is overall neutral — which is why it is also called a dipolar ion. This is why amino acids are high-melting crystalline solids soluble in water.Source · ISC specimen · zwitter ion
Q18Biomolecules1 mark
An essential amino acid is one that:
Explanation‘Essential’ refers to dietary necessity, not importance: the body cannot make it, so it must be supplied by food. Valine, leucine and lysine are examples. Non-essential amino acids such as glycine and alanine are just as necessary but can be synthesised. Ten of the twenty are essential for humans.Source · ISC board · essential amino acids
Q19Biomolecules1 mark
Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose, yet humans can digest only starch. The reason is that:
ExplanationBoth are polymers of D-glucose, but starch uses α-1,4 links and cellulose β-1,4. Human amylase can hydrolyse the α-link only; we have no cellulase, so cellulose passes through as dietary fibre. Ruminants manage it only through microorganisms in the gut. The β-link also lets cellulose chains lie straight, which is what makes it a structural fibre.Source · Competency booklet · starch and cellulose
Q20Biomolecules1 mark
The sequence of amino acids in a protein chain is its:
ExplanationThe primary structure is the order in which the residues are joined — held by covalent peptide bonds. The secondary structure is the local coiling or pleating, the tertiary the overall folding of the whole chain, and the quaternary the assembly of several chains, as in haemoglobin.Source · ISC board · protein structure
Q21Biomolecules1 mark
Which group of vitamins is fat-soluble?
ExplanationA, D, E and K are fat-soluble; they are stored in the liver and adipose tissue, so they need not be taken daily — and an excess is harmful. The B group and C are water-soluble, are excreted in urine and must be supplied regularly.Source · ISC specimen · vitamins
Q22Biomolecules1 mark
Glucose reacts with hydroxylamine to give an oxime and adds HCN to give a cyanohydrin. These reactions show the presence of:
ExplanationOxime formation and cyanohydrin addition are both nucleophilic additions to C=O, so they demonstrate a carbonyl group — in glucose an aldehyde. The five –OH groups are shown instead by acetylation with acetic anhydride giving a penta-acetate.Source · ISC board · glucose reactions
Q23Biomolecules1 mark
In the DNA double helix, adenine always pairs with thymine through:
ExplanationA pairs with T through two hydrogen bonds, while G pairs with C through three. That difference is why a DNA region rich in G–C is harder to separate and has a higher melting temperature. The pairing is complementary, which is what allows replication.Source · Competency booklet · DNA structure
Q24Biomolecules1 mark
A nucleoside differs from a nucleotide in that a nucleoside:
ExplanationNucleoside = base + sugar. Nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate. The phosphate is what allows the phosphodiester links that build the polynucleotide chain, which is why the nucleotide, not the nucleoside, is the repeating unit of a nucleic acid.Source · ISC board · nucleoside
Q25Biomolecules1 mark
Enzymes are highly specific catalysts because:
ExplanationAn enzyme's active site is a pocket whose shape and chemistry fit one substrate — the lock-and-key picture, refined as induced fit. That is the source of specificity. Note the other options are each wrong in a standard way: a catalyst is not consumed, it lowers the activation energy, and enzymes are denatured by high temperature.Source · ISC specimen · enzyme specificity
