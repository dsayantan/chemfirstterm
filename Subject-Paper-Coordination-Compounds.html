Q1Coordination Compounds1 mark
The geometries of [Ni(CN)4]2− and [NiCl4]2− are respectively:
ExplanationBoth are Ni(II), d8. CN− is a strong-field ligand: it pairs the two unpaired electrons, freeing one 3d orbital for dsp2 — square planar and diamagnetic. Cl− is weak-field and cannot force pairing, so the metal falls back on sp3 — tetrahedral and paramagnetic. Same metal, same oxidation state, same coordination number; the ligand decides everything.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q1(A)(ii)
Q2Coordination Compounds1 mark
Which of the following will not show geometrical isomerism?
ExplanationGeometrical isomerism needs at least two of each of two kinds of ligand, so they can sit cis or trans. The complex with five ammines and one nitro is MA5B: with only one odd ligand there is nothing for it to be cis or trans to, and all six octahedral positions are equivalent. The others are MA4B2, M(AA)2B2 and square planar MA2B2, which all have cis and trans forms.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q1(B)(v)
Q3Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Cr(NH3)3Cl3] gives no precipitate with AgNO3. The reason is that:
ExplanationOnly ions outside the square brackets are free in solution and can be precipitated. Here all three Cl are bonded to chromium, so there is no free Cl− and no AgCl. Note the common distractor: the complex does not contain neutral ligands only — the three chlorides are anionic ligands, which is what makes it neutral overall.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q1(B)(vii)
Q4Coordination Compounds1 mark
Arrange in increasing order of molar conductivity: [Co(NH3)3Cl3], [Co(en)3]Cl3, [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2, [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl
ExplanationConductivity follows the number of ions the compound gives, which is the count of groups outside the brackets plus the complex ion itself: 0, 2, 3 and 4 respectively. The first is a neutral non-electrolyte and does not conduct at all.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q7(ii)
Q5Coordination Compounds1 mark
For [Fe(CN)6]4−, the oxidation number of iron, the number of unpaired electrons and the hybridisation are:
ExplanationCharge balance: x + 6(−1) = −4 gives Fe as +2, which is d6. CN− is strong field, so all six electrons pair into the three t2g orbitals — no unpaired electrons, hence diamagnetic — and two inner 3d orbitals are freed for d2sp3, an inner orbital octahedral complex.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q15(i)
Q6Coordination Compounds1 mark
On the basis of crystal field theory, the configuration of a d4 ion when Δo > P is:
ExplanationΔo > P means pairing is cheaper than climbing, so the fourth electron pairs in t2g rather than going up to eg. That gives t2g4: one orbital holds a pair and two hold single electrons, so two are unpaired — the low-spin case. With Δo < P the electron climbs instead, giving t2g3eg1 and four unpaired.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q15(ii)
Q7Coordination Compounds1 mark
EDTA is used in the treatment of lead poisoning because it:
ExplanationEDTA4− binds through four carboxylate oxygens and two nitrogens — hexadentate. Wrapping six donor atoms round one ion gives a large positive entropy of chelation and so an exceptionally large stability constant. The lead is locked into a water-soluble complex and excreted by the kidneys instead of binding to enzymes.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q20(i)-(iii)
Q8Coordination Compounds1 mark
CoBr3·4NH3·2H2O has a molar conductivity corresponding to four ions. Its formula is:
ExplanationFour ions means one complex cation and three bromides, so all three Br are outside the coordination sphere and the complex ion carries +3. Everything else — four NH3 and two H2O — must be inside, giving a coordination number of 6, which is what cobalt(III) prefers. The name is tetraamminediaquacobalt(III) bromide.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q20(iv)
Q9Coordination Compounds1 mark
The IUPAC name of [Pt(NH3)4Cl2][PtCl4] is:
ExplanationThe cation is named first, then the anion. In the cation x + 0 + 2(−1) = +2 gives Pt(IV); in the anion x + 4(−1) = −2 gives Pt(II). Ligands are named alphabetically — ammine before chlorido — and the anionic complex takes the -ate ending.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q20 OR (ii)
Q10Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Co(NH3)5Cl]SO4 and [Co(NH3)5SO4]Cl are examples of:
ExplanationThe chloride and the sulphate have swapped places between the coordination sphere and the counter-ion, so the two give different ions in solution — ionisation isomerism. The test distinguishes them: the first gives a white precipitate with BaCl2 (free sulphate), the second with AgNO3 (free chloride).Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q20 OR (iii)
Q11Coordination Compounds1 mark
For [Ni(CO)4], the oxidation state of nickel, its hybridisation and its magnetic behaviour are:
ExplanationCO is neutral and the complex is neutral, so nickel is in oxidation state zero. Ni(0) is 3d84s2; CO is a very strong field ligand, so the 4s electrons move into 3d, filling it to 3d10. With no unpaired electrons it is diamagnetic, and with 3d full the metal must bond using 4s and 4p — sp3, tetrahedral.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q20 OR (i)
Q12Coordination Compounds1 mark
FeSO4 with (NH4)2SO4 in 1:1 ratio gives the test for Fe2+, but CuSO4 with aqueous NH3 in 1:4 ratio does not give the test for Cu2+, because:
ExplanationMohr's salt is a double salt: it exists only in the solid and in water gives free Fe2+, NH4+ and SO42−. [Cu(NH3)4]SO4 is a coordination compound: it does ionise, into [Cu(NH3)4]2+ and sulphate, but the complex ion itself does not dissociate, so no free Cu2+ is available to test for.Source · Heritage 2024-25 Q7(i)
Q13Coordination Compounds1 mark
The number of unpaired electrons in [CoF6]3− is:
ExplanationCharge balance gives Co(III), which is d6. F− is a weak-field ligand, so Δo < P and the electrons spread out before pairing: t2g4eg2, leaving four unpaired. The complex is high spin, outer orbital (sp3d2) and paramagnetic. With a strong-field ligand the same d6 ion would be t2g6 and diamagnetic.Source · Heritage 2016-17 Q1(d)(i)
Q14Coordination Compounds1 mark
Using crystal field theory, the number of unpaired electrons in [FeCl4]− is:
ExplanationCharge balance: x + 4(−1) = −1 gives Fe(III), which is d5. The complex is tetrahedral, and in a tetrahedral field Δt is only about 4/9 of Δo — always smaller than the pairing energy. So tetrahedral complexes are invariably high spin and all five electrons stay unpaired. Cl− being weak-field points the same way.Source · Heritage 2016-17 Q5(b)
Q15Coordination Compounds1 mark
The formula of potassium trioxalatoaluminate(III) is:
ExplanationThe -ate ending means the complex is the anion, so potassium sits outside the brackets and is written first. Aluminium is +3 and three oxalates give 3 × (−2) = −6, so the complex ion is −3 overall and needs three K+.Source · Heritage 2016-17 Q5(a)
Q16Coordination Compounds1 mark
The IUPAC name of [Co(NH3)6]3+ and its magnetic behaviour are:
ExplanationNH3 as a ligand is named ammine (two m's — one m is the organic amine), and the ion carries +3 with six neutral ligands, so cobalt is +3. Co(III) is d6 and NH3 is strong field for cobalt(III), so all six electrons pair: no unpaired electrons, hence diamagnetic, with d2sp3 hybridisation.Source · ISC specimen · nomenclature
Q17Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Cu(NH3)4][PtCl4] and [Pt(NH3)4][CuCl4] are related as:
ExplanationBoth the cation and the anion are complex ions, and the ligands have been exchanged between the two metal centres. That is the definition of coordination isomerism, and it can only arise when both ions of a salt are complexes.Source · School worksheet · Q1(i)
Q18Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Co(NH3)5NO2]Cl2 can exist in two forms, one binding through N and the other through O. This is:
ExplanationNO2− is an ambidentate ligand: it has two different donor atoms and can attach either way round. Bound through nitrogen it is nitrito-N (nitro); through oxygen it is nitrito-O. Two compounds differing only in which atom is used are linkage isomers.Source · School worksheet · Q3
Q19Coordination Compounds1 mark
For [Cr(H2O)2(C2O4)2]−, the hybridisation and magnetic behaviour are:
ExplanationCharge balance: x + 2(0) + 2(−2) = −1 gives Cr(III), which is d3. Three electrons occupy the three t2g orbitals singly whatever the ligand strength, so two inner 3d orbitals are always vacant and the complex is d2sp3. Three unpaired electrons make it paramagnetic. d3 is the case where high and low spin coincide.Source · School worksheet · Q2(i)
Q20Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Co(NH3)3Cl3] exists as two geometrical isomers known as:
ExplanationFor an MA3B3 octahedral complex the three like ligands can occupy one triangular face of the octahedron — the fac isomer — or lie around a meridian, the mer isomer. cis and trans is the naming used for MA4B2 and square planar MA2B2 instead.Source · School worksheet · Q4
Q21Coordination Compounds1 mark
According to Werner's theory, the secondary valency of a metal in a complex corresponds to its:
ExplanationWerner distinguished two valencies. The primary valency is the oxidation number, is satisfied by negative ions, is ionisable and is non-directional. The secondary valency is the coordination number, is satisfied by ligands, is non-ionisable and is directional — which is why it gives the complex a definite geometry.Source · ISC board · Werner's theory
Q22Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Ti(H2O)6]3+ is violet while [Sc(H2O)6]3+ is colourless because:
ExplanationColour comes from an electron being promoted from t2g to eg — a d–d transition — which needs a partially filled d subshell. Ti3+ is 3d1 and qualifies; Sc3+ is 3d0 with no d electron at all. Note Sc3+ is empty, not full — a full d10 ion such as Zn2+ is colourless for the opposite reason.Source · Competency booklet · colour
Q23Coordination Compounds1 mark
Which lists ligands in order of increasing field strength?
ExplanationThe spectrochemical series runs weak field to strong field. The two ends are what must be remembered: the halides and water are weak (small splitting, so complexes tend to be high spin and paramagnetic), while CN− and CO are strong (large Δ, low spin, often diamagnetic). NH3 sits in the middle and counts as strong field for cobalt(III) and nickel(II).Source · ISC board · spectrochemical series
Q24Coordination Compounds1 mark
[Ni(en)3]2+ is more stable than [Ni(NH3)6]2+ mainly because:
ExplanationBoth complexes give nickel six nitrogen donors, so the bonding is very similar. What differs is that ethane-1,2-diamine is bidentate and closes a five-membered chelate ring. Releasing six NH3 and binding three en increases the number of free particles, so entropy favours the chelate strongly — the chelate effect. Note en is neutral, like NH3.Source · ISC specimen · chelate effect
Q25Coordination Compounds1 mark
Which of the following shows optical isomerism?
ExplanationOptical isomerism needs the ion to have no plane of symmetry, so it is not superimposable on its mirror image. The trans isomer of the bis-en complex does have such a plane and is optically inactive; the cis isomer does not, so it exists as non-superimposable d and l forms. The fully symmetrical hexaammine and the square planar trans complex are both achiral.Source · ISC board · optical isomerism
