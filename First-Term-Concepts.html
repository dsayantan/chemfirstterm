C2Concept
Henry's law — solubility of a gas in a liquid
p = KH · x    where p is the partial pressure of the gas and x its mole fraction in solution
  • KH is the Henry's law constant. It has the unit of pressure.
  • A larger KH means a lower solubility — note the inverse sense, which is the commonest confusion here.
  • KH increases with temperature, so gases become less soluble as the liquid is warmed (dissolution of a gas is exothermic).
ApplicationExplanation
Soft drinks are bottled under high CO2 pressurehigh p forces more CO2 into solution
Deep-sea divers suffer bendsN2 dissolves in blood at depth and bubbles out on rapid ascent; divers use air diluted with helium, which is less soluble
Climbers suffer anoxia at altitudelow partial pressure of O2 lowers its concentration in blood
Aquatic life is more comfortable in cold waterO2 is more soluble at lower temperature

C3Concept
Raoult's law — both components volatile

For a solution of two volatile liquids A and B, each contributes in proportion to its mole fraction:

pA = pAo xA     pB = pBo xB     ptotal = pAoxA + pBoxB

The composition of the vapour is not the composition of the liquid. By Dalton's law the mole fraction of A in the vapour is

yA = pAptotal
Hold on to this  · The vapour is always richer in the more volatile component — the one with the higher po. That single sentence is the whole basis of fractional distillation.

C4Concept
Raoult's law — non-volatile solute, and relative lowering

If the solute is non-volatile it contributes nothing to the vapour, so the vapour pressure of the solution is simply that of the solvent, reduced in proportion to its mole fraction:

p = po x1   ⇒   po − ppo = x2

The quantity (po − p)/po is the relative lowering of vapour pressure, and it equals the mole fraction of the solute — this is Raoult's second law, and it is a colligative property. See proof P1.

C6Concept
Azeotropes, and why fractional distillation fails on them

An azeotrope is a binary mixture of a definite composition that boils at a constant temperature and distils over unchanged — the vapour has the same composition as the liquid, so distillation cannot separate it further.

  • Minimum-boiling azeotrope — from a solution showing positive deviation. Example: ethanol + water, 95% ethanol, boiling at 351·1 K. This is why absolute alcohol cannot be obtained by distillation alone.
  • Maximum-boiling azeotrope — from a solution showing negative deviation. Example: nitric acid + water, 68% HNO3, boiling at 393·5 K.

C7Concept
The four colligative properties

Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles present, and not at all on their nature.

PropertyExpressionConstant
Relative lowering of vapour pressure(po − p)/po = x2—
Elevation of boiling pointΔTb = Kb mKb, molal elevation constant (ebullioscopic)

Memory hook  · All four are proportional to the number of particles. So for the same molar concentration, a solute that splits into more ions shows a larger effect: glucose (1 particle) < NaCl (2) < CaCl2 (3) < K3[Fe(CN)6] (4). Every “arrange these solutions” question is this one idea.

C8Concept
The three working formulae for molar mass

Learn these three; they are the whole numerical content of the unit

Vapour pressure:   M2 = w2 M1 po(po − p) w1Boiling / freezing point:   M2 = 1000 Kb w2ΔTb w1    M2 = 1000 Kf w2ΔTf w1Osmotic pressure:   M2 = w2 R Tπ V

Common slip  · The 1000 in the middle pair exists only to convert w1 from grams to kilograms, because molality is per kilogram. Dropping it is the single commonest arithmetic error in this unit — and it makes the answer wrong by a factor of a thousand, so it is easy to spot if you sanity-check the size of your answer.

C9Concept
Osmosis and osmotic pressure
  • Osmosis — the flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane from the dilute to the concentrated side.
  • Osmotic pressure π — the excess pressure that must be applied to the solution to stop osmosis. π = CRT, with C in mol L−1 and T in kelvin.
  • Isotonic solutions have equal osmotic pressure; a hypertonic solution is more concentrated than the reference and a hypotonic one less concentrated.
  • Reverse osmosis — applying a pressure greater than π drives solvent the other way; this is how sea water is desalinated.
Hold on to this  · Osmotic pressure is the colligative property of choice for macromolecules such as proteins and polymers: their molar masses are so large that ΔTf and ΔTb are too small to measure, but π is measurable at room temperature.

C12Concept
Degree of dissociation and degree of association from i

These two relations are derived in P5; both are worth memorising because they turn a three-mark question into two lines.

Dissociation  —  one particle gives n particles

i = 1 + (n − 1)α   ⇒   α = i − 1n − 1

Association  —  n particles give one particle

i = 1 − α(1 − 1/n)   ⇒   α = n (1 − i)n − 1
For the common case of dimerisation (n = 2), the association formula collapses to the very quotable α = 2(1 − i).

C13Concept
Why an electrolyte's i falls short of the ideal value

KCl should give i = 2, yet measured values are nearer 1·9. The shortfall is interionic attraction: at any instant some K+ and Cl− ions are close enough to behave as a single particle (an ion pair), so the solution contains slightly fewer independent particles than complete dissociation would give. The effect shrinks on dilution, so i approaches 2 as the solution becomes very dilute.

P1Proof
Relative lowering of vapour pressure gives the molar mass
  1. For a non-volatile solute, p = pox1. Since x1 + x2 = 1:
p = po(1 − x2)  ⇒  po − ppo = x2
  1. Write x2 in terms of the masses, with n1 = w1/M1 and n2 = w2/M2:
x2 = n2n1 + n2  ≈  n2n1   for a dilute solution, where n2 ≪ n1po − ppo = w2 M1M2 w1  ⇒  M2 = w2 M1 po(po − p) w1
The approximation in step 2 is what makes this the dilute solution form. ISC expects it; if a question is not dilute, use the exact x2.
■ proved

P2Proof
Elevation of boiling point, and the molar mass formula
  1. Adding a non-volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure, so the solution must be heated further before its vapour pressure reaches atmospheric. Hence the boiling point rises.
  2. For a dilute solution the elevation is proportional to the lowering of vapour pressure, and hence to the mole fraction of solute, and hence to the molality:
ΔTb ∝ m  ⇒  ΔTb = Kb m
  1. Substituting m = (w2/M2) ÷ (w1/1000):
ΔTb = 1000 Kb w2M2 w1  ⇒  M2 = 1000 Kb w2ΔTb w1
Kb is the elevation produced by a 1 molal solution; its unit is K kg mol−1. For water Kb = 0·52.
■ proved

P3Proof
Depression of freezing point, and the molar mass formula
  1. A liquid freezes when its vapour pressure equals that of the solid. The solute lowers the solution's vapour pressure, so that equality is reached at a lower temperature.
  2. By the same dilute-solution argument as P2:
ΔTf = Kf m   ⇒   ΔTf = 1000 Kf w2M2 w1M2 = 1000 Kf w2ΔTf w1
For water Kf = 1·86 K kg mol−1; for benzene 5·12. This is why salt or ethylene glycol is used to stop water freezing.
■ proved

P4Proof
Osmotic pressure and the molar mass of a macromolecule
  1. van't Hoff showed that a dilute solution obeys π V = n2 R T, the same form as the ideal gas equation. Hence
π = n2V R T = C R T
  1. Putting n2 = w2/M2:

Use R = 0·0821 L atm K−1 mol−1 when π is in atm and V in litres. Two solutions are isotonic when their π, and therefore their molar concentrations C, are equal.

Dissociation. Let one mole of a solute give n particles, and let α be the fraction that dissociates.

Interconverting concentration units
Recognise it by“A solution is x molal / x% by mass; find its mole fraction or molarity.”
MethodFix a convenient basis. For molality, take exactly 1 kg = 1000 g of solvent, so the moles of solvent are 1000/M1 and the moles of solute are the molality itself. Then read off whatever is asked.

  2. solute = 2·5 mol (that is what 2·5 molal means)
x2 = 2·52·5 + 55·56 = 2·558·06 = 0·0431

Henry's law
Recognise it byA value of KH and a pressure are given; the solubility (as a mole fraction) is wanted, or the reverse.
MethodUse p = KHx directly, so x = p/KH. Check that the pressure units of p and KH match before dividing — mixing mm Hg with atm is the usual slip.

T3Problem type
Raoult's law for two volatile liquids
Recognise it byTwo pure vapour pressures and the amounts of each liquid are given; the total vapour pressure, or the composition of the vapour, is wanted.
MethodFind the mole fractions in the liquid, apply Raoult to each component, add for ptotal. For the vapour composition use Dalton: yA = pA/ptotal.
Worked example
An ideal solution contains 2 mol of A (pAo = 80 mm Hg) and 3 mol of B (pBo = 120 mm Hg). Find the total vapour pressure and the mole fraction of A in the vapour.
  1. xA = 2/5 = 0·4 , xB = 0·6
  2. pA = 80 × 0·4 = 32 mm ; pB = 120 × 0·6 = 72 mm
ptotal = 32 + 72 = 104 mm Hg     yA = 32104 = 0·308
Sanity check: yA = 0·308 is less than xA = 0·4, so the vapour is richer in B — and B is the more volatile component. That is the direction it must come out.

T4Problem type
Molar mass from the lowering of vapour pressure
Recognise it byThe vapour pressures of the pure solvent and of the solution are both quoted.
MethodUse M2 = w2M1po ⁄ (po − p)w1 from P1. Compute (po − p) first; it is usually a small difference of two similar numbers, so keep the decimals.
Worked example
A solution of 8·45 g of lactose in 100 g of water has a vapour pressure of 4·559 mm Hg at 0 °C. The vapour pressure of pure water is 4·579 mm Hg. Calculate the molecular weight of lactose.  (Heritage 2024–25)
  1. p° − p = 4·579 − 4·559 = 0·020 mm Hg
M2 = 8·45 × 18 × 4·5790·020 × 100 = 696·52·0 = 348 g mol−1
The true molar mass of lactose is 342; the small excess comes from the dilute-solution approximation in P1. An answer near 348 is correct for the method ISC teaches.

T9Problem type
Finding the van't Hoff factor
Recognise it byAn observed colligative property is quoted alongside enough data to compute the theoretical one.
MethodCompute the property assuming i = 1, then divide: i = observed ÷ calculated. Sanity-check the answer — an electrolyte must give i > 1 and an associating solute i < 1. If it comes out the wrong side of 1, you have inverted the fraction.

i = 0·140·0534 = 2·62
  1. K₂SO₄ → 2K⁺ + SO₄²⁻ , so n = 3

Recognise it byA salt in water, with a colligative measurement; the degree of dissociation is wanted.
MethodFind i as in T9, count n from the dissociation equation, then α = (i − 1)/(n − 1). Getting n right matters: NaCl gives 2, CaCl2 and K2SO4 give 3, K3[Fe(CN)6] and AlCl3 give 4.

T11Problem type
Degree of association
Recognise it byAn acid or phenol dissolved in benzene — the giveaway solvent, because it cannot hydrogen bond to the solute and so lets the solute molecules pair up with each other.
MethodSame first step: find i, which will come out less than 1. Then use α = n(1 − i)/(n − 1), or for a dimer simply α = 2(1 − i).

Phenol associates in benzene to form a dimer. A solution of 2·5 g of phenol (M = 94 g mol−1) in 120 g of benzene lowers its freezing point by 0·85 K. Calculate the degree of association. Kf(benzene) = 5·12 K kg mol−1.  (Specimen 2027)

i = 0·851·135 = 0·749α = 2(1 − 0·749) = 0·50, i.e. 50%

T12Problem type
Comparing or ordering colligative properties
Recognise it by“Arrange these solutions of equal concentration in increasing order of freezing point / boiling point / osmotic pressure.”
MethodCount the particles, i.e. find i for each. Then decide the direction: a larger i means a larger ΔTb, ΔTf and π — so a higher boiling point, a lower freezing point, and a higher osmotic pressure.
Worked example
Arrange in increasing order of freezing point: 0·1 M glucose, 0·1 M NaCl, 0·1 M CaCl2, 0·1 M K3[Fe(CN)6].

  2. bigger i ⇒ bigger depression ⇒ lower freezing point

Common slip  · Read the direction the question asks for. Increasing freezing point is the reverse of increasing depression, so the answer runs from the most-dissociated solute to the least. Half the marks on this type are lost by giving the right chemistry in the wrong order.

T13Problem type
Explaining an abnormal molar mass
Recognise it by“The observed molar mass of acetic acid in benzene is 120 instead of 60. Explain.”
MethodDecide whether the observation implies more or fewer particles than expected, then name association or dissociation and give the reason — hydrogen bonding for association, ionisation for dissociation.
Worked example
The molar mass of acetic acid determined in benzene by the depression of freezing point comes out as about 120 g mol−1, twice the expected 60. Explain.
Answer  · An observed molar mass twice the true value means the colligative property was half what was expected, so the solution contained only half as many particles as were dissolved. Acetic acid molecules pair up in benzene through two intermolecular hydrogen bonds, forming a cyclic dimer; benzene, being non-polar, cannot hydrogen bond to the acid and so does nothing to prevent this. Here i = 0·5 and the degree of association is essentially 1.

T14Problem type
Identifying the type of deviation or azeotrope
Recognise it by“What type of azeotrope does a solution of chloroform and acetone form? Justify on the basis of intermolecular interactions.”
MethodAsk whether the A–B interaction is stronger or weaker than A–A and B–B. Stronger ⇒ negative deviation ⇒ maximum-boiling azeotrope. Weaker ⇒ positive deviation ⇒ minimum-boiling azeotrope.
Worked example
What type of azeotropic mixture is formed by chloroform and acetone? Justify your answer.  (Specimen 2027)
Answer  · A maximum-boiling azeotrope. Chloroform and acetone form a hydrogen bond between unlike molecules — the acidic hydrogen of CHCl3 to the carbonyl oxygen of acetone — which is stronger than either of the pure-liquid interactions. Each component is therefore held back from escaping, the vapour pressure is lower than Raoult's law predicts (negative deviation), and a higher temperature is needed to boil the mixture.
Ethanol and water are the standard contrast: hydrogen bonding between ethanol molecules is disrupted by water, so escape becomes easier, giving positive deviation and a minimum-boiling azeotrope at 95% ethanol.

2. Electrochemistry

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C1Concept
The galvanic cell — what is where

A galvanic (voltaic) cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy by letting a spontaneous redox reaction happen in two separated halves, so the electrons must travel through a wire to get from one to the other.

  • Anode — oxidation, and in a galvanic cell it is the negative terminal. Written on the left.
  • Cathode — reduction, the positive terminal. Written on the right.
  • Electrons flow through the wire anode → cathode; conventional current flows the other way.
  • In the solution, cations move towards the cathode and anions towards the anode.
Hold on to this  · An-Ox, Red-Cat: Anode – Oxidation, Reduction – Cathode. This holds for an electrolytic cell too; what flips there is the sign, the anode becoming positive.

Cell notation, and reading a cell backwards

The shorthand puts the anode on the left and the cathode on the right, a single bar for a phase boundary and a double bar for the salt bridge:

Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq, 1 M)  ‖  Cu2+(aq, 1 M) | Cu(s)

Hold on to this  · Do not reverse the sign of the anode's potential and then add. Take both values as printed — standard reduction potentials — and subtract. Getting this backwards is the single commonest arithmetic error in the chapter.

C3Concept
Standard electrode potential and the hydrogen electrode

A single electrode potential cannot be measured; only a difference can. So one electrode is defined as zero and everything is measured against it.

  • The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE): H2 at 1 bar bubbled over platinised Pt dipping in 1 M H+ at 298 K. Its potential is 0·00 V by definition.
  • Standard conditions mean 1 M for every ion, 1 bar for every gas, and 298 K.
  • A positive E° means the species is reduced more readily than H+; a negative E° means less readily.
Hold on to this  · Every table in every ISC paper is of reduction potentials. The more positive the value, the stronger the oxidising agent; the more negative, the stronger the reducing agent.

C5Concept
The salt bridge
  • It completes the circuit, providing the ionic path between the two half cells.
  • It maintains electrical neutrality. Oxidation at the anode makes cations, so anions migrate from the bridge into the anode compartment; reduction at the cathode consumes cations, so cations migrate into the cathode compartment.
  • It keeps the two solutions from mixing, so no liquid junction potential develops.
It is a U-tube of an inert electrolyte — KCl, KNO3 or NH4NO3 — set in agar. Inert means neither ion reacts with either electrolyte, and the two ions have nearly equal mobilities.
Hold on to this  · Remove the salt bridge and the voltage falls to zero at once. Charge builds up in each half cell within an instant and stops the reaction. This is asked as a one-mark question almost every year.

C7Concept
The Nernst equation

Standard potentials assume 1 M. The Nernst equation corrects for any other concentration:

Ecell = E°cell − 2·303 RTnF log Q   =   E°cell − 0·0591n log Q   at 298 K
  • Q is written for the cell reaction: products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient. Pure solids and liquids are omitted.
  • Diluting the anode ion (a product) raises the emf; diluting the cathode ion (a reactant) lowers it.
  • For a single electrode Mn+/M, E = E° + (0·0591/n) log[Mn+].
Hold on to this  · At 298 K the factor is 0·0591/n. Remember it as 0·06 if you must, but write 0·0591 — ISC marks the substituted expression.

  • Conductivity κ is the conductance of 1 cm3 of solution.

At infinite dilution each ion contributes a fixed share to the molar conductivity, independent of the other ion it came with:

C15Concept
Corrosion, and how it is prevented

Rusting is electrochemical. On a wet iron surface one patch acts as anode and another as cathode:

anode: Fe → Fe2+ + 2e−     cathode: O2 + 4H+ + 4e− → 2H2O

Fe2+ is then oxidised further by air to hydrated ferric oxide, Fe2O3·xH2O — rust.

  • Barrier methods — painting, greasing, electroplating.
  • Galvanising — a zinc coat, which protects even when scratched because zinc is the more reactive metal.
  • Cathodic protection — connect the iron to a block of a more reactive metal (Mg or Zn). That block becomes the anode and corrodes instead; the iron is forced to be the cathode and is protected. The block is a sacrificial anode, and this is how ships' hulls and buried pipelines are protected.
Hold on to this  · In cathodic protection the protected metal is the cathode and the sacrificial block is the anode — the wording of the answer matters, and the competency booklet asks it in exactly those terms.

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q
  1. For a cell, ΔG = −nFE and ΔG° = −nFE°. Substitute both:

2·303 × 8·314 × 29896500 = 0·0591  ⇒  E = E° − 0·0591n log Q

P5Proof
Faraday's two laws, and why cells in series deposit equivalent masses
  1. Discharging one mole of Mn+ needs n moles of electrons, i.e. nF coulombs. So for a charge Q the moles discharged are Q/nF and the mass is
w = QnF × (atomic mass) = EF Q    where E = atomic massn

w = Z Q = Z I t
  1. Two cells in series pass the same charge Q. Dividing one expression by the other, F and Q cancel:
w1w2 = E1E2  — Faraday's second law
So one faraday through cells of HCl, AgNO3 and CuSO4 liberates 1 mol H+, 1 mol Ag and ½ mol Cu — masses in the ratio of the equivalent weights, moles in the ratio 2 : 2 : 1.

MethodAnswer through the definition of each quantity: κ is per cm3, Λm is per mole. For a graph, the curve that rises steeply near the axis is the weak electrolyte; the nearly straight ones are strong, and the steeper of those carries the more/faster ions.
Worked example
Yash measures the conductivity of 1 M ethanoic acid and 1 M HCl. Will he get the same value? What happens as he dilutes them?  (Competency booklet)
  1. No. HCl is a strong acid and ionises completely, so it furnishes far more ions per cm3 and its conductivity is much the greater.
  2. On dilution the conductivity of both falls (fewer ions per cm3) while the molar conductivity of both rises — slightly for HCl, which is already fully ionised, and steeply for ethanoic acid, which ionises much further.
The mark is for naming the reason — complete against partial ionisation — not merely for saying which is bigger.

3. Chemical Kinetics

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C1Concept
What kinetics adds to thermodynamics

Thermodynamics tells you whether a reaction can happen and how far it goes; it says nothing about how fast. Chemical kinetics supplies the missing half: the rate, the factors that control it, and the step-by-step mechanism.

Hold on to this  · A reaction can be thermodynamically spontaneous (ΔG negative) and still be immeasurably slow — the rusting of iron, or the conversion of diamond to graphite. Spontaneity and speed are independent questions.

3. Chemical Kinetics

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C2Concept
Rate of reaction: average and instantaneous

Rate is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

average rate = − Δ[R]Δt = + Δ[P]Δt      instantaneous rate = − d[R]dt = + d[P]dt
  • The minus sign for a reactant is bookkeeping only — it makes the rate positive, since [R] is falling. Rate is never negative.
  • Average rate is measured over a finite interval; instantaneous rate is the slope of the tangent to the concentration–time curve at one instant.
  • Unit: mol L−1 s−1. For gases measured by pressure it may be atm s−1.
Hold on to this  · The rate of a reaction is not constant during the reaction — it falls as reactants are used up. What stays constant at a given temperature is k, not the rate.

The rate law is the experimentally established equation linking rate to concentrations:

rate = k[A]x[B]y
  • x and y are found only by experiment. They are not read off the balanced equation.

C6Concept
Order of a reaction

The order is the sum of the powers of the concentration terms in the experimentally determined rate law.

  • It is an experimental quantity.
  • It may be zero, a whole number, a fraction, or even negative.
  • A negative order means that species slows the reaction — usually a product that reverses an earlier equilibrium.
  • The order with respect to one reactant is the power of that reactant alone; the overall order is the sum.
Hold on to this  · For an elementary reaction — and only then — order and stoichiometry coincide. Most reactions are not elementary, which is exactly why order must be measured.

C9Concept

The rate is independent of the concentration of the reactant: rate = k[R]0 = k.

  • Examples: decomposition of NH3 on a hot platinum surface; decomposition of HI on a gold surface; some photochemical reactions; enzyme reactions at saturating substrate.

3. Chemical Kinetics

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C14Concept
The energy profile, and what a catalyst does to it
  • ΔH = (energy of products) − (energy of reactants). It is negative for an exothermic reaction (products lower, as drawn) and positive for an endothermic one.
  • A catalyst provides an alternative path of lower Ea. It lowers the forward and backward barriers equally.
  • A catalyst therefore changes k and the rate, but not ΔH, not Kc, and not the position of equilibrium — only the speed of reaching it.
Common slip  · Three sentences that lose marks: “a catalyst supplies energy to the reactants”, “a catalyst increases the yield”, “a catalyst changes ΔH”. All three are wrong.

3. Chemical Kinetics

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C15Concept
Collision theory

For a reaction to occur the particles must collide — but only a tiny fraction of collisions lead to reaction. An effective collision must satisfy two independent conditions:

  • Energy condition — the colliding molecules must together possess energy ≥ the threshold energy.
  • Orientation condition — they must be correctly aligned at the moment of impact, so that the bonds to be broken and formed are properly presented to each other.
Hold on to this  · Collision frequency in a gas is of the order of 1030 collisions per litre per second, yet most reactions are far slower than that. The gap is explained entirely by these two conditions.

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C16Concept
Effect of temperature

A rise of 10 K roughly doubles the rate of most reactions — the temperature coefficient is about 2.

The average kinetic energy rises only slightly over 10 K. What changes dramatically is the high-energy tail of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution: the number of molecules with E ≥ Ea roughly doubles, and only those molecules can react.

Hold on to this  · The rate depends exponentially on temperature because k = Ae−Ea/RT, with T in the exponent. That is why a small change in T produces a large change in k.

k = A e−Ea/RT      log k = log A − Ea2·303 RT
  • A, the frequency or pre-exponential factor, is the rate constant the reaction would have if every collision were effective. It has the same unit as k.

3. Chemical Kinetics

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C18Concept
Mechanism and the rate-determining step

Most reactions proceed through a sequence of elementary steps. The slowest step is the rate-determining step, and it alone controls the overall rate — just as a queue moves no faster than its slowest counter.

  • The observed rate law reflects the species involved up to and including the slow step.
  • Reactants consumed only in later, faster steps do not appear in the rate law — which is why the rate law often disagrees with the overall stoichiometry.
  • A catalyst can appear in the rate law if it takes part in the slow step, even though it is absent from the overall equation.

  2. Within the complex, name the ligands first, in alphabetical order, then the metal. Alphabetise by the ligand name, ignoring the multiplying prefix.

C10Concept
Crystal field theory and the spectrochemical series
  • In an octahedral field the five d orbitals split into a lower t2g set of three and an upper eg set of two, separated by the crystal field splitting energy Δo.
  • In a tetrahedral field the order is inverted — e below t2 — and the splitting is much smaller, Δt = (4/9)Δo. Tetrahedral complexes are therefore almost always high spin.
  • Whether electrons pair up depends on Δo against the pairing energy P.

The rule that answers every CFT question

Δo > P  (strong field): electrons prefer to pair in t2g → low spin, fewer unpaired electrons.
Δo < P  (weak field): electrons prefer to occupy eg → high spin, more unpaired electrons.
Spectrochemical series (weak field ⟶ strong field)
I−<Br−<Cl−<F−<H2O<NH3<en<NO2−<CN−<CO
Memory hook  · Only the two ends really need memorising: the halides and water are weak field (high spin, often paramagnetic), while CN− and CO are strong field (low spin, often diamagnetic). NH3 sits in the middle and is treated as strong field for cobalt(III) and nickel(II).

C12Concept
Colour — the d–d transition

A transition metal complex is coloured because Δo happens to correspond to the energy of visible light. An electron absorbs a photon and is promoted from t2g to eg — a d–d transition — and the colour seen is the complement of the colour absorbed.

  • A larger Δo means a higher absorbed frequency, so changing the ligand changes the colour. [Cu(H2O)4]2+ is pale blue; [Cu(NH3)4]2+ is deep blue.
  • A complex with no d electrons (d0, e.g. Sc3+, Ti4+) or a full d subshell (d10, e.g. Zn2+, Cu+) has no possible d–d transition and is therefore colourless.
Hold on to this  · The two-part answer that earns full marks: the d subshell is partially filled, so a d–d transition is possible. Both halves are needed — this is set as a fill-in-the-blanks in the specimen papers. Say partially filled, not unpaired electrons: [Fe(CN)6]4− and [Co(NH3)6]3+ are low-spin d6, so they have no unpaired electrons at all, and both are still coloured.

5. Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C1Concept
Nature of the C–X bond
  • The bond is polar, because the halogen is more electronegative than carbon: Cδ+—Xδ−. The carbon therefore carries a partial positive charge and is open to attack by a nucleophile. That single fact drives the whole unit.
  • Bond strength falls and bond length rises down the group: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I. So iodides are the most reactive and fluorides the least — the weakest bond breaks most easily, and I− is also the best leaving group.
  • In a haloarene the halogen is attached to an sp2 carbon and its lone pair conjugates with the ring, giving the C–X bond partial double bond character. It is therefore shorter and stronger, and very much harder to break.
Hold on to this  · Two consequences of that last point run through the whole of the haloarene section: aryl halides resist nucleophilic substitution (C11), and the halogen is deactivating but ortho/para directing (C12).

5. Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

1 · The concepts, in the order they build

C2Concept
Physical properties
  • Boiling point rises with the mass of the halogen and the length of the chain: RF < RCl < RBr < RI. Among isomers, branching lowers the boiling point, as always.
  • Haloalkanes boil above the parent alkanes (greater dipole–dipole and van der Waals forces) but below alcohols of comparable mass, since they cannot hydrogen bond.
  • They are insoluble in water — they cannot form hydrogen bonds with it, and the energy needed to break the water–water bonds is not recovered — but are freely soluble in organic solvents.
  • They are denser than water, and the density increases with the number and mass of halogen atoms.

C5Concept
Optical activity, inversion and racemisation
  • A haloalkane whose halogen-bearing carbon has four different groups is chiral and optically active — e.g. 2-chlorobutane.
  • An SN2 reaction attacks from the side opposite the leaving group, so the configuration is turned inside out like an umbrella in the wind: the product has the opposite configuration. This is Walden inversion.
  • An SN1 reaction goes through a planar carbocation, which the nucleophile can attack equally from either face. Equal amounts of the two forms are produced, so the product is a racemic mixture and is optically inactive.
Hold on to this  · So the stereochemical outcome identifies the mechanism: complete inversion means SN2, racemisation means SN1. That is how the mechanism was established experimentally.

C11Concept
Why haloarenes resist nucleophilic substitution
  • The C–X bond has partial double bond character from resonance with the ring, so it is shorter and stronger and much harder to break (C1).
  • The carbon is sp2 hybridised and therefore more electronegative, holding the electron pair more tightly.
  • The π electron cloud of the ring repels the approaching nucleophile.
  • The phenyl cation that an SN1 route would need is very unstable, and backside SN2 attack on a flat sp2 carbon is geometrically impossible.

So forcing conditions are needed — chlorobenzene with NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm (Dow's process, Part VI). But an electron-withdrawing group at the ortho or para position makes it far easier, because it stabilises the intermediate carbanion:

NaOH, 443 K
With two or three nitro groups, hydrolysis proceeds at room temperature. A group at the meta position gives no such help, because its resonance withdrawal cannot reach the carbon under attack.

C12Concept
Directive influence of halogen — deactivating but o/p directing

A halogen on a benzene ring does two opposing things:

  • By the −I effect it withdraws electron density from the whole ring, making the ring less reactive than benzene towards electrophiles — so halogen is deactivating.
  • By the +R effect its lone pair is donated into the ring, and that donation reaches the ortho and para positions specifically — so halogen is ortho/para directing.
Hold on to this  · Halogen is therefore the standard example of a group that is deactivating yet o/p directing. The −I effect dominates for how fast; the +R effect dominates for where.

2 · The mechanisms

M1Proof
SN2 — one step, backside attack, inversion
  1. The nucleophile approaches the carbon from the side directly opposite the leaving group — the only side not shielded by the halogen.
  2. Bond making and bond breaking happen together, passing through a single transition state in which the carbon is five-coordinate and the three unchanged groups lie in a plane.
  3. As the halide leaves, those three groups fold through, and the configuration is inverted.
HO− + CH3–Br  →  [HO···CH3···Br]−  →  CH3–OH + Br−rate = k[CH3Br][HO−]   — second order, and the nucleophile appears in the rate law
Because the attack needs room at the back, reactivity falls sharply with branching: 1° > 2° > 3°. A tertiary halide is essentially inert to SN2.

  1. Slow, rate determining: the C–X bond ionises on its own, giving a planar carbocation and the halide ion.

Reactivity follows carbocation stability, 3° > 2° > 1°. This is also the mechanism behind the Lucas test in Part VI.

M4Proof
Electrophilic substitution in a haloarene
  1. The electrophile is generated as usual — Cl+ from Cl2/AlCl3, NO2+ from conc. HNO3/H2SO4.

Why does chlorobenzene not undergo nucleophilic substitution readily?

Those positions are therefore the ones attacked. The −I effect governs the rate; the +R effect governs the orientation.

Structure — why phenol is different
  • In an alcohol and in an ether the oxygen is sp3 hybridised and the C–O–H / C–O–C angle is slightly more than tetrahedral (about 109° in alcohols, 111·7° in ethers) because of the bulk of the alkyl groups and the lone pairs.

2 · The mechanisms

M8Proof
Why phenol is acidic — the resonance argument set out formally
  1. Phenol ionises to give the phenoxide ion and H+.
  2. In phenoxide, the lone pair on oxygen overlaps with the π system of the ring, so the negative charge is delocalised on to the two ortho and the para carbons. Five resonance contributors can be written.
  3. Delocalisation spreads charge, and spread charge means lower energy. The phenoxide ion is therefore stabilised, and its formation is favourable.
  4. By contrast, in an alkoxide ion the charge is confined to one oxygen atom, and the +I effect of the alkyl group pushes still more electron density on to it, raising its energy.
  5. A more stable conjugate base means a stronger acid. Hence phenol (Ka ≈ 10−10) is about a million times more acidic than ethanol (Ka ≈ 10−16).

A second, smaller effect points the same way: the –OH oxygen in phenol is attached to an sp2 carbon, which is more electronegative than an sp3 carbon and so pulls the O–H electrons towards itself, weakening the bond.

Why the carbonyl carbon is attacked by nucleophiles

The C=O bond is strongly polar: oxygen is far more electronegative than carbon, and the π electrons are pulled towards it. The carbon therefore carries a substantial δ+ charge and is electrophilic.

Hold on to this  · Everything in the first half of this unit follows from that one polarity. The carbon is attacked by nucleophiles — and because the carbon is sp2 and planar, attack converts it to sp3, which is why the characteristic reaction is nucleophilic addition and not substitution.

Physical properties
  • Aldehydes and ketones boil above the corresponding alkanes and ethers, because of dipole–dipole attraction, but below alcohols of comparable mass, because they have no O–H hydrogen and cannot hydrogen bond to one another.

Reactivity: why an aldehyde beats a ketone
  • Steric — an aldehyde has only one alkyl group beside the carbonyl carbon, a ketone has two. The nucleophile finds the aldehyde easier to reach.

Carboxylic acids — structure and nomenclature
  • The –COOH group; the carbon is sp2 and the group is planar. The two C–O bonds in the carboxylate ion are of equal length, because the charge is delocalised over both oxygens — that resonance is the whole reason for the acidity (M4).

C13Concept
Acidity of carboxylic acids, and the effect of substituents

A carboxylic acid is far stronger than an alcohol or phenol because the carboxylate ion is resonance stabilised over two equivalent oxygens (M4).

Acidic strength
3° alcohol<phenol<H2CO3<RCOOH
  • Electron-withdrawing groups stabilise the anion and strengthen the acid; electron-releasing groups weaken it.
  • Among the aliphatic acids, alkyl groups release electrons, so HCOOH > CH3COOH > CH3CH2COOH: formic acid is the strongest, having no alkyl group at all.
  • Halogens withdraw: CCl3COOH > CHCl2COOH > CH2ClCOOH > CH3COOH, and the effect weakens with distance from the –COOH.
  • On a benzene ring, –NO2 strengthens and –CH3 or –OCH3 weakens: 4-nitrobenzoic acid > benzoic acid > 4-methylbenzoic acid.
Hold on to this  · The ortho effect: any ortho-substituted benzoic acid is stronger than benzoic acid itself, whether the substituent releases or withdraws electrons. The substituent twists the –COOH group out of the plane of the ring, breaking its conjugation with the ring — steric inhibition of resonance.
Remember which way pKa runs: a lower pKa means a stronger acid. So “the pKa of 4-nitrobenzoic acid is lower than that of benzoic acid” is simply a restatement of “the nitro compound is the stronger acid”.

2 · The mechanisms

M1Proof
Nucleophilic addition, worked with HCN
  1. The nucleophile CN− attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon. The π electrons shift on to oxygen, which becomes negatively charged, and the carbon changes from sp2 (planar) to sp3 (tetrahedral).
  2. The alkoxide oxygen then takes a proton from HCN, regenerating CN− and giving the cyanohydrin.
CH3CHO + CN−  →  CH3CH(CN)O−  +HCN→  CH3CH(OH)CN + CN−
The reaction is base catalysed, because the attacking species is CN−, not HCN — so a trace of alkali speeds it up. Hydrolysis of the cyanohydrin gives an α-hydroxy acid: acetaldehyde cyanohydrin gives lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)COOH.
■ mechanism complete

Used when the aldehyde has no α-hydrogen, so no aldol is possible.

  1. Hydroxide adds to the carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral intermediate.
  2. That intermediate transfers a hydride ion (H−) to the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule of the aldehyde.
  3. The molecule that lost the hydride is oxidised to the carboxylate; the one that gained it is reduced to the alcohol.

7. Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

2 · The mechanisms

M4Proof
Why a carboxylic acid is so much more acidic than an alcohol or phenol
  1. Ionisation gives the carboxylate ion, RCOO−.
  2. In that ion the negative charge is delocalised over two equivalent oxygen atoms. The two resonance contributors are of equal energy, which makes the delocalisation exceptionally effective, and the two C–O bonds are found experimentally to be of equal length.
  3. In a phenoxide ion the charge is delocalised too, but on to carbon atoms of the ring, which are less electronegative than oxygen and hold it less comfortably. In an alkoxide ion there is no delocalisation at all.
  4. The more stable the anion, the stronger the acid. Hence RCOOH > H2CO3 > phenol > water > alcohol.
This is also why a carboxylic acid liberates CO2 from NaHCO3 and phenol does not — the single most-used test in the organic part of the paper.
■ proved

Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction
  1. Red phosphorus and the halogen generate PCl3, which converts a little of the acid into its acid chloride.
  2. The acid chloride enolises far more readily than the acid itself, and the enol is halogenated at the α-carbon.
  3. Exchange with more of the parent acid releases the α-halo acid and regenerates the acid chloride.
CH3COOH + Cl2red PClCH2COOH + HCl
Only the α-hydrogen is replaced, and the reaction fails for acids with no α-hydrogen — so formic acid and benzoic acid do not undergo HVZ. Repeating it gives the di- and tri-halo acids, hence the route to trichloroacetic acid.

2 · The mechanisms

M6Proof
Why dialkyl cadmium and not a Grignard reagent

Both reagents will convert an acid chloride into a ketone. The difference is what happens next.

  1. A Grignard reagent is extremely reactive. As soon as the ketone forms, the Grignard reagent still present attacks it again, adding a second alkyl group. The product on hydrolysis is a tertiary alcohol, not the ketone.
  2. Dialkyl cadmium, R2Cd, is a much milder alkylating agent. It is reactive enough to attack the acid chloride, which is highly electrophilic, but not reactive enough to attack the far less electrophilic ketone. The reaction therefore stops cleanly at the ketone.
2CH3COCl + (CH3)2Cd2CH3COCH3 + CdCl2
Hold on to this  · The general principle is worth carrying: when a reaction can run twice, you stop it after one step by using a less reactive reagent, not by using less of a reactive one.

  • The open-chain form does not explain everything — glucose does not give the Schiff's test, and it forms only a monoacetal rather than the diacetal a free –CHO would give. C9Concept
Amino acids, the zwitter ion and the isoelectric point

An α-amino acid has both an amino and a carboxyl group on the same carbon: H2N–CHR–COOH.

  • Because the molecule contains both an acid and a base, a proton transfers internally to give a zwitter ion — +H3N–CHR–COO−. It carries both a positive and a negative charge but is electrically neutral overall, which is why it is also called a dipolar ion.
  • This explains their properties: amino acids are crystalline solids of high melting point, soluble in water and insoluble in organic solvents — they behave like salts, not like ordinary organic compounds.
  • They are amphoteric: in acid the zwitter ion accepts a proton and becomes a cation, in alkali it loses one and becomes an anion.
  • The isoelectric point is the pH at which the amino acid exists entirely as the zwitter ion, so that it has no net charge and does not migrate towards either electrode in an electric field.
Hold on to this  · Glycine, H2N–CH2–COOH, is the only naturally occurring amino acid that is not optically active, because its α-carbon carries two hydrogen atoms and so is not a chiral centre. Every other α-amino acid has four different groups on that carbon.
At a pH below the isoelectric point the amino acid is a cation and migrates to the cathode; above it, it is an anion and migrates to the anode.

2 · The structure proofs

P1Proof
Establishing the open-chain structure of glucose

The molecular formula C6H12O6 alone settles nothing. Each observation below removes one possibility, and together they fix the structure as CH2OH(CHOH)4CHO.

  1. Prolonged heating with HI gives n-hexane. All six carbons are therefore in one unbranched chain.
  2. It forms an oxime with hydroxylamine and adds HCN. A carbonyl group is present.
  3. Bromine water oxidises it to gluconic acid, a monocarboxylic acid. Only one group has been oxidised, and mild oxidation converts –CHO to –COOH but leaves a ketone alone. So the carbonyl group is an aldehyde, and it is at the end of the chain.
  4. Acetylation with excess acetic anhydride gives a penta-acetate. There are five –OH groups.
  5. Warm dilute nitric acid gives saccharic acid, a dicarboxylic acid. A second group has been oxidised to –COOH, and it can only be a primary alcoholic group — so the far end of the chain is –CH2OH.
CH2OH–CHOH–CHOH–CHOH–CHOH–CHO
Step 3 against step 5 is the pair that earns the marks: monocarboxylic from bromine water proves the aldehyde; dicarboxylic from nitric acid proves the primary alcohol at the other end.

2 · The structure proofs

P2Proof
Why the cyclic structure is needed, and what mutarotation proves
  1. The open-chain structure predicts that glucose should give the Schiff's test and should react with NaHSO3, as an aldehyde does. It does neither.
  2. With methanol and dry HCl it forms a monomethyl acetal, whereas a free –CHO would give a dimethyl acetal.
  3. Both observations are explained if the –CHO is not free but is tied up as a cyclic hemiacetal, formed by the C-5 hydroxyl group adding across the C-1 carbonyl to give a six-membered ring.
  4. That makes C-1 a new chiral centre — the anomeric carbon — so two forms exist, α and β.
  5. Mutarotation confirms it. A freshly made solution of pure α-D-glucose has a rotation of +111°, which falls steadily to +52·5°; pure β-D-glucose starts at +19° and rises to the same +52·5°. Both are reaching the same equilibrium mixture, which can only happen if the ring opens and closes in solution, passing through the open-chain form.

The zwitter ion, and why glycine is not optically active

The zwitter ion.

  1. An amino acid contains a basic –NH2 group and an acidic –COOH group in the same molecule.
  2. The acid protonates the base internally:
H2N–CHR–COOH  →  +H3N–CHR–COO−
  1. The result carries one positive and one negative charge and is therefore neutral overall — a dipolar ion. This is why amino acids are high-melting crystalline solids, water soluble, and amphoteric.

Why glycine is the exception.

  1. Optical activity requires a carbon with four different groups.
  2. In a general α-amino acid the α-carbon carries –H, –NH2, –COOH and –R: four different groups, so it is chiral.
  3. In glycine, R = H. The α-carbon therefore carries –H, –H, –NH2 and –COOH — two of its groups are identical, so it is not a chiral centre and the molecule has a plane of symmetry.
Glycine is therefore the only naturally occurring α-amino acid that is optically inactive.

T1Problem type
Classify a carbohydrate, or say whether it reduces
Recognise it by“Classify sucrose”, or “is maltose a reducing sugar? Give a reason.”
MethodTwo questions. How many units on hydrolysis? — that gives the class. Is an anomeric carbon free? — that decides whether it reduces. Sucrose is the only one of the three disaccharides in which both are tied up.
Worked example
Why is sucrose a non-reducing sugar while maltose is reducing?
Answer  · In sucrose the glycosidic linkage joins the C-1 of glucose to the C-2 of fructose — that is, it involves the anomeric carbon of both units. No free aldehyde or ketone group remains, so sucrose cannot reduce Tollens' or Fehling's. In maltose the linkage is C-1 to C-4, so the anomeric carbon of the second glucose unit stays free and can open to the aldehyde form, which reduces.

T2Problem type
What does this reaction of glucose prove?
Recognise it byA reagent and its product are given, and the structural conclusion is wanted.
MethodMatch against the table in C4 — but reason it rather than recall it: ask what changed, and what feature that change requires. A monocarboxylic acid means one oxidisable group; a dicarboxylic acid means two.
Worked example
Glucose gives gluconic acid with bromine water but saccharic acid with warm dilute nitric acid. What does each result establish?
  • Bromine water → gluconic acid, a monocarboxylic acid. Bromine water is a mild oxidant: it oxidises an aldehyde to an acid but leaves a ketone and a primary alcohol untouched. So the carbonyl group of glucose is an aldehyde.
  • Nitric acid → saccharic acid, a dicarboxylic acid. A stronger oxidant has converted a second group into –COOH, and the only candidate is a primary alcoholic (–CH2OH) group at the other end of the chain.

T3Problem type
Distinguish glucose from fructose
Recognise it by“Give a chemical test to distinguish glucose and fructose.”
MethodDo not offer Tollens' or Fehling's — both are alkaline, and fructose tautomerises in alkali and reduces them too (C3). Use bromine water, which is acidic.
Worked example
How would you distinguish between glucose and fructose?
Answer  · Shake each with bromine water. Glucose, being an aldose, is oxidised to gluconic acid and decolourises the bromine water. Fructose, a ketose, gives no reaction and the brown colour remains. Bromine water works because it is acidic, so fructose cannot tautomerise to an aldose as it does in the alkaline Tollens' and Fehling's tests.

T4Problem type
Mutarotation and anomers
Recognise it by“What is mutarotation?”, or the rapid interconversion of the two anomers.
MethodDefine it as the change in specific rotation of a freshly prepared solution until equilibrium is reached, and say why: the ring opens and closes, so the α and β anomers interconvert.
Worked example
The rapid conversion of α-D-glucose and β-D-glucose in solution is known as:  (Heritage 2024–25)
Answer  · Mutarotation. A fresh solution of α-D-glucose has a specific rotation of +111° and one of β-D-glucose +19°; both drift to +52·5°, the value of the equilibrium mixture. The interconversion is possible because the cyclic hemiacetal ring can open to the open-chain form and close the other way round.
Common slip  · It is not racemisation. Racemisation gives an optically inactive 50:50 mixture of enantiomers; the α and β anomers are diastereomers, and the equilibrium mixture is still optically active at +52·5°.

Invert sugar
Recognise it by“Why is the hydrolysis product of sucrose called invert sugar?”
MethodQuote the three rotations and show that the sign changes. That change of sign is the whole point of the name.
Worked example
Explain why the hydrolysis of sucrose is called inversion of cane sugar.
  1. sucrose +66·5°
  2. hydrolysis gives glucose (+52·5°) and fructose (−92·4°), equimolar
  3. net rotation = ½(+52·5 − 92·4) ≈ −20°
Answer  · The rotation changes from +66·5° to about −20° — the sign is inverted, because fructose is strongly laevorotatory and outweighs the dextrorotatory glucose. The mixture is therefore called invert sugar.

T6Problem type
Linkages in disaccharides and polysaccharides
Recognise it by“Name the linkage in maltose”, or “how do starch and cellulose differ?”
MethodGive the units, the positions joined, and whether the linkage is α or β. All three carry marks, and the α/β distinction is the one that matters biologically.
Worked example
How do starch and cellulose differ, although both are polymers of glucose?
Answer  · Starch is built from α-D-glucose, joined α-1,4 in amylose (linear) and α-1,4 with α-1,6 branches in amylopectin. Cellulose is built from β-D-glucose, joined β-1,4 in long unbranched chains that pack side by side into fibres. The difference in the linkage is why starch is digestible by humans while cellulose is not — we have no enzyme that hydrolyses the β-1,4 bond.

T8Problem type
Optical activity of amino acids
Recognise it by“Except glycine, all naturally occurring α-amino acids are optically active. Give a reason.”
MethodState the requirement for chirality — four different groups — then show that glycine fails it because R = H gives it two hydrogens on the α-carbon.
Worked example
Except glycine, all other naturally occurring α-amino acids are optically active. Explain.  (Heritage 2024–25)
Answer  · Optical activity needs a carbon with four different groups. In a general α-amino acid the α-carbon carries –H, –NH2, –COOH and –R, all different, so it is a chiral centre. In glycine R is itself a hydrogen atom, so the α-carbon carries two identical hydrogens; it is not a chiral centre, the molecule has a plane of symmetry, and glycine is therefore optically inactive.

T9Problem type
Protein structure, and converting one level into another
Recognise it byA diagram of an α-helix or β-sheet, with “which structure is this?” and “how can it be converted to the primary structure?”
MethodAn α-helix or a β-pleated sheet is a secondary structure, held by hydrogen bonds in the backbone. To reduce it to the primary structure, break those hydrogen bonds — that is, denature it — while leaving the peptide bonds intact.
Worked example
(a) Which type of structure do an α-helix and a β-pleated sheet represent? (b) How can these structures be converted into the primary structure?  (Heritage 2024–25)
  • (a) Both are secondary structures — the local shape taken up by the polypeptide chain, held in place by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the backbone.
  • (b) By denaturation — heating the protein or changing the pH. This breaks the hydrogen bonds that maintain the helix or sheet, so the chain uncoils into a random shape and loses its biological activity. The peptide bonds are covalent and are not broken, so the primary structure — the sequence of amino acids — survives unchanged.
Familiar examples of denaturation: the coagulation of egg white on boiling, and the curdling of milk on adding acid.
